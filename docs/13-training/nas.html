    Neural architecture search — six ways to choose the activations

    Intuition #

    Architecture design is usually a human loop: try a network, read the validation curve, change one thing, repeat. Neural architecture search (NAS) automates the loop. The search space here is deliberately tiny so that every method can be run to completion: two cells, each cell picks one of four activations — identity, \(\tanh\), ReLU or \(\sin\) — for \(4^2=16\) genomes. The task is a periodic boundary, \(\sin(6x)+0.3y>0\), so the genome that matters is the one containing a periodic unit. Exhaustive search finds sin/sin at 0.894, and the all-affine network scores 0.569: the signal is real, and the space is small enough that "good search" has a definite meaning.

    Six strategies attack the same 16-cell space. darts relaxes the discrete choice into a softmax over operations and takes gradients through the relaxation. enas keeps a controller that samples genomes and is trained by REINFORCE from their rewards. amoebanet and regularized_evolution run tournaments and mutate winners, with different replacement rules. supernet trains one pool of weights by sampling paths, then reads every genome out of the shared pool. once_for_all trains one wide network per second-cell choice and slices narrower widths out of it for free.

    The results are not a ranking of "best method". AmoebaNet, regularized evolution and the supernet find sin/sin or sin/tanh at 0.894 and 0.879; DARTS and once-for-all settle at 0.700, because the relaxed optimum and the fixed trunk are not the discrete optimum; ENAS pays 9,900 gradient steps to reach 0.757. That spread is the lesson: the search algorithm, the proxy it optimises, and the budget it spends are three different things, and a NAS paper that reports only the final accuracy is hiding two of them.

    Variants #

    variant mechanism genome score nets steps

    once_for_all one wide net, sliced widths tanh/sin 0.700 7 2100
    supernet shared pool, sampled paths sin/sin 0.894 3 2100

    Exhaustive search over all 16 genomes costs 48 networks and 14,400 steps for the same 0.894. "Nets" counts every training, and each genome is trained three times with fixed seeds so that the objective is deterministic; the seeds are identical for every variant, so all six searches optimise the same benchmark.

    Derivation #

    The objective #

    A genome \(g=(g_1,g_2)\) selects one activation per cell. Training a genome means minimising the usual softmax cross-entropy, and scoring it means measuring test accuracy. Because a single initialisation is noisy, the benchmark averages \(R\) fixed seeds:

    \[g^\star=\arg\max_{g\in\mathcal G}\ \frac1R\sum_{r=1}^{R} \mathrm{Acc}\big(\mathcal D_{\mathrm{test}};\theta(g,s_r)\big), \qquad (1)\]

    with \(|\mathcal G|=16\) and \(R=3\). Exhaustive search evaluates all 16 terms of (1): 48 trainings, 14,400 gradient steps, and the answer sin/sin at 0.894. Every variant below is a way to avoid evaluating all 16.

    The network #

    Each cell is an affine map followed by the chosen activation:

    \[h_1=f_{g_1}(W_1x+b_1),\qquad h_2=f_{g_2}(W_2h_1+b_2),\qquad p=\mathrm{softmax}(W_3h_2+b_3), \qquad (2)\]

    and the loss is \(-\log p_y\). The two cells share a head but not their weights; the search changes only which \(f\) is applied where.

    ENAS: a controller and REINFORCE #

    ENAS parameterises a distribution over genomes with a controller \(\pi_\theta(g)=\prod_\ell \pi_{\theta_\ell}(g_\ell)\) and samples genomes from it. The reward is the validation accuracy, and the controller is updated by

    \[\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{g\sim\pi_\theta} \left[\big(R(g)-b\big)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(g)\right], \qquad (3)\]

    where \(b\) is a running baseline. The code uses the score-function form directly: for the sampled cell, the gradient of \(\log\pi\) is \(e_{g_\ell}-p_\ell\), and the update is

    \[\theta_\ell\leftarrow\theta_\ell+\eta\,\big(R-b\big) \big(e_{g_\ell}-p_\ell\big). \qquad (4)\]

    The baseline \(b\) is an exponential moving average of rewards, which does not change the expectation of (3) but removes most of its variance. ENAS draws 16 genomes, of which 11 are distinct, and the duplicates are free because evaluate caches by genome.

    DARTS: relax the choice #

    DARTS replaces the discrete pick with a softmax over operations:

    \[\bar o_\ell(x)=\sum_{o\in\mathcal O} \frac{\exp(\alpha_{\ell,o})}{\sum_{o'}\exp(\alpha_{\ell,o'})}\,o(x), \qquad (5)\]

    so the network is differentiable in \(\alpha\). With \(w_{\ell,o}\) the softmax weight, the derivative with respect to the logit is

    \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial\alpha_{\ell,o}} =\sum_i \frac{\partial L}{\partial\bar o_\ell(x_i)}\, w_{\ell,o}\big(o(x_i)-\bar o_\ell(x_i)\big), \qquad (6)\]

    which is exactly the d_alpha accumulation in _mix_grads. The original method also differentiates through a few steps of the inner training (bilevel); the code takes the first-order shortcut,

    \[\nabla_\alpha L_{\mathrm{val}} \approx \nabla_\alpha L_{\mathrm{val}} \big(\theta-\xi\nabla_\theta L_{\mathrm{train}}\big), \qquad (7)\]

    ignoring the Hessian term. The relaxed model trains for 300 steps, the architecture logits take a descent step of 0.05 per iteration, and the winner is the argmax of each row. The direction of that step is load-bearing: with alpha += ... the search ascends the loss and returns a near-affine genome instead. Here the descent direction lands on sin/relu.

    Weight sharing: the supernet #

    A supernet trains one parameter pool \(\theta\) against a distribution over paths:

    \[\min_\theta\ \mathbb E_{g\sim p(g)}\, L\big(g;\theta\big),\qquad p\ \text{uniform on }\mathcal G . \qquad (8)\]

    Each path uses its own \((W_1,W_2)\) blocks and the shared head, so sampling a path updates the blocks it touches. The code samples in shuffled rounds of 16, four rounds in total, so every genome is updated four times instead of the sparse coverage of independent sampling. After training, every genome is read out with the same \(\theta\); the readout is the "shared estimate".

    The estimate is biased. A shared pool is optimised for the average path, and the head in particular sees a different hidden distribution at every step, so

    \[\mathrm{Acc}\big(g;\theta_{\mathrm{shared}}\big)\neq \mathrm{Acc}\big(g;\theta(g)\big), \qquad (9)\]

    and the ranking it induces need not agree with (1). Here the shared estimate for the winner is 0.833 while the benchmark is 0.894, and in general the correlation between the two rankings is well below one — which is why one-shot NAS reports a ranking, then retrains the winner.

    Evolution and aging #

    The evolutionary variants keep a population of genomes, pick a parent by \(k\)-way tournament, mutate one cell, and insert the child. The regularized variant replaces the worst member; AmoebaNet replaces the oldest, a rule that prevents a single lineage from taking over. Mutation changes one cell with probability 0.4, the other with 0.4, and both with 0.2:

    \[g'=\begin{cases}(u,g_2)&u\sim U(\mathcal O),\ \text{prob }0.4\\ (g_1,u)&\text{prob }0.4\\ (u,v)&\text{prob }0.2\end{cases} \qquad (10)\]

    The demo's AmoebaNet starts from the four distinct one-cell genomes, expands them to (c,c), and ages the population; it reaches sin/sin after 12 trainings. Regularized evolution starts from six random genomes and reaches sin/tanh after 24; the extra cost is the price of the wider initial population and of replacing the worst rather than the oldest.

    Once-for-all: elastic width #

    OFA trains one wide network and derives sub-networks by masking channels:

    \[h=\big(W\odot m_w\big)x,\qquad m_w\in\{0,1\}^{d}, \qquad (11)\]

    so a width-\(w\) slice is a prefix of the wide weight matrix. The demo trains one width-8 network per second-cell operation with the first cell fixed to tanh, then evaluates widths 4, 6 and 8 by slicing. It finds tanh/sin at 0.700. The first cell is frozen, so the periodic unit can never appear where the task needs it, and that ceiling is visible in the score: the method is searching widths, not activations.

    What the budget buys #

    Counting gradient steps rather than wall-clock makes the comparison portable. Each genome costs \(R\) trainings of \(T\) steps, so exhaustive search costs

    \[C_{\mathrm{exhaustive}}=|\mathcal G|\,R\,T=16\cdot 3\cdot 300=14{,}400, \qquad (12)\]

    and a search that scores \(N\) distinct genomes costs \(N R T\) plus any architecture-level overhead. AmoebaNet's \(N=4\) gives 3,600 steps for the same 0.894; the supernet pays one 1,200-step shared pass and then three retrains for 2,100. ENAS's \(N=11\) costs 9,900 and still misses the optimum — sample efficiency is not automatically better than enumeration on a 16-cell space.

    Code walkthrough #

    The forward pass is the genome made concrete: two lookups into the parameter dictionary, one activation each, and a shared head.

    def _forward(params: dict, x: np.ndarray, genome):
    pre_one = x @ params[(0, genome[0])]["W"] + params[(0, genome[0])]["b"]
    first = ACTS[genome[0]](pre_one)
    pre_two = first @ params[(1, genome[1])]["W"] + params[(1, genome[1])]["b"]
    second = ACTS[genome[1]](pre_two)
    logits = second @ params["head"]["W"] + params["head"]["b"]
    return logits, (x, pre_one, first, pre_two, second)

    The benchmark is the mean over fixed seeds, and the cache is what makes repeated sampling in ENAS and evolution cheap:

                for seed in range(self.restarts):
                params = _init(np.random.default_rng(seed))
                _train(params, x, y, genome, self.steps, self.lr, self.decay)
                self.n_trained += 1
                self.n_steps += self.steps
                probs = _softmax(_forward(params, test_x, genome)[0])
                scores.append(float(np.mean(probs.argmax(axis=1) == test_y)))
            self.cache[genome] = float(np.mean(scores))

    DARTS builds the mixture (5) by evaluating all four operations on the same pre-activation and combining the results with the softmax weights:

        def _mix_forward(self, params, x, alpha):
        weight1 = _softmax(alpha[0])
        weight2 = _softmax(alpha[1])
        pre1 = [x @ params[(0, op)]["W"] + params[(0, op)]["b"] for op in OPS]
        act1 = [ACTS[op](value) for op, value in zip(OPS, pre1, strict=True)]
        hidden = sum(weight1[i] * act1[i] for i in range(len(OPS)))

    and then takes the descent step on the logits — the sign that decides whether the search works:

                grads, d_alpha = self._mix_grads(params, cache, delta)
            _apply(params, grads, first, second, step, self.lr, self.decay)
            alpha -= 0.05 * d_alpha

    ENAS's controller update is (4) with a score-function gradient per cell and a moving-average baseline:

                baseline = 0.7 * baseline + 0.3 * score
            for layer in range(2):
                grad = -probs[layer].copy()
                grad[OPS.index(genome[layer])] += 1.0
                theta[layer] += 0.5 * (score - baseline) * grad

    The two evolutionary variants share the tournament and the mutation, and differ only in what leaves the population. AmoebaNet ages:

                score = self.evaluate((child, child), x, y, test_x, test_y)
            population.pop(0)
            scores.pop(0)
            population.append(child)
            scores.append(score)

    while regularized evolution replaces its worst member. The supernet trains in shuffled rounds so that every genome is visited before any is revisited:

            for step in range(1, rounds * self.steps + 1):
            slot = (step - 1) % len(GRID)
            if slot == 0:
                order = [int(index) for index in self.rng.permutation(
                    len(GRID))]
            genome = GRID[order[slot]]

    OFA derives a narrower subnet by slicing the prefix of each weight matrix and running the same forward pass:

                    sliced = {
                    (0, "tanh"): {
                        "W": params[(0, "tanh")]["W"][:, :width],
                        "b": params[(0, "tanh")]["b"][:width]},
                    (1, op): {
                        "W": params[(1, op)]["W"][:width, :width],
                        "b": params[(1, op)]["b"][:width]},
                    "head": {"W": params["head"]["W"][:width],
                             "b": params["head"]["b"]}}

    Complexity #

    Let \(|\mathcal G|\) be the number of genomes, \(T\) the steps per training and \(R\) the restarts.

    • Exhaustive: \(|\mathcal G|RT\) gradient steps. Here \(16\cdot3\cdot300 =14{,}400\), and it is the reference the other five are measured against.
    • DARTS: one relaxed training plus one retrain, \(2T\) steps and one architecture level. The mixture evaluates all four operations per cell per step, so each step is four times the arithmetic of a discrete forward pass; the step count does not show that multiplier.
    • ENAS: \(N R T\) for \(N\) distinct sampled genomes, plus a controller whose cost is negligible at this scale.
    • Evolution: \(N R T\) with \(N\) the number of distinct genomes ever scored; the tournament and mutation are \(O(1)\).
    • Supernet: one shared pass of four rounds of \(|\mathcal G|\) steps, plus \(R T\) for the retrain. The shared pass is cheap per step but sees only one path at a time.
    • OFA: one training per first-cell choice, then a forward pass per slice; the slice evaluation is free compared with training.

    The exhaustive probe alone is 14,400 steps; the six searches add about 26,000 more. Every training is a two-layer network on 240 points, so the constant is small; the point of the table is the ratio between \(N R T\) and \(|\mathcal G|RT\), not absolute time.

    Failure modes #

    The relaxed optimum is not the discrete optimum. DARTS trains the mixture in (5) and then takes an argmax, but the mixture's best weights need not point at the best discrete genome. Here it picks sin/relu at 0.700 while sin/sin scores 0.894. The gap is the price of the relaxation, and it grows when the operations have very different scales — four activations with different output ranges are close to a worst case.

    Weight sharing biases the ranking. The supernet's shared estimate is 0.833 for a genome that retrains to 0.894, and every one-shot method inherits some version of (9). Reporting the shared number as the architecture's accuracy overstates the method by a wide margin. Always retrain the winner before quoting a score.

    The controller needs more samples than the space has cells. ENAS spends 9,900 steps and 33 trainings to reach 0.757, more than AmoebaNet's 3,600 for 0.894. REINFORCE on a 16-armed bandit is sample-hungry; on a space this small, enumeration is cheaper than learning a policy. ENAS pays off when the space is combinatorial and exhaustive search is impossible.

    A frozen cell caps the score. OFA fixes the first cell to tanh and searches only the second, so it can never build the periodic unit where the task needs it; its 0.700 is a ceiling, not a search failure. Elastic-width methods assume the macro architecture is already right.

    Seed noise can beat the search signal. Before the restarts in (1), the same genome scored anywhere from 0.55 to 0.93 depending on initialisation, and a search could "win" by drawing a lucky seed. Averaging three seeds closes most of that gap; it does not eliminate it, and a search that reports one run is reporting one draw.

    The benchmark is not the deployment task. Every variant here optimises test accuracy on the same distribution it is scored on. In practice the search runs on a proxy — a smaller dataset, a shorter schedule, a validation split — and the proxy's ranking can differ from the full task's. The shared-estimate bias and the proxy bias compound.

    Budget accounting hides the constant. Counting trainings treats a DARTS mixture step and a discrete step as equal, but the mixture evaluates four operations. Counting steps ignores that a supernet step touches one path's blocks. There is no single fair unit; state the unit and the constant.

    Architecture search can overfit the validation set. With 16 genomes and a 3-seed average the risk is small, but a search that evaluates thousands of genomes against one validation split will select noise. The usual fix is a held-out test set that the search never sees, which is what the demo's test_x/test_y would have to be if the space were larger.

    Search spaces encode assumptions. Four activations and two cells cannot express a residual connection, a convolution or a normalisation layer. The winner here is "the activation that matches the data", which is a statement about this space, not about NAS in general.

    Mutation is not local in a two-gene genome. Changing one of two cells is a large jump: there is no "nearby" architecture to hill-climb. Evolution works here because the space is tiny; on real cell-based spaces the mutation operators are designed to keep most of the graph intact.

    One shared pool can co-adapt. The head sees a different hidden distribution on every supernet step, so it learns a compromise. That is the mechanism behind the 0.833 estimate; techniques such as path dropout and progressive shrinking exist to reduce it.

    Applications #

    NAS entered practice through image-classification backbones, where the searched cell is repeated at several resolutions and the search cost is amortised over a family of models. AmoebaNet and its descendants produced backbones that were competitive with hand-designed ones, and DARTS made the search cheap enough to run on a single machine — at the cost of the relaxation gap above.

    Weight-sharing methods are now the default because they decouple search cost from the number of architectures: train one supernet, evaluate thousands of subnets, retrain a handful. Once-for-all turned that into a deployment story, where one trained network serves a range of latency budgets by slicing width, depth and resolution, and hardware-aware variants add latency to the objective so the search returns models that fit a device.

    Evolutionary search survives where the space is discrete and the objective is noisy or non-differentiable: hardware-aware NAS, compiler autotuning, feature selection, and neural-network pruning, where each candidate is a mask rather than a differentiable weight. The controller approach is less common now but remains the cleanest example of treating architecture choice as a policy.

    Outside deep learning, the same patterns recur: Bayesian optimisation over hyperparameters (chapter 13's hyperparameter_search), bandit algorithms for allocating a training budget, and evolutionary search over pipelines. The vocabulary changes; the tension between a cheap proxy and the true objective does not.
