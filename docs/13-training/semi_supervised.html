    13 · Training/tier 2/7 variants/source

    Semi-supervised learning — seven ways to use unlabelled points

    Intuition #

    Semi-supervised learning starts from a strange bargain: you have a few labels, many inputs, and no extra information about what the missing labels are. Everything it can exploit is already in the input distribution — the shape of the data, the smoothness you impose on the model, and the guess that nearby points share a label.

    The seven variants here take two routes. Pseudo-labelling methods run the model on unlabelled inputs and train on its own predictions: self-training adds the confident ones as hard labels, soft pseudo-labelling keeps the probabilities, FixMatch adds strong augmentation, MixMatch mixes labelled and unlabelled batches. Consistency methods compare two views of the same input: the mean teacher uses an exponential moving average of the weights, and the ladder reconstructs clean activations from noisy ones. Label propagation ignores the network and solves a graph problem.

    On two moons with eight labels, the supervised network scores 0.690. The consistency and graph methods are far ahead — mean teacher 0.985, label propagation 0.998, ladder 0.733 — while every pseudo-labelling method lands within 0.003 of the baseline. That is the result worth taking seriously: a model trained on its own predictions has a fixed point, and a bad boundary is a very stable fixed point.

    Variants #

    variant uses signal extra cost
    self_training hard pseudo-labels confidence threshold \(R\) fits
    pseudo_label soft pseudo-labels all unlabelled, weighted one fit
    fixmatch strong augmentation weak view gives the label one fit
    mean_teacher EMA teacher KL between two views two forwards
    mixmatch sharpen + MixUp mixed targets one fit
    label_propagation kNN graph closed-form diffusion one solve
    ladder input noise denoising cost one noisy forward

    One learner, one labelled set, one unlabelled pool. The variants differ in what they add to the cross-entropy, not in the network: a 2→32→32→2 tanh MLP trained with Adam and weight decay.

    Derivation #

    The objective #

    With labelled data \((\mathcal L)\) and unlabelled inputs \((\mathcal U)\), the target is still classification accuracy, but the loss has two terms:

    \[L(\theta)=\underbrace{\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal L} \ell\!\left(f_\theta(x),y\right)}_{\text{supervised}} +\lambda\underbrace{\sum_{u\in\mathcal U} \rho\!\left(f_\theta,u\right)}_{\text{unsupervised}}, \qquad (1)\]

    and every variant below is a different \(\rho\). The unlabelled term carries no label information, so it can only shape the function between the labelled points. If that shape is wrong, more unlabelled data makes the model more confidently wrong.

    Hard pseudo-labels #

    Self-training picks the model's own answer where it is confident:

    \[\hat y(u)=\arg\max_c p_\theta(c\mid u),\qquad \mathcal U^{+}=\{u:\max_c p_\theta(c\mid u)\ge\tau\}, \qquad (2)\]

    and then retrains on \(\mathcal L\cup\{(u,\hat y(u))\}\). Retraining from the current weights rather than from scratch makes the procedure a fixed-point iteration. On the demo it reaches 367 of 392 points at \(\tau=0.9\) and the accuracy does not move at all (0.690, the same as the baseline).

    The reason is visible in the gradient. Once every accepted point satisfies \(p_\theta(u)\approx e_{\hat y(u)}\), the pseudo-label term contributes

    \[\nabla_W L_{\mathrm{pseudo}} =\sum_{u\in\mathcal U^{+}}\left(p_\theta(u)-e_{\hat y(u)}\right)\phi(u)^\top \approx 0, \qquad (3)\]

    so the boundary is already a stationary point of its own labelling. The threshold sweep makes the same point four ways: 389, 377, 367 and 320 labels at thresholds 0.60, 0.80, 0.90 and 0.99, and 0.690 accuracy every time. The network is overconfident almost everywhere, so the threshold selects a quantity of points, not a quality of labels.

    Soft pseudo-labels #

    Instead of committing, keep the distribution and weight it:

    \[L_{\mathrm{soft}}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal U}w(u)\, H\!\left(p_\theta(u),\,p_{\bar\theta}(u)\right), \qquad (4)\]

    where \(\bar\theta\) is the model that produced the target and \(w\) is the variant's weight — 1.0 here. Soft targets carry the model's uncertainty, but they carry no more information than the hard ones; the demo's soft variant also lands at 0.690. Both are useful when the starting model is already good, which is why they work in distillation-style pipelines and not from scratch.

    FixMatch #

    FixMatch makes the pseudo-label from a weak view and applies it to a strong view of the same input:

    \[L_{\mathrm{fix}}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal U} \mathbf{1}\!\left[\max_c p_\theta(c\mid \alpha_w(u))\ge\tau\right] H\!\left(\hat y_w(u),\,p_\theta(\alpha_s(u))\right). \qquad (5)\]

    The weak view here is a small jitter, the strong view a rotation, a rescale and a larger jitter. The two views force the prediction to be invariant to transformations that should not change the label, which is information the model did not have from eight points. FixMatch reaches 0.785, and adding the strong view is the only change from plain pseudo-labelling.

    Mean teacher #

    Keep a second copy of the weights and update it as an exponential moving average of the student:

    \[\bar\theta_t=\gamma\bar\theta_{t-1}+(1-\gamma)\theta_t,\qquad L_{\mathrm{mt}}=H\!\left(p_{\bar\theta}(\alpha_w(u)),\, p_\theta(\alpha_s(u))\right). \qquad (6)\]

    The teacher is an average over the whole training trajectory, so it is smoother and more stable than the student; the student is pulled towards it on the strong view. With \(\gamma=0.99\) the teacher lags by about a hundred steps. This is the strongest method in the demo: 0.985 against 0.690, from eight labels, because the consistency term does not need the labels to be right — only the invariance to hold.

    MixMatch #

    MixMatch sharpens the averaged unlabelled guesses and mixes them with the labelled batch:

    \[\bar q(u)=\mathrm{Sharpen}\!\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} p_\theta(u_k),\,T\right),\qquad \tilde x=\lambda x_i+(1-\lambda)x_j, \qquad (7)\]

    with \(\lambda\sim\mathrm{Beta}(0.75,0.75)\) and the same mix applied to the targets. Sharpening with \(T=0.5\) turns a soft guess into a nearly one-hot target, and MixUp interpolates both inputs and labels. On the demo it scores 0.693: the mixing regularises, but the targets still come from the same boundary, so the fixed point survives.

    Label propagation #

    Build a k-nearest-neighbour graph over the labelled and unlabelled points and diffuse the labels:

    \[F=(I-\alpha S)^{-1}Y,\qquad S=D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}, \qquad (8)\]

    where \(W_{ij}=\exp(-\lVert x_i-x_j\rVert^2/2\sigma^2)\) on the kNN edges, \(D\) is its degree matrix, \(Y\) holds the one-hot labels on labelled rows and zeros elsewhere, and \(\alpha\in(0,1)\) controls how far the diffusion travels. The inverse is a closed-form solve, not an iteration, and it is the only method here that never trains a network. At \(k=7\), \(\sigma=0.3\) and \(\alpha=0.8\) it scores 0.998: the two moons are two connected components of the graph, and propagation finds them.

    The ladder #

    The ladder adds noise at each layer and asks the network to reconstruct the clean activations. With one decoder at the top, the cost is

    \[L_{\mathrm{ladder}}=L_{\mathrm{sup}} +\beta\sum_{u\in\mathcal U} \left\lVert \tilde h_2(u)-h_2(u)\right\rVert^2, \qquad (9)\]

    where \(h_2\) is the clean second hidden layer and \(\tilde h_2\) is the same layer computed from a noisy input. The reconstruction term forces the hidden representation to be stable under corruption, which is the same invariance argument as FixMatch, applied one layer down. The full ladder reconstructs every layer with its own decoder; this atom keeps one to stay readable, and the demo's 0.733 shows the idea survives the simplification.

    The network #

    Every variant shares the same two-hidden-layer MLP:

    \[h_1=\tanh(W_1x+b_1),\quad h_2=\tanh(W_2h_1+b_2),\quad z=W_3h_2+b_3, \qquad (10)\]

    trained on soft targets \(t\) with the weighted cross-entropy

    \[\ell=-\frac{1}{\sum_i w_i}\sum_i w_i\sum_c t_{ic}\log p_{ic}. \qquad (11)\]

    The soft-target form is what lets the same training step serve hard labels, soft pseudo-labels, mixed labels and consistency targets: only \(t\) and \(w\) change.

    What the numbers say #

    The demo's summary is:

    method accuracy pseudo-labels
    supervised only 0.690 0
    self_training 0.690 367
    pseudo_label 0.690 367
    fixmatch 0.785 339
    mean_teacher 0.985 0
    mixmatch 0.693 392
    label_propagation 0.998 392
    ladder 0.733 0

    The methods that move are the ones whose loss adds information the model could not have generated itself: an invariance, a graph, or a smoother teacher. The methods that do not move are the ones whose targets are a function of the current boundary.

    Code walkthrough #

    The augmentation is the only place the two views differ, so it is worth reading first. The strong view rotates, rescales and jitters; the weak view only jitters:

    def _augment(x: np.ndarray, rng: np.random.Generator,
             strong: bool) -> np.ndarray:
    """Jitter alone is weak; a rotation and a rescale make it strong."""
    values = x.copy()
    if strong:
        angle = rng.uniform(-0.6, 0.6, len(values))
        scale = rng.uniform(0.8, 1.2, len(values))
        cosine, sine = np.cos(angle), np.sin(angle)
        centre = values.mean(axis=0)
        shifted = values - centre
        values = np.column_stack([
            cosine * shifted[:, 0] - sine * shifted[:, 1],
            sine * shifted[:, 0] + cosine * shifted[:, 1]])
        values = values * scale[:, None] + centre
        values += rng.normal(0.0, 0.15, values.shape)
    else:
        values += rng.normal(0.0, 0.05, values.shape)
    return values

    The network is deliberately small, and the backward pass is written once and shared by every variant:

    def _backward(params: dict, cache, dlogits: np.ndarray) -> dict:
    x, first, second = cache
    del x, first
    grads = {"W3": second.T @ dlogits, "b3": dlogits.sum(axis=0)}
    d_second = (dlogits @ params["W3"].T) * (1.0 - second ** 2)
    grads.update(_backward_second(params, cache, d_second))
    return grads

    The soft-target cross-entropy is (11) as a gradient, which is the single function all seven variants call:

    def _loss_grad(params: dict, cache, target: np.ndarray,
               weight: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Weighted soft-target cross-entropy, returned as d(loss)/d(logits)."""
    logits = cache[2] @ params["W3"] + params["b3"]
    probs = _softmax(logits)
    return (probs - target) * weight[:, None] / weight.sum()

    Self-training is the fixed-point loop of (2)–(3): the added mask stops a point from being bought twice, and n_added reports the union over rounds:

        def _self_training(self, x_lab, y_lab, x_unlab) -> None:
        added = np.zeros(len(x_unlab), dtype=bool)
        for _ in range(self.rounds):
            self._train(x_lab, y_lab, self.steps)
            probs = self._probs(x_unlab)
            confidence = probs.max(axis=1)
            fresh = (confidence >= self.threshold) & ~added
            keep = np.where(fresh)[0]
            if not keep.size:
                break
            added[keep] = True
            x_lab = np.vstack([x_lab, x_unlab[keep]])
            y_lab = np.concatenate([y_lab, probs[keep].argmax(axis=1)])
        self.n_added = int(added.sum())

    FixMatch is the mask plus the strong view; note that the pseudo-label is read from the weak probabilities and the gradient flows through the strong ones:

                weak = _augment(x_unlab, self.rng, strong=False)
            strong = _augment(x_unlab, self.rng, strong=True)
            probs = _softmax(_forward(self.params, weak)[0])
            confidence = probs.max(axis=1)
            mask = confidence >= self.threshold
            x = np.vstack([x_lab, strong[mask]])
            y = np.concatenate([y_lab, probs[mask].argmax(axis=1)])

    The mean teacher computes the consistency gradient as student - teacher, which is the derivative of \(\mathrm{KL}(p_{\bar\theta}\,\Vert\,p_\theta)\) with respect to the student logits, then adds it to the labelled gradient:

                teacher = _softmax(_forward(self.teacher, weak)[0])
            _, cache = _forward(self.params, strong)
            student = _softmax(cache[2] @ self.params["W3"]
                               + self.params["b3"])
            consistency = _backward(self.params, cache, self.lam * (
                student - teacher) / len(strong))

    Sharpening is a temperature applied to the averaged guesses, and MixUp mixes both inputs and targets with the same coefficient:

            guesses = np.mean([self._probs(x_unlab) for _ in range(2)], axis=0)
        sharp = guesses ** (1.0 / 0.5)
        sharp = sharp / sharp.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

                fraction = self.rng.beta(0.75, 0.75, len(x))
            lam = np.maximum(fraction, 1.0 - fraction)[:, None]
            mixed_x = lam * x[order] + (1.0 - lam) * x[shuffle]
            mixed_y = lam * target[order] + (1.0 - lam) * target[shuffle]

    Label propagation builds a symmetric kNN graph, normalises it, and solves (8) in one linear solve:

            adjacency = np.maximum(adjacency, adjacency.T)
        degree = adjacency.sum(axis=1) + 1e-12
        inverse = 1.0 / np.sqrt(degree)
        normalised = adjacency * inverse[:, None] * inverse[None, :]
        seed = np.zeros((len(pool), CLASSES))
        seed[np.arange(len(y_lab)), y_lab] = 1.0
        scores = np.linalg.solve(
            np.eye(len(pool)) - self.alpha * normalised, seed)

    The ladder adds noise to the labelled inputs, computes the noisy hidden layer, and backpropagates the reconstruction error from that layer alone — the clean activation is the constant target:

                _, clean_cache = _forward(self.params, x_unlab)
            noisy_x = x_unlab + self.rng.normal(0.0, 0.3, x_unlab.shape)
            _, noisy_cache = _forward(self.params, noisy_x)
            cost = 2.0 * self.lam * (noisy_cache[2] - clean_cache[2]) / len(
                x_unlab)
            denoise = _backward_second(self.params, noisy_cache, cost)

    Inference for the graph method is a weighted vote of the nearest propagated points, which is how a transductive method answers a question about a point it never trained on:

                gap = values[:, None, :] - self.pool_x[None, :, :]
            distance = np.sqrt(np.sum(gap ** 2, axis=-1))
            order = np.argsort(distance, axis=1)[:, :self.neighbours]
            weights = np.exp(-np.take_along_axis(
                distance, order, axis=1) ** 2 / (2.0 * 0.3 ** 2))
            scores = np.take_along_axis(
                self.pool_scores[None, :, :], order[:, :, None], axis=1)
            probed = np.sum(weights[:, :, None] * scores, axis=1)

    Complexity #

    With \(n\) labelled points, \(m\) unlabelled points, \(d=2\) inputs, \(h=32\) hidden units and \(R\) rounds:

    • Supervised training: \(O((n+m)h^2)\) per epoch, dominated by the hidden matrix; the demo's 400 full-batch steps on 400 points is a few million multiply-adds.
    • Self-training: \(R\) supervised runs, so \(R\) times the cost, plus \(R\) predict passes over the pool.
    • Soft pseudo-labels, MixMatch: one extra run over \(n+m\) points with soft targets; the soft cross-entropy costs the same as the hard one.
    • FixMatch, mean teacher: one fit, but two augmented forward passes per step (weak and strong), so roughly 2× the supervised cost. The teacher adds one more forward pass and a copy of the parameters.
    • Label propagation: an \(m\times m\) distance matrix, \(O(m^2d)\), plus the dense solve \(O(m^3)\). For 392 points this is instant; for a million points it is the reason people use sparse graph approximations or mini-batch diffusion.
    • The demo: seven variants plus a threshold sweep and an agreement check, about three seconds on one core. The network is small enough that the augmentation and the linear solve, not the matrix multiplies, dominate.

    Failure modes #

    Pseudo-labelling a weak model is a fixed point. The demo's self-training, soft pseudo-labels and MixMatch all land within 0.003 of the 0.690 baseline. The gradient on confident points is already zero (3), so retraining reproduces the same boundary; a bad boundary is a stable one. Pseudo-labelling helps when the starting model is strong — a pretrained network, a large teacher — not when it is the thing you are trying to improve.

    Confidence is not correctness. The demo's threshold sweep accepts 320 to 389 of 392 points depending on the threshold, and every setting scores the same. A 32-unit tanh network trained for 400 steps is confidently wrong in the region it has never seen. Temperature scaling or an entropy penalty is the usual mitigation; neither is in this file.

    Consistency needs the right invariances. Mean teacher's 0.985 comes from rotation and scale being label-preserving for two moons. On digits, rotation is nearly free; on text, it is meaningless. A strong augmentation that changes the label turns the consistency loss into a source of error, and the model will happily learn the wrong invariance.

    The EMA teacher can stall. With \(\gamma\) close to one the teacher is almost constant, so the student chases a target that never moves. With \(\gamma\) small the teacher is just the student and the term adds noise. The demo's 0.99 is a starting point, not a rule; the right value depends on the number of steps.

    MixMatch's sharpening is a temperature, and it can overshoot. \(T=0.5\) turns a 60/40 guess into roughly 69/31, which is mild; \(T=0.1\) would turn it into a near-one-hot target and reintroduce the pseudo-label fixed point. The paper's value is tuned for image benchmarks.

    Graph methods are transductive and quadratic. The solve is over the pool, so a new point requires either a new solve or the kNN vote used here, and the \(O(m^3)\) cost rules out the full pool at scale. The graph also assumes that nearby points share a label, which fails at class boundaries that pass through dense regions.

    The graph can leak the test distribution. The demo builds the graph from labelled and unlabelled training points only, and classifies test points by a kNN vote. Including the test inputs in the graph — the standard transductive setup — would use their features without their labels; that is legal, but it changes what "test accuracy" means and needs to be stated.

    Few labels means high variance. The supervised baseline swings between 0.69 and 0.85 across label seeds, and the ranking of the weaker methods moves with it. The demo fixes one seed; the table is a demonstration, not a benchmark. A real comparison needs several seeds and error bars.

    Accuracy hides class imbalance. Both moons are balanced, so overall accuracy is a fair score. On an imbalanced pool, a model that predicts the majority class everywhere can look respectable while being useless, and the consistency losses will happily reinforce it.

    The unlabelled pool is not free. The demo treats 392 points as available without cost. In practice unlabelled data has to be collected, cleaned and stored, and it can be out of distribution relative to the test set. The regulariser is only as good as the distribution it is computed on.

    Applications #

    Pseudo-labelling is the workhorse of industrial semi-supervised pipelines: a strong teacher labels a large corpus, a smaller student trains on the result, and the loop repeats. Soft pseudo-labels and noisy-student training are the same idea with a temperature, and they underpin most of the recent progress in speech and vision with limited labels.

    Consistency regularisation — the mean teacher, FixMatch, and their descendants — is the standard recipe for image classification with a few labels per class, and the same two-view trick appears in self-supervised pretraining. The ladder is the historical ancestor of both the denoising autoencoder and the modern consistency losses.

    Label propagation and its relatives are used wherever the data is naturally a graph: citation networks, social networks, molecules, and the kNN graphs built over embeddings. It is also the cleanest illustration of the semi-supervised bargain — no network, no training, just the assumption that the graph neighbourhood carries the label.
