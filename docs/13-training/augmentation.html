    13 · Training/tier 2/8 variants/source

    Data augmentation — eight policies that make one image many

    Intuition #

    A label says what an image is, not which pixels it must contain. A horizontal bar shifted by one row is still the same class; a photograph with different light is still the same object. Training on the handful of images a dataset happens to contain teaches a model those exact pixels, and augmentation declares the larger set the label actually covers.

    The eight variants declare different sets. mixup and cutmix build new training points between two labelled ones, with targets to match. cutout and random_erasing hide part of an image so the model cannot rely on a single region. randaugment and auto_augment compose several elementary transforms into a policy with a small number of knobs. augmix mixes several random chains of transforms and adds a consistency term. color_jitter changes brightness, contrast, saturation and hue in a random order.

    The demo ends with the sharpest version of the argument. Six labelled images of a bar, randomly placed. A plain network reaches 0.468 on held-out data — chance — because with uniformly random shifts the two classes have identical pixel means, so no linear signal exists. Training with random translations on the same six images reaches 1.000. Mixup and cutmix stay near chance: they smooth a boundary, they do not invent a missing invariance.

    Variants #

    variant family image label needs labels
    mixup mixing convex combination of two mixed one-hot yes
    cutmix mixing a box from another image mixed one-hot yes
    cutout occlusion one square zeroed unchanged no
    random_erasing occlusion sampled rectangle unchanged no
    randaugment policy \(n\) ops at severity \(m\) unchanged no
    augmix policy Dirichlet mixture of chains unchanged no
    color_jitter photometric four ordered factors unchanged no
    auto_augment policy one sampled sub-policy unchanged no

    All eight accept a batch of (B, H, W, C) images in \([0,1]\) and return (images, targets, lam). Targets are always a soft (B, classes) matrix — one-hot unless the variant mixes two rows — so one training loop can consume every policy. lam is the weight of the untouched image and 1.0 for the policies that do not mix.

    Derivation #

    Mixup #

    Draw \(\lambda\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\alpha)\) and a permutation \(\pi\):

    \[\tilde x=\lambda x_i+(1-\lambda)x_{\pi(i)},\qquad \tilde y=\lambda e_{y_i}+(1-\lambda)e_{y_{\pi(i)}}. \qquad (1)\]

    Because cross-entropy is linear in the target, training on \((\tilde x,\tilde y)\) is exactly

    \[L=\lambda\,\mathrm{CE}\!\left(f(\tilde x),y_i\right) +(1-\lambda)\,\mathrm{CE}\!\left(f(\tilde x),y_{\pi(i)}\right), \qquad (2)\]

    and the symmetric Beta has \(\mathbb E[\lambda]=\tfrac12\): the demo's 400 draws average 0.492. The pair is a sample from the line segment between two training points, so the model is asked to interpolate its prediction along that segment. That is the vicinal risk of (1) replacing the empirical risk of the isolated points, and it is why mixup behaves like a regulariser rather than a source of new information.

    CutMix #

    CutMix replaces a rectangle instead of blending everything:

    \[\tilde x_{i}[r]=\begin{cases} x_{\pi(i)}[r], & r\in\text{box},\\ x_i[r], & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases} \qquad \lambda=1-\frac{h\,w}{H\,W}, \qquad (3)\]

    sampling \(h=H\sqrt{1-\lambda_0}\) and \(w=W\sqrt{1-\lambda_0}\) from a Beta draw \(\lambda_0\), then recomputing \(\lambda\) from the clipped box. The recompute matters: rounding \(h,w\) to integers and clamping at the border changes the true area, and the target must use the area that was actually pasted. The demo's box is \(7\times7\), so the returned weight is \(1-49/64=0.234\), not the sampled value.

    Occlusion #

    Cutout and random erasing apply a mask:

    \[\tilde x=M\odot x,\qquad \frac{|\{r:M_r=0\}|}{HW}=\text{coverage}. \qquad (4)\]

    Cutout fixes the coverage and samples the position, which makes the erased fraction exact — the demo's half-width square zeroes exactly 25% of an \(8\times8\) image. Random erasing samples the area from \([s_l,s_h]\) and the aspect ratio log-uniformly from \([r_l,r_h]\), then the position, and applies the mask with probability \(p\). Both are input-space dropout: they force the evidence for a class to be redundant across the image.

    Policies over elementary ops #

    Let \(\mathcal T\) be a set of transforms, each taking a severity \(m\in[0,1]\). RandAugment applies \(n\) of them:

    \[\tilde x=T_{n}\circ\cdots\circ T_{1}(x),\qquad T_j\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal T), \qquad (5)\]

    so the search space is two numbers instead of a table of sub-policies. The demo's severity 0 is the identity by construction, and solarize flips a growing share of a ramp as the severity rises: 0.00, 0.28, 0.62, 0.89.

    AutoAugment keeps a table of sub-policies and samples one per image:

    \[\tilde x=\prod_{(T,p,m)\in\text{policy}} T^{\,\mathbb 1[u

    With three two-op policies and every probability set to 1, the demo draws one policy per image and applies all six ops' worth — 60 policies, 120 ops.

    AugMix #

    AugMix builds \(k\) chains of depth \(d\), mixes them with Dirichlet weights, and adds a consistency term:

    \[\tilde x=\sum_{k}w_k\,b_k,\quad w\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\mathbf 1),\quad b_k=T_{k,d}\circ\cdots\circ T_{k,1}(x), \qquad (7)\]
    \[\mathrm{JSD}(p_0,\dots,p_k)= \frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{j}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_j\,\Big\|\,\bar p\right),\qquad \bar p=\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_j p_j. \qquad (8)\]

    The weights are non-negative and sum to one, so every mixed pixel is a convex combination of the branches — the demo checks that bound pixel by pixel. The JSD is zero for identical branches and positive otherwise (0.8128 here); in a real run the model is trained so the prediction on the mixture agrees with the predictions on the branches, which is what makes the invariance stick.

    Colour jitter #

    Four affine operations, applied in a random order with factors drawn around one:

    \[x'=f_b\,x,\qquad x'=(x-\mu)f_c+\mu,\qquad x'=(x-g)f_s+g, \qquad (9)\]

    where \(\mu\) is the per-image channel mean and \(g\) the per-pixel mean over channels. Hue is a matrix:

    \[x'=H(a)x,\qquad H(a)=\begin{pmatrix} 0.213+0.787c-0.213s & \cdots\\ \vdots & \ddots \end{pmatrix}, \qquad (10)\]

    whose rows sum to one for every \(a\): it moves colour around the wheel without changing a grey pixel. The demo verifies exactly that, to \(3.3\times10^{-16}\). Order matters — contrast after brightness is not brightness after contrast — which is why the four steps are shuffled rather than fixed.

    Why translation cannot be learned from six images #

    Take the two classes to be a white bar, horizontal or vertical, shifted uniformly. For a linear model only the class means matter:

    \[\langle w,\mathbb E[x\mid y=0]\rangle -\langle w,\mathbb E[x\mid y=1]\rangle =\left\langle w,\mu_0-\mu_1\right\rangle, \qquad (11)\]

    and averaging a bar over all shifts makes every pixel equally likely, so \(\mu_0=\mu_1\) and the expression is zero for every \(w\): the linear part of any model carries no signal here, and what is left is second-order structure that six examples cannot pin down. The demo's shallow network lands at 0.468, which is chance with sampling noise. Augmentation changes the training distribution instead: averaging over the shift group projects the learned feature onto the invariant subspace, and the same network reaches 1.000 once the shifts are seen. Mixup interpolates between classes along a line, which is a different subspace entirely — hence 0.476.

    Code walkthrough #

    Mixup is three lines: one Beta draw, one permutation, and the same convex combination applied to pixels and targets:

        def mixup(self, x: np.ndarray, labels: np.ndarray):
        lam = float(self.rng.beta(self.alpha, self.alpha))
        other = self.rng.permutation(x.shape[0])
        images = lam * x + (1.0 - lam) * x[other]
        target = lam * _onehot(labels, self.classes) + (1.0 - lam) * _onehot(
            labels[other], self.classes)
        return images, target, lam

    CutMix overwrites a rectangle and then recomputes the weight from the box it actually pasted — the line that keeps the target honest after integer rounding:

            images = x.copy()
        images[:, top:top + box_h, left:left + box_w] = x[
            other, top:top + box_h, left:left + box_w]
        lam = 1.0 - box_h * box_w / float(height * width)

    Cutout places one square per image and counts the pixels it removes, which is how the demo knows the fraction is exactly the coverage:

            for index in range(x.shape[0]):
            top = int(self.rng.integers(0, height - box_h + 1))
            left = int(self.rng.integers(0, width - box_w + 1))
            out[index, top:top + box_h, left:left + box_w] = 0.0
            self.counts["erased"] += box_h * box_w

    Random erasing samples area and aspect before deriving the box, and the fill mode decides whether the hole is black, the image mean, or noise:

                area = self.rng.uniform(*self.erase_scale) * height * width
            aspect = np.exp(self.rng.uniform(np.log(self.ratio[0]),
                                             np.log(self.ratio[1])))
            box_h = min(height, max(1, int(round(np.sqrt(area / aspect)))))
            box_w = min(width, max(1, int(round(np.sqrt(area * aspect)))))

    The severity gate lives in one place, so every policy inherits the rule that magnitude zero means no change:

        def _apply(self, name: str, image: np.ndarray, magnitude: float,
               rng) -> np.ndarray:
        if magnitude <= 0.0:
            return image
        return OPS[name](image, magnitude, rng)

    AugMix's whole structure is the weight matrix and the sum it drives; keeping the branches on self is what lets the demo test the convexity bound:

            weights = self.rng.dirichlet(np.ones(self.k), size=x.shape[0])
        branches = []
        for _ in range(self.k):
            branch = x.copy()
            for _ in range(self.depth):
                name = str(self.rng.choice(list(self.ops)))
                magnitude = float(self.rng.random()) * self.m / 10.0
                branch = self._apply(name, branch, magnitude, self.rng)
            branches.append(branch)
        mixed = sum(weights[:, index, None, None, None] * branch
                    for index, branch in enumerate(branches))

    The JSD is the mean KL to the mean distribution, with a floor so an exact zero does not produce an infinite logarithm:

            stack = np.stack([np.asarray(p, dtype=float) for p in probs])
        mean = stack.mean(axis=0)
        terms = []
        for member in stack:
            safe = np.maximum(member, 1e-12)
            terms.append(np.sum(safe * (np.log(safe)
                                        - np.log(np.maximum(mean, 1e-12))),
                                axis=-1))
        return np.mean(terms, axis=0)

    Colour jitter is a shuffled sequence of four affine maps; the shuffle is the reason the loop is a loop and not four lines in a fixed order:

            for step in self.rng.permutation(4):
            if step == 0:
                factor = self.rng.uniform(1.0 - self.brightness,
                                          1.0 + self.brightness, (batch, 1, 1, 1))
                out = out * factor

    Hue is the only step that mixes channels, and it does so with a per-image \(3 \times3\) matrix applied by einsum:

                    angle = self.rng.uniform(-self.hue, self.hue, batch)
                matrix = np.stack([_hue_matrix(value) for value in angle])
                out = np.einsum("bij,bhwj->bhwi", matrix, out)

    The training demo's model is a small MLP whose only unusual feature is that its target is a distribution, not an index:

        def step(self, x: np.ndarray, target: np.ndarray, lr: float) -> None:
        probs, hidden = self.probabilities(x)
        difference = (probs - target) / x.shape[0]
        grad_pre = (difference @ self.W2.T) * (1.0 - hidden ** 2)
        self.W2 -= lr * hidden.T @ difference
        self.b2 -= lr * difference.sum(axis=0)
        self.W1 -= lr * x.T @ grad_pre
        self.b1 -= lr * grad_pre.sum(axis=0)

    Complexity #

    For a batch of \(B\) images of size \(H\times W\times C\) with \(N=BHWC\) values:

    • Mixing: mixup is one multiply and one add over \(N\), plus \(O(BC)\) for the targets. CutMix copies at most \(N\) values and recomputes one area.
    • Occlusion: cutout and erasing are \(O(Bhw)\) writes, where \(hw\) is the box area; everything else is a copy of the batch.
    • Policy: each elementary op is \(O(N)\) — rot90 and roll are views or copies, posterize and solarize are elementwise. RandAugment costs \(O(nN)\), AugMix \(O(kdN)\), AutoAugment \(O(B\,|p|\,N/B)=O(|p|N)\).
    • Photometric: four passes over \(N\); hue adds a \(3\times3\) contraction.
    • Memory: every policy needs one extra batch-sized copy, and AugMix holds \(k\) of them plus the branches.
    • The demo: 60 images of \(8\times8\times3\) (1920 values each), four recipes at 600 steps over five seeds. The transforms are microseconds; the 12,000 full-batch training steps dominate and still finish in about a second.

    Failure modes #

    A geometric op that changes the label is not augmentation. The demo's rotate turns a horizontal bar into a vertical one, which flips the class; it is safe only because no demo trains on it. Rotation, shears and crops need label transforms or a dataset where orientation carries no meaning.

    Mixup's permutation must be per batch, not per sample. Applying the same permutation to images and targets is the definition; permuting only the images produces corrupted labels, and permuting with a fresh draw for the target mixes the wrong pair.

    The mixing weight must match the pixels. CutMix's returned \(\lambda\) has to be recomputed after clipping (3); using the sampled \(\lambda_0\) leaves the target inconsistent with the image by the rounding error.

    Alpha at the extremes is not mixing. As \(\alpha\to0\) the Beta concentrates at 0 and 1 and mixup is the identity; as \(\alpha\to\infty\) it concentrates at \(1/2\) and every pair becomes the same 50/50 blend. Both are legitimate settings and neither is the default one.

    Coverage near one destroys the sample. Cutout with coverage 0.9 leaves a sliver of the image; the target survives but the input cannot support it, and the loss turns into noise. Random erasing's upper area bound exists for the same reason.

    Colour jitter clips. Brightness or contrast can push values outside \([0,1]\), and the clip loses the highlight rather than scaling it. In an 8-bit pipeline the same clip is a real information loss.

    Order is part of the policy. Contrast is defined against the image mean, so applying it after brightness gives a different result from before. AutoAugment encodes the order in the sub-policy; a shuffled color_jitter deliberately does not.

    AugMix without the consistency term is just a random blend. The JSD in (8) is what ties the mixture's prediction to the branches'. Drop it and the policy is a weaker form of RandAugment with a larger compute bill.

    Augmenting before splitting leaks. Applying random crops before the train/test split puts near-duplicates of test images into training. Augment the training split only, and generate the test set once, unmodified.

    Test-time augmentation changes calibration. Averaging predictions over augmented test views usually helps accuracy and shifts the confidence scale; thresholds tuned without it no longer apply.

    Augmentation cannot invent information. The demo is the proof: six images with a missing invariance stay at chance under smoothing and jump to 1.000 only when the invariance is supplied. Mixing and erasing regularise; they do not teach a symmetry the training distribution never expresses.

    Seeded policies are not reproducible across frameworks. Which op a policy draws depends on the order of the random stream. The demo fixes one seed per augmenter; a data loader with several workers needs one stream per worker.

    Applications #

    RandAugment, AutoAugment and AugMix are the standard ImageNet recipes, usually combined with mixup or CutMix; the four together are worth several points of top-1 accuracy over plain training. Mixup and CutMix also mix bounding boxes and masks, which is why they are standard in detection and segmentation rather than only in classification.

    Occlusion policies double as robustness training: a model trained with cutout degrades less under real occlusion, and random erasing is used in person and vehicle re-identification where occluders are common. Colour jitter is the cheap way to buy invariance to lighting and camera white balance, and the CSS hue matrix of (10) is the same operator used in browser filters.

    Outside vision, mixup applies to tabular data and to audio spectrograms, and augmentation is the engine of semi-supervised learning: FixMatch pairs a weak and a strong augmentation of the same image and trains the strong view to match the weak view's pseudo-label, which is the consistency idea of (8) taken to its conclusion.
