    13 · Training/tier 3/6 variants/source

    Federated learning — six ways to combine models that never see the data

    Intuition #

    Averaging the weights of models trained on different data does not give you the model trained on all of it. It gives you something between the best client and the worst, and which one you get depends on how far each client walked before the average.

    The demo makes that concrete with five clients and ten classes on a ring. Client j holds classes 2j and 2j+1 and never sees the other eight, so every client is a specialist with a two-way problem. The local budget is deliberately uneven — [40, 40, 40, 40, 200] epochs — because that is what a real deployment looks like when one phone is idle and charging while the other four are busy.

    variant          global   local  worst   comm
centralised       0.778   0.773  0.705     --
fedavg            0.728   0.731  0.430     1x
fedprox           0.770   0.764  0.725     1x
scaffold          0.747   0.758  0.595     2x
secure_agg        0.728   0.731  0.430     1x
dp_fedavg         0.725   0.727  0.485     1x
personalization   0.728   0.892  0.855     1x

    The pooled model scores 0.778 and plain averaging scores 0.728, but the mean is not where the damage is. Client 3 — the one that ran 200 local epochs — scores 0.430 on its own classes under the averaged model and 0.855 under a model fine-tuned on its own data. Five clients averaging to 0.73 hides a model that is unusable for one of them.

    The ranking is not fixed. The three methods cross over:

    round  fedavg  fedprox  scaffold
    1   0.224    0.208     0.224
    3   0.379    0.421     0.547
    5   0.511    0.499     0.684
   10   0.629    0.617     0.716
   20   0.708    0.730     0.739
   30   0.728    0.770     0.747

    Plain averaging needs thirty rounds to pass 0.71; the control-variate method is there at round ten. The proximal term is not faster early — it is a shorter step — but by round 30 it is the best of the three, and it is the only one that repairs the client that overfits.

    What separates the six variants is what travels in those arrows. fedavg sends a model. fedprox sends a model that was not allowed to stray. scaffold sends a model plus a gradient estimate, and pays double the bandwidth. secure_agg sends a model hidden inside random masks. dp_fedavg sends a clipped model plus calibrated noise. personalization sends the same model and then keeps a copy.

    Variants #

    fedavg — average the weights. One round is E_k local epochs per client followed by an unweighted mean. It is the baseline every paper compares against and it is surprisingly hard to beat when clients are balanced; the drift in the table comes from the 5× spread in local work, not from the partition alone.

    fedprox — add \(\frac{\mu}{2}\lVert w-w^{t}\rVert^{2}\) to each local objective with \(\mu=0.1\). The gradient gains a term \(\mu(w-w^{t})\) that grows as the client walks away, so no client can travel more than about \(G/\mu\) from the round's starting point no matter how many epochs it is given. On this run it recovers 0.042 global accuracy and moves the worst client from 0.430 to 0.725, which is better than the pooled model's 0.705 on that client.

    scaffold — give each client a control variate \(c_k\) and the server one \(c\), and correct every local gradient to \(g_k + c - c_k\). The correction makes each client follow the global gradient instead of its own, so it is at 0.716 by round 10 where averaging is at 0.629, and its round-20 accuracy (0.739) already beats what averaging reaches at round 30. It costs twice the upload bandwidth because the variates travel too, and it plateaus at 0.747 rather than at the pooled 0.778, because the correction is only accurate while the local step is small.

    secure_agg — clients add pairwise random masks that cancel in the sum, so the server only ever sees \(\sum_k m_k = \sum_k w_k\). The masks here move one client's vector 29.5 away from its true value while two clients are only 0.7 apart, so a single masked vector says nothing. It is arithmetic, not cryptography: the shares cancel exactly, so the server gets the same sum it would have got anyway, and the masking is only as strong as the assumption that the shares stay secret. Deployed protocols add a secret-sharing round so the shares can be rebuilt when a client drops out mid-round.

    dp_fedavg — clip each client's update to norm \(C=1.0\) and add Gaussian noise with standard deviation \(\sigma C/K\) before averaging. At \(\sigma=0.1\) the accuracy is unchanged (0.725 against 0.727) because the noise is small next to the signal, and the worst client even improves. At \(\sigma=0.2\) the global accuracy falls to 0.599 and at \(0.4\) to 0.521. The mechanism is the same in both cases; only the budget differs.

    personalization — train the global model as usual, then give every client a copy and let it fine-tune for 200 epochs on its own data. The global model is unchanged, so global accuracy is unchanged at 0.728; the mean local accuracy goes from 0.731 to 0.892 and the worst client from 0.430 to 0.855. It is the cheapest win in the table, and the one with a deployment question attached: you now have five models, not one.

    Derivation #

    The objective #

    With \(K\) clients holding \(n_k\) examples each and \(p_k=n_k/n\), federated learning minimises the weighted average of the local risks:

    \[F(w)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_kF_k(w), \qquad F_k(w)=\frac1{n_k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal D_k}\ell(w;x_i,y_i). \qquad (1)\]

    Everything below is a different approximation to the same \(F\). The data is never pooled, so no client ever evaluates \(\nabla F\) — that is the whole constraint, and it is why the algorithm has to reconstruct a global direction out of \(K\) local ones.

    What one round actually does #

    Let all clients run \(E\) local steps from \(w^{t}\), writing \(w_k^{t,e}\) for the iterate after \(e\) steps of round \(t\), and let \(\eta\) be the local step size. Since aggregation is a plain average of models,

    \[w^{t+1}=w^{t}-\eta\sum_k p_k\sum_{e=0}^{E-1}\nabla F_k(w_k^{t,e}). \qquad (2)\]

    Add and subtract the honest gradient step \(E\nabla F(w^{t})\):

    \[w^{t+1}=w^{t}-\eta E\nabla F(w^{t}) +\eta\sum_k p_k\sum_{e=0}^{E-1}\left[\nabla F_k(w^{t}) -\nabla F_k(w_k^{t,e})\right]. \qquad (3)\]

    The first two terms are \(E\) steps of gradient descent on (1). The third is client drift: the average over clients of how much each local gradient has turned away from the value it had at the start of the round. It is a bias, not noise, and it does not average out over rounds because it is a function of the partition, which is fixed.

    The size of the drift #

    Assume \(\nabla F_k\) is \(L\)-Lipschitz and bounded by \(G\). After \(e\) local steps \(\lVert w_k^{t,e}-w^{t}\rVert\le\eta eG\le\eta EG\), so each bracket in (3) is at most \(L\eta EG\) and the sum over \(E\) steps gives

    \[\left\lVert\text{drift}\right\rVert \;\le\;\eta L\sum_k p_k\sum_{e=0}^{E-1}\eta eG \;=\;\frac{L\eta^{2}G\,E(E-1)}{2}. \qquad (4)\]

    Drift is quadratic in the step size and quadratic in the number of local epochs. That single line explains the whole design space: every method below is a way of making one of those two factors smaller without giving up the compute saved by local work. Cut \(E\) to one and you have centralised SGD with \(K\) times the communication; cut \(\eta\) and you barely move.

    FedProx as a trust region #

    The proximal objective

    \[\min_{w}\;F_k(w)+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\lVert w-w^{t}\right\rVert^{2} \qquad (5)\]

    has a stationary point satisfying \(\nabla F_k(w_k^{*})+\mu(w_k^{*}-w^{t})=0\), so

    \[\left\lVert w_k^{*}-w^{t}\right\rVert =\frac{\left\lVert\nabla F_k(w_k^{*})\right\rVert}{\mu} \;\le\;\frac{G}{\mu}. \qquad (6)\]

    The bound does not mention \(E\) or \(\eta\). A client with 200 epochs and a client with ten are both confined to a ball of radius \(G/\mu\) around the round's start, which is exactly the property you want when the local budgets are unequal. The price is the same bound applied to clients that were behaving: they are slowed down too, which is why fedprox is the slowest of the three in the early rounds.

    SCAFFOLD as variance reduction #

    Each client keeps \(c_k\), the server keeps \(c\), and the local step becomes

    \[w_k\leftarrow w_k-\eta\left(\nabla F_k(w_k)+c-c_k\right). \qquad (7)\]

    If \(c_k=\nabla F_k(w^{t})\) and \(c=\nabla F(w^{t})\), the bracket is \(\nabla F_k(w_k)-\nabla F_k(w^{t})+\nabla F(w^{t})\): the client's own gradient with its start-of-round value removed, plus the global one. The leading term of the drift in (3) is cancelled by construction, and what is left is second order in \(\eta E\).

    The variates have to be estimated without a second pass over the data. After the local run, the average step is available for free:

    \[c_k=\frac{w^{t}-w_k^{t,E}}{E\eta},\qquad c=\sum_k p_kc_k. \qquad (8)\]

    Equation (8) is the non-obvious line of the atom. The displacement divided by the total step size is the mean of the \(E\) gradients the client actually used, which is an estimate of \(\nabla F_k\) at the start of the round — no extra gradient evaluations, one extra vector per client on the wire.

    Differential privacy #

    With a clipping norm \(C\), client \(k\)'s update becomes

    \[\tilde\Delta_k=\Delta_k\cdot\min\left(1,\frac{C}{\lVert\Delta_k\rVert}\right), \qquad w^{t+1}=w^{t}-\frac1K\left(\sum_k\tilde\Delta_k+\mathcal N(0,\sigma^{2}C^{2}I)\right). \qquad (9)\]

    Changing one client's data changes the clipped sum by at most \(2C\), so the mechanism is \((\varepsilon,\delta)\)-DP whenever

    \[\sigma\;\ge\;\frac{\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)}}{\varepsilon}. \qquad (10)\]

    The noise enters as \(\sigma C/K\) per coordinate because it is added to the sum and then divided by \(K\). The signal-to-noise ratio of a round is therefore \(K/(\sigma\sqrt d)\): with \(K=5\) and \(d=922\) that is \(0.17/\sigma\), which is what puts the demo's cliff between \(\sigma=0.1\) and \(\sigma=0.2\). Deployments use thousands of clients per round precisely because the ratio is linear in \(K\).

    Secure aggregation #

    Client \(k\) samples a share \(s_{kj}\) with every client \(j>k\) and sends

    \[m_k=w_k+\sum_{j>k}s_{kj}-\sum_{j

    so every share appears twice in the sum with opposite signs:

    \[\sum_km_k=\sum_kw_k. \qquad (12)\]

    The server learns the sum and nothing else, as long as it does not collude with a client and no client drops out. Dropout is the hard part: if client \(j\) leaves after collecting its shares, the terms no longer cancel, and the protocol has to fall back on secret-sharing a reconstruction of the dropped client's shares — which is why real secure aggregation implementations are much longer than eleven lines.

    Personalisation #

    The two-level objective is

    \[\min_{w}\;\sum_kp_kF_k(v_k) \quad\text{subject to}\quad v_k\in\arg\min_{v}F_k(v), \qquad (13)\]

    approximated here by letting \(v_k\) be \(P\) local steps from the global model,

    \[v_k=\mathrm{local\_sgd}(w,\mathcal D_k,P,\eta), \qquad (14)\]

    which is a proximal step on \(F_k\) with penalty \(1/(2P\eta)\) rather than an exact minimiser. This is the cheapest form of the idea; the literature's versions either meta-learn \(w\) so that a few steps reach a good \(v_k\) (maml, in this chapter) or learn \(w\) and \(v_k\) jointly with an explicit penalty.

    Why averaging weights is not averaging functions #

    A last piece of pessimism that the numbers hide. For a nonlinear model,

    \[\sum_kp_kf_{w_k}(x)\;\ne\;f_{\bar w}(x), \qquad (15)\]

    with \(\bar w=\sum_kp_kw_k\). Two clients whose hidden units have permuted roles produce an average with no units in either role. Nothing in the algorithm fixes this; it is why averaged models lose accuracy even when every client's local model is good, and the reason the literature contains everything from matching permutations before averaging to never averaging at all.

    Code walkthrough #

    The partition is where the difficulty comes from, so it comes first. Client j draws from exactly two of the ten centres, which makes every client's local problem well posed and the global one harder than any single local one.

        for j in range(CLIENTS):
        rows, labels = [], []
        for c in (2 * j, 2 * j + 1):
            rows.append(centres[c] + rng.normal(0.0, SPREAD, (per_class, 2)))
            labels.append(np.full(per_class, c))
        x = np.vstack(rows)
        y = np.concatenate(labels)

    Every variant shares one local solver. mu and the control variates are optional terms in the same loop, which is the point: the three methods differ in one gradient correction each, not in their training code.

        for _ in range(epochs):
        grads = _grad(current, x, y)
        for key, grad in grads.items():
            if c is not None:
                grad = grad + c[key] - ci[key]
            if mu:
                grad = grad + mu * (current[key] - anchor[key])
            current[key] = current[key] - lr * grad

    anchor has to be a separate dictionary. current is stepped in place, so a penalty written against current would be identically zero and mu would silently do nothing; passing the round's starting model in is what makes the term a pull toward the place the client began. The SCAFFOLD correction is a plain addition to the same gradient, which is why the two compose without special cases.

    The dispatch in fit is one branch per aggregation rule. scaffold needs the local models and their drift estimates, so it cannot share the common path.

                    drift = [{k: (self.model[k] - q[k]) / (epochs[j] * self.lr)
                          for k in q} for j, q in enumerate(updates)]
                self.model = self._average(updates)
                c = {k: np.mean([d[k] for d in drift], axis=0) for k in c}
                ci = drift

    epochs[j] * self.lr is the denominator in (8), and it must be the client's own budget. Using one client's budget for all of them mis-scales four of the five variates, the correction stops cancelling the drift, and the run collapses to 0.100 — chance on ten classes, from 0.747. This is the line to get wrong.

                    if self.variant == "dp_fedavg":
                    self.model = self._noisy_average(updates, turn)
                elif self.variant == "secure_agg":
                    self.model = self._masked_average(updates, turn)
                else:
                    self.model = self._average(updates)

    The DP path clips before it averages, and it counts a clip whenever the scale factor binds. self.clipped is the honest diagnostic: at \(C=1.0\) only 9 of the 150 client updates in the run ever hit the norm, which is why the noiseless \(\sigma=0\) row scores the same as fedavg.

            for update in updates:
            delta = {key: self.model[key] - update[key] for key in update}
            scales.append(min(1.0, self.clip / (_norm(delta) + 1e-12)))
            deltas.append(delta)

    The noise is added to the average, so its scale is \(\sigma C/K\); adding it per client instead would give \(\sigma C/\sqrt K\) and silently buy accuracy with privacy.

                noise = rng.normal(0.0, self.noise * self.clip / n,
                               self.model[key].shape)
            out[key] = self.model[key] - mean + noise

    Masking is sign bookkeeping. Client i adds the share it owns for every j>i and subtracts the share j owns for every j<i; each share is created exactly once by the lower-indexed client, so the two appearances are the same numbers.

            for j in range(n):
            if j > i:
                for key in out:
                    out[key] = out[key] + share[i, j][key]
            elif j < i:
                for key in out:
                    out[key] = out[key] - share[j, i][key]

    The demo's 4.4e-16 residual is floating-point summation order, not a leak. The masks cancel as real numbers; they do not cancel bit for bit, because the sum is evaluated left to right and each share's rounding depends on where it sits.

    Personalisation happens after the loop, so the global model is exactly the fedavg model and the comparison in the table is fair.

            if self.variant == "personalization":
            self.personal = [local_sgd(self.model, x, y, self.finetune, self.lr)
                             for x, y in clients]

    Evaluation falls back to the global model whenever there is no personal copy, so local_metrics means "what this client would actually run" for every variant.

            models = self.personal if self.personal is not None else (
            [self.model] * len(tests))

    Complexity #

    One round costs \(\sum_k n_kE_kd\) multiply-adds, with \(d=2\cdot24+24\cdot24+24\cdot10+58=922\) parameters; the demo's 30 rounds over 864k example-epochs is seven seconds of numpy. The pooled reference needs the same number of example-epochs and is not faster in wall-clock terms, because its matmuls are \(K\) times wider.

    Communication is the actual constraint: one model up and one down per client per round, so \(2Kd\) floats. scaffold also uploads \(c_k\), which makes it \(3Kd\) and 1.5× the traffic — the comm column says \(2\times\) because it counts uploads only. At \(d=922\) the upload is 9,220 floats, or 74 kB per round for five clients: trivial. At a language model's \(d=10^{10}\) it is the reason the field exists.

    Memory is one model on the server plus one model and one variate per client, and in secure_agg one share per pair — \(\binom{K}{2}d\) floats, which is the real cost of masking and the reason secure aggregation is applied to a subsampled round rather than to everyone.

    The stopping criterion is a separate budget: the demo trains for a fixed 30 rounds and reports where each variant got to. Real deployments stop on a validation signal, which is the early_stopping atom with a global model and a per-client average.

    Failure modes #

    The gap depends on the operating point, and the demo picks one that shows it. The same run at \(\eta=0.015\) is worse (0.711 for fedavg), because a smaller step means clients travel less per round and the run is cut short at 30 rounds; raise it to \(\eta=0.05\) and the drift disappears — fedavg reaches 0.781, above the pooled reference at 0.778 and above scaffold's 0.743. The table is a measurement at one operating point, not a ranking. The diagnostic is to plot global accuracy against local epochs with everything else fixed: a method that is drift-limited has a monotone gap, one that is step-size-limited does not care.

    fedprox does not improve monotonically in \(\mu\). The same run gives 0.770 at \(\mu=0.1\), 0.750 at \(\mu=1\) and 0.757 at \(\mu=10\). The bound in (6) is a cap, not a target: once \(\mu\eta\) is large enough that the proximal term dominates, every client returns essentially \(w^{t}\) and the round becomes one small averaged step, which is slow centralised training with a round trip in the middle — it never drifts, and it never uses the local compute either. The sweet spot is the smallest \(\mu\) that stops the worst client, and it has to be found, not assumed.

    scaffold is out of its regime when \(\eta E\) is large. The correction in (7) assumes the client's gradient does not change much along the local trajectory. At \(\eta E=12\) — 600 local epochs at \(\eta=0.02\) — the variates are stale by the time they are applied, and the run scores 0.528 against fedavg's 0.693. The correction is a first-order argument; it removes the drift term only while the second-order term is small. Keep \(\eta E\) to a few units, or accept that the method degrades faster than the thing it was meant to fix.

    DP noise at five clients is not a utility trade-off, it is a utility cliff. Each coordinate gets \(\sigma C/K\) of noise while the model has \(d=922\) of them, so the total noise norm is \(\sigma C\sqrt d/K\approx 6.1\sigma\). At \(\sigma=0.1\) that is 0.61 against update norms of about 1.0 — survivable. At \(\sigma=0.2\) it is 1.2, larger than the signal, and accuracy drops 0.13 in one step. The demo's \(\sigma=0.1\) row is a small budget: five clients with 400 examples between them cannot support a meaningful \(\varepsilon\).

    Masks hide values only while the round is complete. Drop one client from mask_updates and the shares it was supposed to cancel survive, so the sum is wrong by a masked amount nobody can remove until those shares are reconstructed. The masking also says nothing about a client that lies: a malicious client can send any vector it likes, and the server will average it in without ever seeing it. Both problems are why deployed secure aggregation is a multi-round protocol with commitments and secret-shared recovery, and why dp_fedavg is usually layered on top. The demo's 29.5-versus-0.7 statistic is the passive, complete round only.

    A personal model is a deployment commitment. personalization lifts the mean local accuracy by 0.16 here because every client has 80 points of its own. With ten points it overfits; with no labelled data it cannot run at all, and the global model is then the only model you have. The demo's fallback in local_metrics exists for exactly that case: a client with no personal copy must still be served.

    Averaging assumes the clients are telling the truth about their data. The partition here is honest label skew. A client that duplicates its data, or weights it, moves the global model toward itself — and with the masks of secure_agg the server cannot see it happening. Detection needs a different mechanism entirely (contribution accounting, robust aggregation), and it is out of scope for a file this long.

    Applications #

    Federated averaging is what runs when the data cannot move: keyboard prediction trained on phones (Gboard), next-word models on messaging apps, handwriting models, and the clinical-imaging work where patient records stay inside the hospital. The same machinery is used for less obvious reasons — a bank with regulated regional subsidiaries, a manufacturer whose machines are on customer premises, a consortium that wants a shared model without a shared dataset.

    fedprox is the standard fix for uneven participation: mobile devices have wildly different amounts of compute available per charging window, and the proximal term is what stops the phones that ran 200 epochs from drowning out the ones that ran 20. scaffold is the choice when the number of rounds is the bottleneck, at twice the bandwidth.

    DP-FedAvg is deployed at scale with tens of thousands of clients per round, where the \(1/K\) noise averaging in (9) is what makes the budget affordable, and with secure aggregation layered on top so the server cannot inspect the clipped updates it is averaging. personalization is what most real products actually ship: one global model for cold start, one fine-tuned model per user for everything after.

    The trap is treating this as a privacy technique by itself. Averaging weights is not DP; without clipping and noise, and without masking, a curious server can reconstruct training examples from updates. The two privacy variants here are mechanisms, and both of them are load-bearing.
