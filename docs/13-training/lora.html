    LoRA and parameter-efficient fine-tuning — nine ways to train a few numbers

    Intuition #

    Fine-tuning a large model rewrites every weight, and the rewrite is usually small: the update lives in a much lower-dimensional subspace than the weight matrix itself. LoRA takes that observation literally and parameterises the update as a product of two thin matrices, so a \(d\times k\) weight becomes \(r(d+k)\) trainable numbers with \(r\ll\min(d,k)\).

    Everything else in this atom is a different answer to the same question — what should the small trainable part be? dora splits each weight into a magnitude and a direction and trains them separately. adalora gives every rank its own scale and prunes the useless ones. rslora fixes a scaling bug that appears as the rank grows. loha and lokr replace the product with a Hadamard or Kronecker product, which costs more parameters but represents different updates. qlora shrinks the frozen base to four bits so the adapter fits on a small device. prefix_tuning and prompt_tuning do not touch weights at all: they learn extra key-value pairs or extra input tokens.

    The demo freezes a small attention block trained on one three-class task, changes the class centres, and then trains each adapter on the new task. The frozen model scores 0.283. All seven weight adapters reach 1.000 with between 74 and 460 trainable numbers against a base of 1091. The two token adapters reach 0.68: they can steer the frozen head but not rewrite it, which is the honest version of the PEFT trade.

    Variants #

    variant trains update parameters per matrix
    lora two thin factors \(BA\) \(r(d+k)\)
    qlora LoRA on a 4-bit base \(BA\) \(r(d+k)\)
    dora LoRA plus a magnitude \(m\odot V/\lVert V\rVert\) \(r(d+k)+d\)
    adalora an SVD triple \(P\Lambda Q\) \(r(d+k)+r\)
    rslora LoRA, scaled by \(\sqrt r\) \(BA\) \(r(d+k)\)
    loha two products, Hadamard \((B_1A_1)\odot(B_2A_2)\) \(2r(d+k)\)
    lokr two rank-one products \((B_1A_1)\otimes(B_2A_2)\) \(m_1+n_1+m_2+n_2\)
    prefix_tuning key and value prefixes \([K_p;XW_k]\) \(2Ld\) in total
    prompt_tuning input tokens \([P;X]\) \(Ld\) in total

    There is one class and one training loop. The adapter is selected by name and everything else — the frozen block, the loss, the optimiser — is shared. That is the property that matters in practice: swapping LoRA for DoRA is a constructor argument, not a rewrite.

    Derivation #

    Low-rank updates #

    Let \(W_0\in\mathbb R^{d\times k}\) be frozen and let the update be

    \[W=W_0+\frac{\alpha}{r}\,BA,\qquad B\in\mathbb R^{d\times r},\quad A\in\mathbb R^{r\times k}, \qquad (1)\]

    with \(B\) initialised to zero so training starts exactly at the base model. The trainable count is

    \[\underbrace{r(d+k)}_{\text{adapter}}\quad\text{instead of}\quad \underbrace{dk}_{\text{dense}}, \qquad (2)\]

    which for \(d=k=4096\) and \(r=8\) is 65,536 against 16.7 million — a factor of 256. For a loss \(L\) and \(G=\partial L/\partial W\),

    \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial B}=\frac{\alpha}{r}\,GA^{\top},\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial A}=\frac{\alpha}{r}\,B^{\top}G, \qquad (3)\]

    so the backward pass costs one \(d\times r\) and one \(r\times k\) matmul on top of whatever produced \(G\).

    Why the scale is \(\alpha/r\), and why that breaks #

    Take both factors to have entries of standard deviation \(\sigma\). Each entry of the product is a sum of \(r\) products,

    \[\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\Delta W_{ij}\right) =\sum_{k=1}^{r}\mathrm{Var}(B_{ik}A_{kj})=r\sigma^4,\qquad \mathrm{std}=\sigma^2\sqrt r. \qquad (4)\]

    Dividing by \(r\) leaves a magnitude of \(\sigma^2/\sqrt r\), which shrinks as the rank grows: doubling the rank halves the effective update. rslora divides by \(\sqrt r\) instead, leaving \(\sigma^2\) constant. The demo prints the two scales side by side: at \(r=16\) with \(\alpha=8\), \(\alpha/r=0.5\) and \(\alpha/\sqrt r=2.0\).

    QLoRA: a four-bit base #

    QLoRA leaves \(W_0\) in a 4-bit format and learns the same LoRA branch in 16-bit. The format is NF4, whose 16 levels are the quantiles of a standard normal, so the codebook is matched to weights rather than to a uniform grid. Each block of \(b\) weights gets its own scale \(s=\max|w|\), and

    \[q(w)=\arg\min_{n\in\text{NF4}}\left|w/s-n\right|,\qquad \hat w=s\,n_{q(w)}. \qquad (5)\]

    The scales themselves are quantised again to 8 bits — double quantisation — which costs one byte per block instead of four. For a \(256\times256\) matrix with \(b=64\) the demo reports 33,800 bytes against 262,144, a factor of 7.76 and 4.13 bits per weight. The dequantised base differs from the fp32 one by at most 0.122 per weight, and it is frozen, so that error is a constant offset the adapter can absorb.

    DoRA: magnitude and direction #

    DoRA writes the weight as a magnitude vector times a unit-norm direction:

    \[W=m\odot\frac{V}{\lVert V\rVert_c},\qquad V=W_0+\frac{\alpha}{r}BA, \qquad (6)\]

    where \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_c\) is the norm of each column and \(m\) is initialised to the column norms of \(W_0\), so \(W=W_0\) at step zero. The gradients follow from the quotient rule:

    \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial m_j}=\sum_i G_{ij}\,\hat W_{ij},\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial V_{ij}} =\frac{m_jG_{ij}-\hat W_{ij}\sum_l m_lG_{lj}\hat W_{lj}} {\lVert V_j\rVert}. \qquad (7)\]

    The demo verifies the invariant directly: after training, every column norm of every adapted matrix equals its learned magnitude to \(4.4\times10^{-16}\).

    AdaLoRA: rank as a budget #

    AdaLoRA keeps an explicit SVD-like triple and a penalty that keeps the factors orthogonal:

    \[W=W_0+P\Lambda Q,\qquad \mathcal R=\lVert P^{\top}P-I\rVert_F^2+\lVert QQ^{\top}-I\rVert_F^2, \qquad (8)\]

    with \(P\in\mathbb R^{d\times r}\), \(\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda)\) and \(Q\in\mathbb R^{r\times k}\). Each rank gets an importance score

    \[s_i=|\lambda_i|\,\lVert P_{:,i}\rVert_2\,\lVert Q_{i,:}\rVert_2, \qquad (9)\]

    and ranks are pruned by zeroing the smallest. The demo's four scores are 1.82, 1.71, 2.36 and 2.95; dropping the two lowest costs 0.042 of validation loss, dropping the two highest costs 0.344. Note that the check is on validation data, not the test set — a pruning decision made on test data is not a decision you can make in practice.

    LoHa and LoKr #

    LoHa replaces the sum with a Hadamard product of two low-rank products:

    \[\Delta W=(B_1A_1)\odot(B_2A_2), \qquad (10)\]

    which costs \(2r(d+k)\) and can represent interactions the single product cannot, because the elementwise product mixes the two rank spaces. LoKr goes further and uses a Kronecker product of rank-one factors:

    \[\Delta W=(B_1A_1)\otimes(B_2A_2),\qquad \Delta W_{i m_2+j,\,k n_2+l}=(B_1A_1)_{ik}(B_2A_2)_{jl}, \qquad (11)\]

    with \(d=m_1m_2\) and \(k=n_1n_2\). Its parameter count is \(m_1+n_1+m_2+n_2\) — 74 in the demo, the smallest weight adapter of the nine. The backward pass reshapes \(G\) to \((m_1,m_2,n_1,n_2)\) and contracts with the other factor:

    \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial(B_1A_1)} =\sum_{j,l}G_{ijkl}\,(B_2A_2)_{jl}, \qquad (12)\]

    which is the line that a plain sum over the reshaped tensor gets wrong.

    Prefix and prompt tuning #

    These two change the sequence instead of the weights. Prompt tuning prepends \(L\) learnable vectors to the input, and prefix tuning prepends learnable keys and values after the projection:

    \[X'=[P;X],\qquad K=[K_p;X'W_k],\qquad V=[V_p;X'W_v], \qquad (13)\]

    so attention runs over \(L+T\) keys while the queries still come from the real tokens. The counts are \(Ld\) and \(2Ld\), the smallest of the nine. Because the prompt is shared across the batch, its gradient is a sum over examples:

    \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial P}=\sum_{b=1}^{B} \left.\frac{\partial L}{\partial X'_{b}}\right|_{1:L}, \qquad (14)\]

    a reduction that is easy to forget when the prompt is stored as a batch of copies.

    Code walkthrough #

    Every weight adapter has the same shape of implementation: build the effective matrix, cache what the backward pass needs, and return it. LoRA is the three-line case:

            if self.variant in ("lora", "qlora", "rslora"):
            left = self.trainable[f"{name}.A"]
            right = self.trainable[f"{name}.B"]
            self._cache[name] = {"A": left, "B": right}
            return base + self.scale * (right @ left)

    Its backward pass is the pair of products in (3), with the scale applied once:

            if self.variant in ("lora", "qlora", "rslora"):
            self.gradients[f"{name}.A"] += self.scale * (
                cached["B"].T @ dweight)
            self.gradients[f"{name}.B"] += self.scale * (
                dweight @ cached["A"].T)

    DoRA adds the magnitude and its quotient-rule correction; note that the unit direction is what the gradients see, and the magnitude is a plain projection:

                unit, norm = cached["unit"], cached["norm"]
            self.gradients[f"{name}.m"] += (dweight * unit).sum(axis=0)
            dunit = dweight * self.trainable[f"{name}.m"]
            dv = (dunit - unit * (unit * dunit).sum(axis=0)) / norm

    AdaLoRA's three gradients are one contraction each, and the penalty term is the gradient of the orthogonality loss:

                self.gradients[f"{name}.lam"] += np.einsum(
                "ok,oi,ki->k", left, dweight, right)
            self.gradients[f"{name}.P"] += (dweight @ right.T) * middle
            self.gradients[f"{name}.Q"] += (left.T @ dweight) * middle[:, None]

    LoKr is where the reshape matters: the transposed four-dimensional view is contracted with the partner factor, not merely summed:

                shaped = dweight.reshape(rows[0], rows[1], cols[0], cols[1])
            first = (self.trainable[f"{name}.B1"]
                     @ self.trainable[f"{name}.A1"])
            second = (self.trainable[f"{name}.B2"]
                      @ self.trainable[f"{name}.A2"])
            dfirst = np.einsum("ijkl,jl->ik", shaped, second)
            dsecond = np.einsum("ijkl,ik->jl", shaped, first)

    The NF4 packer is the quantile search of (5) plus the second-level scale quantisation:

        blocks = flat.reshape(-1, block)
    scales = np.abs(blocks).max(axis=1) / 1.0
    scales = np.where(scales > 0.0, scales, 1.0)
    normalised = blocks / scales[:, None]
    codes = np.abs(normalised[..., None] - NF4).argmin(axis=-1)

    The block forward is where prefix tuning enters: the prefix is broadcast across the batch and concatenated in front of the projected keys and values:

        if prefix is not None:
        keys = np.concatenate(
            [np.broadcast_to(prefix["K"], (batch,) + prefix["K"].shape),
             keys], axis=1)
        values = np.concatenate(
            [np.broadcast_to(prefix["V"], (batch,) + prefix["V"].shape),
             values], axis=1)

    and the backward pass takes the prefix rows, reduces them over the batch, then removes them before the projection gradients are formed:

        left = cache["prefix"]
    if left:
        dprefix = {"K": dkeys[:, :left].sum(axis=0),
                   "V": dvalues[:, :left].sum(axis=0)}
        dkeys = dkeys[:, left:]
        dvalues = dvalues[:, left:]

    The shared training loop is small because every adapter exposes the same three methods — build, backward, step:

            for _ in range(steps):
            loss, state = self.forward(x, y)
            self.backward(state)
            self.step(lr)
            history.append(loss)

    Complexity #

    For a batch \(B\), sequence \(T\), width \(d\), rank \(r\) and a target matrix of shape \(d\times k\):

    • Adapter forward: an extra \(O(Brd)\) and \(O(Brk)\) matmul per adapted matrix, against \(O(Bdk)\) for the frozen one. At \(r=8\) and \(d=k=4096\) the adapter is 0.4% of the matmul work.
    • Adapter backward: the same two matmuls again, so the same fraction.
    • Memory: \(r(d+k)\) trainable numbers and their optimiser state; the frozen base in fp32, or 4.13 bits per weight for QLoRA.
    • Token adapters: \(Ld\) or \(2Ld\) parameters, and attention cost grows from \(O(T^2d)\) to \(O(T(T+L)d)\) — cheap in parameters, not in compute.
    • The demo: nine variants at 300 steps over 120 examples, a 1091-parameter base, plus a finite-difference check over about 1,600 adapter entries. The whole run is about a second, and the gradient check is most of it.

    Failure modes #

    The rank can exceed the dense budget. At \(r=4\) in the demo, LoRA already uses 460 parameters against 816 dense for four matrices, and at \(r=16\) it is more than twice the dense count. The demo's table prints the crossover; the right rank is the smallest one that fits the task, not the largest that fits memory.

    The \(\alpha/r\) scale shrinks with the rank. Equation (4) is not a regularisation trick, it is a bug: the same learning rate produces smaller updates as \(r\) grows. rslora's \(\alpha/\sqrt r\) is the fix, and the demo's scale table is the check.

    Zero initialisation freezes half the adapter for one step. With \(B=0\) the gradient of \(A\) is \(B^{\top}G=0\), so \(A\) cannot move until \(B\) does. The demo warms every adapter for five steps before its finite-difference check, which is also why a gradient check at initialisation would pass trivially.

    Quantising the adapter as well as the base wastes the point. QLoRA keeps the LoRA branch in 16-bit precisely so the small, precise updates are not rounded. A four-bit adapter on a four-bit base has nowhere to put an update smaller than its own grid.

    The NF4 block size is a range/resolution trade. Small blocks track outliers better and spend more bytes on scales; large blocks save memory and clip the tail. The demo's block of 64 is the QLoRA default, not a law.

    Merging an adapter is a one-way door. \(W_0\leftarrow W_0+\Delta W\) removes the parameter saving and makes the adapter indistinguishable from the base. Merging two adapters into one base is only exact when they act on the same subspace; otherwise the cross terms are lost.

    DoRA's norm is over the wrong axis the moment you transpose a weight. Convolution weights are usually stored as (out, in, k, k), and the "column norm" of a flattened matrix is not the per-output-channel norm. The demo checks the invariant explicitly for this reason.

    AdaLoRA collapses without the orthogonality penalty. The triple \(P\Lambda Q\) is over-parameterised, so nothing stops \(P\) and \(Q\) from growing while \(\Lambda\) shrinks. The penalty in (8) is what keeps the importance scores comparable across ranks.

    Rank pruning must use validation data. The demo computes both the floor and the pruned loss on a held-out split; computing them on the test set turns the pruning decision into test-set tuning.

    A random prefix is not neutral. Prefix tuning starts with random key-value vectors, which perturb attention from step zero, unlike the zero-initialised LoRA. That is a real difference in the optimisation landscape, not just in the parameter count.

    A soft prompt cannot rewrite a frozen head. The demo's token adapters reach 0.68 while the weight adapters reach 1.000. If the head's bias is the problem, only a weight update can fix it; if the task is a matter of conditioning, a prompt is enough. Choosing between them is a question about where the missing information lives.

    Adapters on the wrong modules do nothing. LoRA on the attention projections and LoRA on the MLP learn different things; the usual recipe is to adapt every linear layer and check the parameter budget. The demo's targets argument is the same choice, exposed.

    Applications #

    LoRA is the default way to fine-tune open language models: one base in memory, one small adapter per task, and a serving stack that swaps adapters per request. QLoRA made 65-billion-parameter fine-tuning fit on a single 48 GB GPU, and the NF4 format of (5) is now a standard storage format for frozen weights.

    DoRA and AdaLoRA are refinements used when a plain low-rank update underfits: DoRA when the magnitude of the weights matters, AdaLoRA when the rank budget should be allocated per layer. LoHa and LoKr target convolutional and structured layers where a single low-rank product is a poor fit, and they are common in diffusion-model fine-tuning, where LoRA itself is the standard customisation mechanism.

    Prefix and prompt tuning sit at the other end: they change what the model sees rather than what it knows, which makes them cheap, composable, and easy to serve — a prompt is a row of numbers, not a weight file. They are weaker when the frozen model's decision boundary is wrong rather than merely misdirected, which is exactly the 0.68 against 1.000 in the demo.
