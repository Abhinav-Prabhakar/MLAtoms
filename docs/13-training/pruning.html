    13 · Training/tier 2/8 variants/source

    Pruning — eight ways to delete weights and keep the accuracy

    Intuition #

    A trained network contains far more weights than the task needs, so most of them can be set to zero with little loss. That part is easy. The two decisions that actually matter are which weights to delete and when to delete them, and the eight variants here are different answers to those two questions.

    The implementation makes the deletion explicit with a binary mask per matrix. The mask multiplies the weight in the forward pass, so a pruned weight contributes nothing, and it multiplies the gradient in the backward pass, so a pruned weight never moves again. Every variant then reduces to a rule for filling those masks: the weight's magnitude, its accumulated movement, the norm of the hidden unit it belongs to, the singular values of the matrix, or a schedule that grows the masks over training.

    The demo trains a 643-parameter MLP on a three-class problem, then compresses it. At 80% sparsity seven of the eight variants keep at least 0.979 accuracy, which says something about how redundant the model is, not about how clever the criteria are. The separation appears at 98%: a single prune collapses to 0.333, iterative pruning reaches 0.662, rewinding to the initial weights reaches 0.967, and a gradual schedule reaches 0.996. When the budget is genuinely tight, when matters more than which.

    Variants #

    variant score unit decision
    magnitude \(\lvert w\rvert\) weight iterative, per layer
    one_shot \(\lvert w\rvert\) weight once, after training
    unstructured \(\lvert w\rvert\) weight once, global threshold
    movement \(\sum_t\lvert w^{(t)}g^{(t)}\rvert\) weight once, accumulated
    structured \(\lVert W_{:,j}\rVert_2\lVert W_{j,:}\rVert_2\) hidden unit once
    gradual \(\lvert w\rvert\) weight cubic schedule during training
    lottery_ticket \(\lvert w\rvert\) weight iterative, rewind to init
    svd_prune singular values rank low-rank factorisation

    The schedule column is the interesting one once the budget gets tight. The score column decides which weights die; the schedule decides whether the network gets a chance to route around the damage.

    Derivation #

    The masked network #

    Let \(M_l\) be a binary mask the shape of \(W_l\). The forward pass and the gradient are both masked:

    \[h=\tanh\!\left((W_1\odot M_1)\,x+b_1\right),\qquad z=(W_2\odot M_2)\,h+b_2, \qquad (1)\]
    \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_l}=M_l\odot G_l,\qquad W_l\leftarrow W_l-\eta\,M_l\odot G_l. \qquad (2)\]

    Equation (2) is why a mask is not merely a view: an entry with \(M_{ij}=0\) has zero gradient, so it stays exactly where it was and can be restored later without a saved copy. The pruned values are dead weight in memory but they are never read by the forward pass, which is why n_stored counts the mask rather than the array.

    For a matrix with \(k\) survivors this costs

    \[\text{stored}=k+\underbrace{\dim b_1+\dim b_2}_{\text{never pruned}}, \qquad (3)\]

    so the demo's per-layer 80% model keeps 121 of 608 weights plus 35 biases and reports 156 numbers against 643.

    What to remove #

    The simplest score is the weight itself. Magnitude pruning keeps the \(k\) largest entries,

    \[M_{ij}=\mathbf{1}\!\left[\,|W_{ij}|\ge \tau_k\,\right], \qquad (4)\]

    where \(\tau_k\) is the \(k\)-th largest magnitude. This is a first-order approximation to the loss change from deleting a weight. Expanding around the trained point, the second-order model of LeCun's optimal brain damage is

    \[\delta L_i\approx \tfrac12\,W_i^2\,H_{ii}, \qquad (5)\]

    so magnitude is exact only when the diagonal Hessian is constant across weights. It ignores the fact that a weight can be large and still redundant because another unit already computes the same feature.

    Movement pruning replaces the static score with the first-order term accumulated along the training trajectory:

    \[s_{ij}=\sum_{t}\left|W^{(t)}_{ij}\,g^{(t)}_{ij}\right|, \qquad (6)\]

    which is the total first-order loss change attributable to that weight, and it is what the demo accumulates in the last 100 steps before masking. On the demo task it flips 104 of 512 first-layer decisions relative to a pure magnitude rule at the same sparsity, and scores 0.967 instead of 1.000: a different mask is not automatically a better one.

    Global against per-layer budgets #

    A per-layer rule spends the sparsity evenly, \(k_l=\lfloor(1-s)|W_l|\rfloor\), while a global rule spends it where the large weights are:

    \[\sum_l k_l=(1-s)\sum_l|W_l|. \qquad (7)\]

    Both target the same total, but the global threshold is free to empty a layer whose weights happen to be small. In the demo it zeroes 82% of W1 and 70% of W2, and still scores 0.988; the layer-wise rule lands exactly on 80/80 and scores 1.000. Neither is universally right: global is better when layers have very different scales, per-layer when every layer is load-bearing.

    Structured pruning #

    Structured pruning removes whole hidden units, which is the only kind that makes the matrix physically smaller. The score for unit \(j\) multiplies its incoming and outgoing norms:

    \[s_j=\lVert W_{1,:,j}\rVert_2\;\lVert W_{2,j,:}\rVert_2, \qquad (8)\]

    and the same indicator zeroes column \(j\) of \(W_1\) and row \(j\) of \(W_2\). In the demo six of 32 units survive at 80% sparsity, accuracy stays at 1.000, and the stored count is 149 — the smallest of the mask-based variants, because the surviving rows and columns are genuinely gone rather than multiplied by zero.

    Schedules: gradual, iterative, rewinding #

    A gradual schedule raises the target sparsity during one training run:

    \[s_t=s\left(1-\left(1-\tfrac{t}{T}\right)^3\right), \qquad (9)\]

    so the model is dense when it is still learning the task and sparse only once the easy structure has been found. Iterative pruning does the same in discrete rounds and retrains between them:

    \[s_r=1-(1-s)^{r/R}, \qquad (10)\]

    with \(R\) rounds. The lottery-ticket variant adds one extra step: before each round it rewinds the weights to their initial values,

    \[W^{(r)}\leftarrow W^{(0)}\odot M^{(r-1)}, \qquad (11)\]

    so the mask and the weights are selected together instead of the mask being fitted to whatever weights the previous round happened to reach. On the demo, at 98% sparsity, one prune gives 0.333, iterative pruning 0.662, rewinding 0.967, and the gradual schedule 0.996.

    Low-rank pruning #

    The last variant does not mask at all. It replaces each matrix by its truncated singular value decomposition,

    \[W\approx U_r\,\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1:r})\,V_r^{\top}, \qquad (12)\]

    which stores \(r(d+k)\) numbers instead of \(dk\). The demo allocates rank greedily across both matrices, always buying the singular value with the largest energy per stored number:

    \[\text{gain}_i=\frac{\sigma_i^2}{d+k}, \qquad (13)\]

    until the entry budget or \(1-\text{tolerance}\) of the total energy is reached. At 50% it keeps ranks 4 and 3 and scores 1.000; at 80% the budget buys rank 1 for W1 and rank 2 for W2, and accuracy falls to 0.667. A rank-one output layer cannot separate three classes, and no amount of clever allocation fixes that: the constraint is the rank, not the criterion.

    Code walkthrough #

    The mask enters in exactly two places, and both are one expression. Forward:

    def _forward(parameters: dict, masks: dict, x: np.ndarray):
    pre = x @ (parameters["W1"] * masks["W1"]) + parameters["b1"]
    hidden = np.tanh(pre)
    logits = hidden @ (parameters["W2"] * masks["W2"]) + parameters["b2"]
    return logits, {"x": x, "pre": pre, "hidden": hidden}

    Backward multiplies each gradient by the same mask, including the hidden-layer gradient that flows through the masked second matrix:

        dhidden = (dlogits @ (parameters["W2"] * masks["W2"]).T) * (
        1.0 - cache["hidden"] ** 2)
    grads = {
        "W1": x.T @ dhidden * masks["W1"],
        "b1": dhidden.sum(axis=0),
        "W2": cache["hidden"].T @ dlogits * masks["W2"],
        "b2": dlogits.sum(axis=0),
    }

    Choosing the survivors is a stable top-\(k\) over the flattened score, so ties do not depend on the platform's sort order:

    def _keep_top(scores: np.ndarray, sparsity: float, shape) -> np.ndarray:
    flat = np.asarray(scores, dtype=float).reshape(-1)
    keep = max(1, int(round((1.0 - sparsity) * flat.size)))
    keep = min(keep, flat.size)
    order = np.argsort(-flat, kind="stable")[:keep]
    mask = np.zeros(flat.size)
    mask[order] = 1.0
    return mask.reshape(shape)

    The global variant concatenates both layers before taking the top-\(k\), then slices the result back apart; every other mask variant applies the same helper per layer:

            if self.variant == "unstructured":
            flat = np.concatenate([scores[name].reshape(-1)
                                   for name in LEARNED])
            keep = _keep_top(flat, target, flat.shape)
            offset = 0
            for name in LEARNED:
                size = scores[name].size
                mask = keep[offset:offset + size].reshape(scores[name].shape)
                self.masks[name] = self.masks[name] * mask
                offset += size

    Movement accumulates the product of weight and gradient at every step, which is the discrete form of (6), before the same _prune_masks call consumes it:

        def _accumulate(self, x, y, steps: int, lr: float) -> None:
        for _ in range(steps):
            _, grads, _ = _loss_and_grads(self.parameters, self.masks, x, y)
            for name in LEARNED:
                self.scores[name] += np.abs(
                    self.parameters[name] * grads[name])
            self.history.append(self._step(x, y, lr))

    The gradual schedule is (9) evaluated once per step, with a prune every interval steps and a final prune at the end:

        def _fit_gradual(self, x, y, steps: int, lr: float,
                     interval: int) -> None:
        for step in range(steps):
            fraction = (step + 1) / steps
            target = self.sparsity * (1.0 - (1.0 - fraction) ** 3)
            if step % interval == 0 or step == steps - 1:
                self._prune_masks(target)
            self.history.append(self._step(x, y, lr))

    Iterative pruning is the same loop over rounds, and the rewind flag is the only difference between magnitude and lottery_ticket:

        def _fit_iterative(self, x, y, steps: int, lr: float,
                       rewind: bool) -> None:
        round_steps = max(1, steps // self.rounds)
        for round_index in range(self.rounds):
            if rewind:
                for name in LEARNED:
                    self.parameters[name] = self.init[name] * self.masks[name]
                for key in ("b1", "b2"):
                    self.parameters[key] = self.init[key].copy()
            self._train(x, y, round_steps, lr)

    Structured pruning scores units rather than entries, then broadcasts one indicator into a column mask and a row mask:

        def _prune_structured(self) -> None:
        norm_in = np.linalg.norm(self.parameters["W1"], axis=0)
        norm_out = np.linalg.norm(self.parameters["W2"], axis=1)
        score = norm_in * norm_out
        keep = max(1, int(round((1.0 - self.sparsity) * self.hidden)))
        order = np.argsort(-score, kind="stable")[:keep]
        keep_mask = np.zeros(self.hidden)
        keep_mask[order] = 1.0
        self.masks["W1"] = keep_mask[None, :] * np.ones_like(self.masks["W1"])
        self.masks["W2"] = keep_mask[:, None] * np.ones_like(self.masks["W2"])

    The low-rank variant is the only one that changes the arrays rather than the mask, and its loop is (13) written as a greedy auction over ranks:

            while recovered < (1.0 - self.tolerance) * total:
            best = None
            for name in LEARNED:
                singular = factor[name][1]
                if ranks[name] >= singular.size:
                    continue
                cost = (self.parameters[name].shape[0]
                        + self.parameters[name].shape[1])
                if used + cost > budget:
                    continue
                gain = float(singular[ranks[name]] ** 2) / cost
                if best is None or gain > best[0]:
                    best = (gain, name, cost)
            if best is None:
                break
            _, name, cost = best
            recovered += float(factor[name][1][ranks[name]] ** 2)
            ranks[name] += 1
            used += cost

    Complexity #

    For a weight matrix of shape \(d\times k\) and a batch of \(B\) examples:

    • Masked forward and backward: still \(O(Bdk)\); multiplying by a mask does not skip work. A sparse mask saves memory, not time, unless the storage and kernel are also sparse.
    • Choosing a mask: \(O(dk\log(dk))\) for the sort, done once per prune event. Gradual pruning repeats it every interval steps, which is why the interval is a real cost knob.
    • Structured pruning: reduces the hidden width from \(h\) to \(h'\), so the matmuls shrink to \(O(Bdh')+O(Bh'c)\) — the only variant here that is faster at inference on ordinary hardware.
    • SVD pruning: one \(O(\min(d,k)\,dk)\) factorisation per matrix, then \(O(r(d+k))\) storage and \(O(Br(d+k))\) per forward pass.
    • The demo: eight variants over 500 steps on 240 examples, plus four schedules and a finite-difference-free training loop. The whole run is under a second, and the assertions are on stored counts and held-out accuracy rather than on gradients, because pruning never changes the gradients, only gates them.

    Failure modes #

    A single prune at 98% destroys the model. The demo reports 0.333 for one_shot against 1.000 for the dense model. The mask was chosen from weights that had never operated under sparsity, and the next two variants show what a schedule buys: 0.662 for four rounds of prune-and-retrain, 0.967 for rewinding.

    Iterative pruning is not the same as a good mask. magnitude reaches 0.662 at 98%, better than one shot and far worse than rewinding or gradual. Four rounds of prune-and-retrain help, but the later rounds are fitting weights that already carry the damage of the early rounds.

    The lottery ticket's win is over reinitialisation, not over magnitude. At 80% every method scores 1.000; at 98% rewinding reaches 0.967. The control in the original paper is a random reinitialisation at the same mask, which the demo does not include, so the honest reading is that rewinding helps this task, not that it is universally the best criterion.

    Global thresholds can empty a layer. The demo's global rule gives W2 70% sparsity while W1 gets 82%; on a network with one narrow layer, the same rule can delete all of it. The per-layer rule exists for that reason.

    Structured pruning can delete a class. Unit scores are aggregated over all outputs, so a unit that only serves one class can look unimportant. The demo is lucky — six units carry three classes — but the failure is the reason structured pruning is usually followed by fine-tuning.

    Sparsity is not speed. A mask of 80% zeros still runs a dense matmul at full cost. Only structured and svd_prune reduce compute, and only the structured one reduces it on hardware that cannot exploit sparsity.

    The mask is applied to gradients, not to the optimiser state. If the optimiser keeps momentum for a pruned weight, restoring the mask later resumes with stale momentum. This implementation is plain SGD and sidesteps the issue; Adam-based pruning stacks have to zero the state explicitly.

    Pruning before training is a different algorithm. one_shot here prunes after training. Pruning at initialisation and then training the sparse mask is the other one-shot recipe, and it behaves differently because the surviving weights never see the dense solution.

    Low rank is a hard constraint, not a budget. At 80% sparsity svd_prune keeps rank 1 in W1 and rank 2 in W2 and scores 0.667, because three classes need a rank-two output. Allocating the same number of stored values as a mask does not buy the same function class.

    The reported sparsity is over prunable weights only. Biases are never pruned here, so total sparsity is lower than the per-matrix number. The demo prints both n_stored and n_dense for this reason.

    Applications #

    Magnitude and movement pruning are the default compression step for large language models, usually combined with quantisation: prune to 50–60%, then quantise what remains. Movement pruning's accumulated score is the basis of several production recipes because it can be computed during fine-tuning without a separate pass. Structured pruning is what ships to phones and accelerators, because removing a channel is the only kind of sparsity that a dense kernel can exploit.

    The lottery-ticket line of work changed how people think about over-parameterised training: the mask found on a dense run is often trainable from the original initialisation, which suggests the dense network's role is partly to find a good sparse subnetwork. Low-rank pruning is the bridge to the previous atom: truncated SVD is the same decomposition that LoRA learns, applied to a frozen weight instead of an update. In practice the strongest compression pipelines stack all three — prune for zeros, factorise for rank, quantise for bits — and distil the result into a smaller dense model.
