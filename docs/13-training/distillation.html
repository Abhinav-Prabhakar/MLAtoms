    13 · Training/tier 1/8 variants/source

    Knowledge distillation — eight ways to move knowledge between models

    Intuition #

    A trained teacher's logits are richer than its labels. The label says which class is right; the logits also say which wrong classes the teacher considers plausible, and by how much. Distillation trains a smaller student to reproduce that whole distribution rather than the one-hot target, which transfers information the labels never contained.

    The eight variants differ in what gets transferred. response matches softened logits — the original Hinton recipe. feature matches hidden states through a learned regressor, as in FitNets. relation matches the pairwise structure of a batch, so the student learns how examples relate rather than what each one scores. attention_transfer matches the token attention maps. dkd splits the logit loss into a target-class term and a non-target term, and weights them separately.

    The remaining three are procedures rather than new losses. self_distill uses a slow exponential moving average of the student as its own teacher. born_again trains a sequence of students where generation \(g\) teaches \(g+1\) with no external teacher. online_distill trains two networks at once, each distilling from the other, so no pretrained teacher is needed at all.

    The demo trains a teacher on 140 examples, then gives a student only eight labels. The hard-label student reaches 0.550 on held-out data; the distilled student reaches 0.617, because it also sees the 132 unlabelled inputs through the teacher's soft targets.

    Variants #

    variant transferred extra parameters needs labels
    response softened logits none no
    feature hidden states through a regressor \(H_tH_s\) no
    relation pairwise distances in a batch none no
    self_distill logits of an EMA of itself none no
    born_again logits of the previous generation none no
    online_distill logits of a jointly trained peer a second network no
    attention_transfer token attention maps none no
    dkd target and non-target logit terms none yes

    Every variant accepts an optional label vector. When labels are present the loss is alpha cross-entropy plus the transfer term; with alpha = 1 the model is trained normally, and with alpha = 0 it is trained only by the teacher.

    Derivation #

    The response loss #

    With temperature \(T\), both networks produce softened distributions

    \[p_s=\mathrm{softmax}(z_s/T),\qquad p_t=\mathrm{softmax}(z_t/T), \qquad (1)\]

    and the student minimises

    \[L=\alpha\,\mathrm{CE}(y,p_s)+(1-\alpha)\,T^2\, \mathrm{KL}\bigl(p_t\,\|\,p_s\bigr). \qquad (2)\]

    The \(T^2\) factor exists because the KL gradient carries a \(1/T\): differentiating (1) and the KL gives

    \[\frac{\partial L_{\text{soft}}}{\partial z_s} =\frac{T}{B}\,(p_s-p_t), \qquad (3)\]

    so without the \(T^2\) the soft term would shrink by \(1/T\) as the temperature rises and \(\alpha\) would no longer mean what it says. With it, changing \(T\) changes the shape of the target without changing the overall gradient scale.

    Large \(T\) flattens both distributions, which is the point: at \(T=1\) the demo's trained teacher has a top-class gap of 0.978, and at \(T=4\) the gap is 0.565 with entropy rising from 0.052 to 0.736. The non-target probabilities become large enough to carry a usable signal.

    Feature distillation #

    FitNets matches an intermediate layer, with a regressor \(R\) that maps the teacher's width to the student's:

    \[L_f=\frac{1}{BTH}\bigl\|H_s-H_tR\bigr\|_F^2,\qquad \frac{\partial L_f}{\partial R} =-\frac{2}{BTH}H_t^{\top}\bigl(H_s-H_tR\bigr). \qquad (4)\]

    \(R\) is trained jointly with the student, so the student is free to choose any linear re-encoding of the teacher's features. In the demo \(H_t\) has width 8, \(H_s\) has width 6, and the regressor is \(8\times6\); a student that reproduces \(H_tR\) scores exactly zero. This is the "hint" of FitNets, and the regressor is why the student does not need the same architecture as the teacher.

    Relation distillation #

    RKD compares the geometry of a batch rather than individual outputs. With \(d_{ij}=\lVert z_i-z_j\rVert\) and \(\tilde d=d/\mathrm{mean}(d)\),

    \[L_r=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i

    Normalising by the batch mean makes the loss invariant to a global rescale of the logits, and summing over unordered pairs makes it invariant to the order of the batch. The demo checks both: tripling both sets of logits and reversing the batch leave the loss at 0.2007.

    The gradient needs the normalisation differentiated too. With \(r=\tilde d^s-\tilde d^t\) and \(c=\mathrm{mean}(r\odot\tilde d^s)\),

    \[\frac{\partial L_r}{\partial z_i} =\sum_{j\neq i}w_{ij}\,(z_i-z_j),\qquad w_{ij}=\frac{2\,(r_{ij}-c)}{N\,\mu_s\,d_{ij}}, \qquad (6)\]

    where \(\mu_s=\mathrm{mean}(d^s)\). The matrix of weights is symmetric, so the implementation builds it once and evaluates the sum as a row-sum times \(z\) minus the matrix product.

    Attention transfer #

    For hidden states \(H\in\mathbb R^{B\times T\times H}\), the attention map is

    \[A=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{HH^{\top}}{\sqrt H}\right),\qquad \hat A=\frac{A}{\lVert A\rVert_F}, \qquad (7)\]

    with the softmax along the key axis, so every row sums to one. The loss is \(\lVert\hat A_s-\hat A_t\rVert^2\) averaged over the batch. The row-stochastic property is the useful part: the student learns where the teacher looks, not what it represents. The backward pass is a softmax Jacobian followed by the normalisation, and then the bilinear form contributes \((dS+dS^{\top})H/\sqrt H\) because \(S\) is symmetric in its two token axes.

    Decoupled knowledge distillation #

    DKD divides the logit loss into two terms that measure different errors. Let \(p_t\) be the target-class probability and let \(q\) be the distribution on the remaining classes after renormalising:

    \[q_i=\frac{p_i}{1-p_t}\quad (i\neq t),\qquad L_{\text{TCKD}}=T^2\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl([p_t^{t},1-p_t^{t}]\, \|\,[p_t^{s},1-p_t^{s}]\bigr), \qquad (8)\]
    \[L_{\text{NCKD}}=T^2\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl(q_t\,\|\,q_s\bigr). \qquad (9)\]

    TCKD is a single binary decision: is the target class likely or not. NCKD is the shape of the remaining mass, which is where the inter-class similarities live. Their gradients w.r.t. the student logits are

    \[\frac{\partial L_{\text{TCKD}}}{\partial z_j} =T\,c\,p_j\,(\delta_{jt}-p_t),\qquad c=-\frac{p_t^{t}}{p_t^{s}}+\frac{1-p_t^{t}}{1-p_t^{s}}, \qquad (10)\]
    \[\frac{\partial L_{\text{NCKD}}}{\partial z_j} =T\,(q_{sj}-q_{tj})\ \ (j\neq t),\qquad 0\ \ (j=t). \qquad (11)\]

    The demo separates the cases: a student that matches the teacher exactly scores zero on both; a student that gets the target right but flattens the non-target mass scores 0.167 TCKD and 0.031 NCKD; a student that gets the target wrong scores 4.61 and 0.76. The paper's finding is that NCKD carries most of the benefit, which is why the weights are exposed separately.

    Symmetric KL for online distillation #

    Deep mutual learning has no pretrained teacher: two networks each minimise their own cross-entropy plus the KL of the other to themselves. The combined soft loss is the symmetric divergence

    \[S=\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)+\mathrm{KL}(p\|q), \qquad (12)\]

    and its gradient is not just the difference of the two distributions, because each KL depends on both arguments:

    \[\frac{\partial S}{\partial z_s} =\frac1T\Bigl[(p-q)+p\odot\bigl(\log(p/q)-S_p\bigr)\Bigr], \qquad S_p=\mathrm{KL}(p\|q). \qquad (13)\]

    Dropping the log-ratio term is a plausible-looking bug: the loss still falls and the networks still train, but the finite-difference check reports an error around \(2\times10^{-2}\). The demo's version passes at \(1.9\times10^{-10}\).

    Self-distillation and born-again #

    A mean teacher keeps a slow copy of the student and distils from it:

    \[\bar\theta\leftarrow\beta\bar\theta+(1-\beta)\theta,\qquad \beta=0.99. \qquad (14)\]

    The teacher never receives gradients, so the target drifts slowly and acts as a temporal ensemble. Born-again instead trains generations sequentially:

    \[\theta_{g+1}=\arg\min_\theta\ \alpha\,\mathrm{CE}(y;\theta) +(1-\alpha)T^2\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl(p_{\theta_g}\,\|\,p_\theta\bigr), \qquad (15)\]

    with generation 0 trained on labels alone. The demo's four generations on noisy labels score 0.583, 0.633, 0.650 and 0.650, against 0.583 for one plain run on the same data.

    Code walkthrough #

    Cross-entropy returns its own gradient, which every variant reuses as the hard term:

    def _cross_entropy(logits: np.ndarray, labels: np.ndarray):
    probs = _softmax(logits)
    batch = logits.shape[0]
    loss = -np.mean(np.log(probs[np.arange(batch), labels] + 1e-12))
    grad = probs.copy()
    grad[np.arange(batch), labels] -= 1.0
    return loss, grad / batch

    The KL helper returns the loss and the \(T(p_s-p_t)/B\) gradient together. Keeping them in one place is what makes the \(T^2\) bookkeeping consistent:

        log_probs = _log_softmax(student_logits / temperature)
    loss = temperature ** 2 * float(
        np.mean(np.sum(teacher_probs * (np.log(teacher_probs + 1e-12)
                                        - log_probs), axis=-1)))
    probs = np.exp(log_probs)
    grad = temperature * (probs - teacher_probs) / student_logits.shape[0]
    return loss, grad

    Both networks are the same three lines, so the student and teacher differ only in their parameters. The mean over tokens is the only place the two token positions interact:

        def _mlp(self, prefix: str, x: np.ndarray):
        p = self.parameters
        pre = np.einsum("bti,ih->bth", x, p[f"{prefix}.W1"]) + p[f"{prefix}.b1"]
        hidden = np.tanh(pre)
        logits = hidden.mean(axis=1) @ p[f"{prefix}.W2"] + p[f"{prefix}.b2"]
        return hidden, logits

    The response branch is then one line, and the same code serves the EMA teacher and the born-again teacher because both live in self.parameters["teacher.*"]:

    Feature distillation adds a direct gradient to the hidden state, and the regressor gradient is the outer product of the teacher's features and the residual. Note the two different index patterns in the same block:

    Relation distillation folds the normalisation into a symmetric weight matrix. The row-sum-times-\(z\) form is the vectorised version of \(\sum_j w_{ij}(z_i-z_j)\):

            residual = normalised_s - normalised_t
        count = distance_s.size
        share = float(np.mean(residual * normalised_s))
        weight = 2.0 * (residual - share) / (count * mean_s)
        weight = weight / np.maximum(distance_s, 1e-12)
        spread = np.zeros((z_s.shape[0], z_s.shape[0]))
        spread[upper] = weight
        spread = spread + spread.T
        return spread.sum(axis=1)[:, None] * z_s - spread @ z_s

    Attention transfer is three backward passes stacked: normalisation, softmax, and the bilinear score. The last line symmetrises the score gradient because \(S_{ij}\) and \(S_{ji}\) both touch \(h_i\):

            inner = np.sum(scaled_s * grad_scaled, axis=(1, 2), keepdims=True)
        grad_attention = (grad_scaled - scaled_s * inner) / norm_s
        inner = np.sum(attention_s * grad_attention, axis=-1, keepdims=True)
        grad_scores = attention_s * (grad_attention - inner)
        scale = 1.0 / np.sqrt(h_s.shape[-1])
        return (grad_scores + grad_scores.transpose(0, 2, 1)) @ h_s * scale

    The DKD target term is the binary KL differentiated through the softmax. The coefficient is large exactly when the student is confident and wrong:

            coefficient = (-target_t / np.maximum(target_s, 1e-12)

    The mean-teacher update is the whole of self_distill; there is no gradient path into the teacher at all:

            teacher = self.parameters[f"teacher.{name}"]

    Complexity #

    Let \(B\) be the batch, \(T\) the tokens, \(d\) the input width, \(H_s,H_t\) the hidden widths, \(C\) the classes and \(N=B(B-1)/2\).

    • Two MLP passes: \(O(BTdH+BHC)\) forward and the same backward, twice for online_distill and once otherwise.
    • Response, self, born-again, online: \(O(BC)\) for the KL and its gradient.
    • Feature: \(O(BTH_tH_s)\) for the regressor and its gradient; the largest per-example term when \(H\) is wide.
    • Relation: \(O(B^2C)\) for the pairwise distances and \(O(B^2)\) for the weight matrix, independent of the widths. It is the only variant that scales quadratically in the batch.
    • Attention transfer: \(O(BT^2H)\) for the scores and their backward.
    • DKD: \(O(BC)\), plus the two masked renormalisations.
    • The demo: batches of 60 to 140, widths up to 16, eight variants. Each finite-difference loop evaluates one forward pass per parameter entry, and the training blocks run 600 steps over 140 examples. Everything is milliseconds except the fitting loops.

    Failure modes #

    A weak teacher teaches weakness. Every loss here is a divergence from the teacher, so a teacher with 0.53 held-out accuracy caps what the student can learn from soft targets. The demo's student beats its teacher only because the hard labels anchor it; a student trained with alpha = 0 would inherit the teacher's errors directly.

    Temperature interacts with the loss weight. The \(T^2\) factor keeps the gradient scale fixed, but it also scales the reported loss. Tuning \(T\) and \(\alpha\) independently on the loss value rather than held-out accuracy is a common way to pick a bad pair.

    The feature regressor can hide a bad student. \(R\) is trained at the same time as the student, so it will happily absorb a mismatch the student should have fixed — the loss goes to zero while the student's features stay nothing like the teacher's. FitNets freezes the hint layer or fixes \(R\) for this reason.

    Relation losses are invariant to what matters most. Because the distances are normalised by their batch mean, the loss cannot transfer absolute logit scale or a rotation of the feature space. That is desirable for robustness and bad if the calibration itself is the knowledge.

    Attention maps with few tokens carry almost nothing. With two tokens each row of \(A\) has one free parameter, so the transfer is a single number per example. Attention transfer was designed for convolutional maps with hundreds of spatial positions; here it demonstrates the mechanism, not its power.

    Self-distillation with a stale teacher drifts. The EMA teacher lags by \(1/(1-\beta)\) steps. If the student diverges, the teacher keeps pulling it back toward the divergence for a while. A very large \(\beta\) is stable but slow; a small one turns the teacher into a noisy copy of the student.

    Born-again is not a free win. Each generation inherits its teacher's bias, and with a small model on a small dataset the extra rounds can be flat or slightly worse. The demo's first generation already sees every label; the gain comes from soft targets averaging label noise, not from the recursion itself.

    Online distillation needs the symmetric gradient. Each network's loss depends on both logits, so differentiating only the plain difference of probabilities is wrong by the log-ratio term in (13). The loss still decreases, which is why only a gradient check catches it.

    DKD needs the target index. TCKD and NCKD are defined relative to the ground-truth class, so dkd cannot run without labels; the constructor allows y=None and the loss raises instead.

    Numerical zeros in KL. A teacher that assigns probability exactly zero to a class makes \(\log p_t\) infinite. Every log here adds \(10^{-12}\), which changes the gradient slightly but keeps a saturated teacher from producing NaNs.

    Teacher and student must see the same preprocessing. Soft targets are computed from the teacher's view of the input. Augmenting the student's input differently from the teacher's breaks the correspondence silently.

    Applications #

    Distillation is the standard way to compress a large model into a deployable one: train the teacher once, then fit a smaller student to its outputs. It is also the backbone of semi-supervised learning — Noisy Student, self-training and mean-teacher methods all generate targets for unlabelled data from a model and train on them. FitNets and attention transfer are used when intermediate supervision is cheaper than matching outputs, for example when the student has a different head.

    DKD and its relatives are now standard in detection and segmentation, where the background class dominates and the target/non-target split matters. Deep mutual learning trains an ensemble without a pretrained teacher, and born-again networks are a cheap way to squeeze another point from a fixed architecture. The same recipes scale to language models, where a small model is distilled from a large one's token distributions instead of its labels.
