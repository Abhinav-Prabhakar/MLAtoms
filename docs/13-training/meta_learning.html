    Meta-learning — seven ways to learn from a handful of examples

    Intuition #

    A classifier that has never seen a task cannot learn it from five examples. A meta-learner has seen hundreds of tasks drawn from the same family, so five examples are enough to identify which member of the family it is facing. The difference is the distribution, not the architecture.

    The family here is concentric rings: three classes, each a ring of random radius, with the radii resampled for every task. A ring's mean is the origin, so nearest-mean on the raw inputs is nearly useless — it scores 0.358, and a random encoder scores 0.454. A representation that encodes the radius can tell the rings apart, and learning that representation across tasks is the meta-learning problem.

    Seven algorithms attack it. maml adapts a copy of the parameters on the support set and differentiates through the adaptation to score the query set. first_order_maml drops the second-order term. reptile moves the initialisation toward the adapted parameters without ever touching the query set. meta_sgd learns a per-parameter inner learning rate. The three metric methods skip adaptation entirely: prototypical classifies by distance to the class means in embedding space, matching by a soft nearest-neighbour over the support set, and relation by a learned similarity module.

    After 400 meta-iterations the prototype head leads at 0.850, matching reaches 0.838, the relation module 0.848, MAML 0.795, first-order MAML 0.788, meta-SGD 0.755 and Reptile 0.684. The ordering is not a verdict on the algorithms: it says that this task family is metric, so a fixed nearest-prototype rule over a good embedding is exactly the right inductive bias, and adaptation on five points adds variance without adding information.

    Variants #

    variant adapts at test time what moves score
    maml yes, full second order initialisation 0.795
    first_order_maml yes, first order initialisation 0.788
    reptile yes, first order initialisation 0.684
    meta_sgd yes, learned steps initialisation and \(\alpha\) 0.755
    prototypical no encoder 0.850
    matching no encoder 0.838
    relation no encoder and relation module 0.848

    The five learned variants share one encoder — a 2→64→16 ReLU network — and the three MAML-family variants add a linear head on top. The metric variants use the encoder alone. Every variant sees the same 400 meta-iterations of four tasks, each with three classes, five support points and fifteen query points.

    Derivation #

    The episodic objective #

    A task \(\mathcal T=(\mathcal D_{\mathrm{sup}},\mathcal D_{\mathrm{qry}})\) is drawn from a family \(p(\mathcal T)\). Meta-training minimises the query loss after adapting on the support set:

    \[\min_\theta\ \mathbb E_{\mathcal T\sim p(\mathcal T)} \left[L_{\mathrm{qry}}\big(\theta'(\theta,\mathcal D_{\mathrm{sup}})\big) \right], \qquad (1)\]

    and meta-testing repeats the same procedure on a task the learner has never seen. The number that matters is the expected query accuracy after adaptation, which is what evaluate measures over 200 fresh tasks.

    MAML #

    The inner loop takes one or more gradient steps on the support loss:

    \[\theta'=\theta-\alpha\nabla_\theta L_{\mathrm{sup}}(\theta). \qquad (2)\]

    Differentiating (1) through (2) gives the meta-gradient

    \[\frac{\partial L_{\mathrm{qry}}(\theta')}{\partial\theta} =\left(I-\alpha\nabla^2_\theta L_{\mathrm{sup}}(\theta)\right) \nabla_{\theta'}L_{\mathrm{qry}}(\theta'), \qquad (3)\]

    so the outer update needs a Hessian-vector product at every inner step. The code computes it by finite difference,

    \[Hv\approx\frac{\nabla L_{\mathrm{sup}}(\theta+\epsilon v) -\nabla L_{\mathrm{sup}}(\theta)}{\epsilon}, \qquad (4)\]

    which costs two support gradients and is exact to \(O(\epsilon)\) without a second-order autodiff. The reverse pass then multiplies the factors in (3):

    \[v\leftarrow v-\alpha\,H_t\,v,\qquad t=T-1,\dots,0 . \qquad (5)\]

    First-order MAML drops the Hessian and uses \(\nabla_{\theta'}L_{\mathrm{qry}}\) directly. On this task the difference is small — 0.795 against 0.788 — because the inner loop takes three small steps and the curvature term contributes little. On tasks where the inner loop moves far, the gap is larger, and (4) is what closes it.

    Reptile #

    Reptile never evaluates the query loss during meta-training. It adapts on the support set and pulls the initialisation toward the adapted parameters:

    \[\theta\leftarrow\theta+\beta\big(\theta'-\theta\big). \qquad (6)\]

    With one inner step, (6) is \(\beta\alpha\nabla L_{\mathrm{sup}}\), an SGD step on the support loss; with several steps it approximates the gradient of a multi-step objective without the Hessian. The demo uses \(\beta=0.5\) and gets 0.684, the weakest of the seven — the price of ignoring the query set when the support set is only 15 points.

    Meta-SGD #

    Meta-SGD replaces the scalar inner rate with a vector \(\alpha\) of the same shape as \(\theta\):

    \[\theta'=\theta-\alpha\odot\nabla_\theta L_{\mathrm{sup}}(\theta), \qquad (7)\]

    and learns \(\alpha\) alongside the initialisation. The gradient with respect to \(\alpha\) follows from (7) and the chain rule:

    \[\frac{\partial L_{\mathrm{qry}}}{\partial\alpha} =-g_t\odot v_{t+1}, \qquad (8)\]

    where \(g_t\) is the support gradient at step \(t\) and \(v_{t+1}\) is the backpropagated query gradient at the next iterate. The code accumulates (8) while running the same reverse recursion as MAML, and clips \(\alpha\) to \((10^{-3},1)\) to keep the inner loop stable. Meta-SGD reaches 0.755: better than Reptile, worse than a plain MAML with a well-chosen scalar rate, because a second copy of every parameter is a lot to learn from 400 tasks.

    Prototypical networks #

    The encoder maps each point to \(\mathbb R^{16}\). A class prototype is the mean of its support embeddings, and a query is classified by negative squared distance:

    \[p(y=c\mid q)=\mathrm{softmax}_c\left(-\|\phi(q)-\mu_c\|^2\right),\qquad \mu_c=\frac1K\sum_{k}\phi(s_{ck}). \qquad (9)\]

    There is no inner loop: the support set is the classifier. The demo reaches 0.850, the best score, because the task family is metric — the rings are separable in a space where radius is a coordinate, and the mean is the right summary of a ring.

    Matching networks #

    Matching replaces the mean with a soft nearest-neighbour over the support points of each class:

    \[\ell_{qc}=\log\sum_{k=1}^{K} \exp\left(-\frac{\|\phi(q)-\phi(s_{ck})\|^2}{\tau}\right), \qquad (10)\]

    which is a log-sum-exp of negative distances. As \(\tau\to0\) it becomes 1-nearest-neighbour; as \(\tau\to\infty\) it becomes a class mean. The demo uses \(\tau=0.5\) and reaches 0.838, slightly below prototypical, because five points per class make the attention noisy and the mean is the lower-variance summary. The original matching network also conditions on the whole support set with an LSTM; (10) is the attention-only core.

    Relation networks #

    The relation module learns the similarity instead of fixing it:

    \[r(q,c)=f_\psi\big([\phi(q),\mu_c,|\phi(q)-\mu_c|]\big), \qquad (11)\]

    with a two-layer MLP \(f_\psi\) over the concatenation of the query, the prototype and their absolute difference. The score replaces the distance in (9), and the encoder and the relation module are trained end-to-end on the episodic loss. It reaches 0.848, essentially tying prototypical: on a task where Euclidean distance is already the right metric, learning a metric buys nothing, and the extra parameters are pure variance.

    Shots, and what the support set buys #

    The support set is the only supervision at test time, so its size sets the ceiling. Prototypical with the same encoder and 250 meta-iterations scores 0.813 at one shot, 0.818 at three and 0.844 at five. The gain is small because the rings are well separated and one point already pins down a radius. On tasks where classes overlap more, the curve is steeper, and the difference between a good and a bad meta-learner is largest at one shot.

    Cost #

    Let \(T\) be the inner steps and \(G\) the cost of one support gradient. MAML's per-task cost is

    \[C_{\mathrm{MAML}}=(T+1)G+2TG=(3T+1)G, \qquad (12)\]

    while prototypical costs one forward pass over support and query and no adaptation at all. At \(T=3\) the second-order pass is about ten support gradients per task; the demo still runs in seconds because the network is tiny, but on a real backbone that factor decides whether the method is affordable. This is why first-order MAML and Reptile exist: they trade a small accuracy gap for a large constant.

    Code walkthrough #

    The task generator is the whole inductive bias: rings of random radius, each one class, so the mean of every class is the origin.

        angles = rng.uniform(0.0, 2.0 * np.pi, (n_way, n_shot + n_query))
    points = radii[:, None, None] * np.stack(
        [np.cos(angles), np.sin(angles)], axis=-1)
    points = points + rng.normal(0.0, 0.10, points.shape)
    return points[:, :n_shot], points[:, n_shot:]

    The encoder is two ReLU layers, and the head is linear; the same forward pass serves the MAML family and, through _metric_logits, the metric family.

    def _encode(params: dict, x: np.ndarray):
    z1 = x @ params["W1"] + params["b1"]
    a1 = np.maximum(z1, 0.0)
    z2 = a1 @ params["W2"] + params["b2"]
    a2 = np.maximum(z2, 0.0)
    return a2, (x, z1, a1, z2)

    The inner loop saves every iterate, because the reverse pass of (5) needs the Hessian at each one:

        def _adapt(self, params: dict, sup: np.ndarray, steps: int, **kwargs):
        trail = [params]
        gradients = []
        for _ in range(steps):
            grads, _ = self._support_grads(trail[-1], sup)
            gradients.append(grads)
            scaling = kwargs.get("alpha")
            if scaling is None:
                step = {key: self.inner_lr * grads[key] for key in grads}
            else:
                step = {key: scaling[key] * grads[key] for key in grads}
            trail.append({key: trail[-1][key] - step[key] for key in trail[-1]})
        return trail[-1], trail, gradients

    The Hessian-vector product of (4) is two support gradients, no second-order machinery:

        def _hessian_vector(self, params: dict, sup: np.ndarray,
                        vector: dict) -> dict:
        base, _ = self._support_grads(params, sup)
        eps = 1e-4
        shifted = {key: params[key] + eps * vector[key] for key in params}
        moved, _ = self._support_grads(shifted, sup)
        return {key: (moved[key] - base[key]) / eps for key in base}

    and the reverse pass applies (5) from the last inner step to the first:

                vector = query
            for index in range(self.inner_steps - 1, -1, -1):
                product = self._hessian_vector(trail[index], sup, vector)
                vector = {key: vector[key] - self.inner_lr * product[key]
                          for key in vector}
            return vector, None

    Reptile is the same inner loop with a different outer signal — the difference between the adapted and original parameters, with no query loss in sight:

            if self.variant == "reptile":
            adapted, _, _ = self._adapt(self.params, sup, self.inner_steps)
            return ({key: adapted[key] - self.params[key]
                     for key in self.params}, None)

    Meta-SGD accumulates the learning-rate gradient (8) during the same reverse recursion, then steps \(\alpha\) with its own optimiser:

                for index in range(self.inner_steps - 1, -1, -1):
                d_alpha = {key: d_alpha[key] - gradients[index][key] * vector[key]
                           for key in d_alpha}
                product = self._hessian_vector(trail[index], sup, vector)
                vector = {key: vector[key] - self.alpha[key] * product[key]
                          for key in vector}
            return vector, d_alpha

    The prototypical head is (9) in three lines, and its gradient is the chain rule through the mean:

        if variant == "prototypical":
        centre = s.mean(axis=1)
        logits = -((q[:, :, None, :] - centre[None, None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(-1)
        return logits, (q, s, centre, s_cache, q_cache)

    Matching is the same idea with a log-sum-exp over the support points:

        if variant == "matching":
        pair = q[:, :, None, None, :] - s[None, None, :, :, :]
        similarity = -(pair ** 2).sum(-1) / TAU
        shift = similarity.max(axis=-1, keepdims=True)
        weight = np.exp(similarity - shift)
        total = weight.sum(axis=-1, keepdims=True)
        logits = (shift + np.log(total))[..., 0]
        return logits, (q, s, pair, weight / total, s_cache, q_cache)

    The relation module concatenates the query, the prototype and their gap, then scores the triple with a two-layer MLP; its gradients come from the same cross-entropy, backpropagated through the encoder.

    Complexity #

    With \(N\) meta-iterations, \(B\) tasks per iteration, \(K\) shots, \(Q\) queries, \(T\) inner steps and an encoder of \(P\) parameters:

    • Prototypical and matching: one encoder pass over \((K+Q)\,N_{\mathrm{way}}\) points per task, \(O(NB(K+Q)P)\) for the whole run. No adaptation.
    • Relation: the same plus \(N_{\mathrm{way}}^2\) MLP evaluations per query; the module is small, so the encoder still dominates.
    • MAML: \((3T+1)\) support or query gradients per task, from (12). The demo's \(T=3\) means about ten gradient evaluations per task; the finite difference is two of them per inner step.
    • First-order MAML: \(T+1\) gradients per task, roughly a third of the second-order cost.
    • Reptile: \(T\) gradients per task, the cheapest of the adaptive family, because the query set is never evaluated during meta-training.
    • Meta-SGD: MAML's cost plus one extra parameter tensor of size \(P\); the inner loop is elementwise, so the arithmetic is nearly unchanged.

    The demo's 400 iterations × 4 tasks × 7 variants is a few seconds, dominated by the second-order variants. The constant that matters in practice is (12): the same search on a ResNet backbone is where MAML's Hessian becomes the reason people use first-order methods.

    Failure modes #

    The support set can be too small for adaptation. MAML with three inner steps and 15 support points overfits the support loss: the adapted model fits the five points per class and the query accuracy falls. The demo's 0.795 versus prototypical's 0.850 is that effect. Fewer steps, a smaller inner rate or a head-only inner loop are the usual fixes.

    Meta-overfitting. With 400 tasks the encoder can memorise the family rather than learn a transferable representation. The tell is a large gap between two independent evaluations — here the demo's two 200-task runs agree to within 0.02, which is why its table is trustworthy. On a small task family, hold out task parameters, not just samples.

    Second-order is not automatically better. First-order MAML scores 0.788 against 0.795, a difference smaller than the task-to-task noise. The Hessian term matters when the inner loop moves far; with three small steps it is a rounding error at ten times the cost. Measure the gap before paying for it.

    Reptile ignores the query set. Its outer signal is the support-adapted parameters, so it optimises for support-set performance, not query-set performance. When support and query are drawn from the same task that is fine; when the task has a distribution shift between the two splits, it is not.

    Metric methods assume the right metric. Prototypical wins here because squared distance in a learned embedding is the correct inductive bias for rings. On a task where class membership depends on a relation between points rather than their position, a fixed metric fails and the relation module's learned similarity is the only one of the three that can recover.

    The prototype is a mean, and means hide structure. A class with two modes has a prototype between them, in a region with no data. The same failure appears in the raw baseline: every ring's mean is the origin. Clustering the support before averaging, or using attention as in (10), is the usual remedy.

    A learned inner rate can collapse or explode. Meta-SGD's \(\alpha\) is clipped because the unclipped version drives some coordinates to zero and others past one within a few hundred iterations. The clip is part of the algorithm, not a detail.

    Evaluation leaks through the task sampler. If meta-training and meta-test tasks share the same random radii drawn once, the test set is not fresh. The demo resamples the radii for every task; the evaluate loop draws new tasks from the same generator, which is the correct protocol but a subtle one to get right in a real pipeline.

    The number of classes is fixed in the code. All heads have N_WAY=3 outputs, so a four-way task would need a different network. Metric methods generalise to a new \(N_{\mathrm{way}}\) at test time; MAML's linear head does not without resizing. That asymmetry is easy to miss when comparing scores.

    Few-shot accuracy is a noisy statistic. With 15 queries per class and 200 tasks the standard error is around 0.01, so a 0.005 gap between two methods is not a result. Report the interval, not the third decimal.

    Applications #

    Few-shot image classification is the benchmark that drove the field: Omniglot and miniImageNet episodes with five classes and one or five shots. Prototypical and matching networks are still strong baselines there, and MAML-style adaptation is used when the task shift is large enough that a fixed metric fails.

    Meta-learning also appears wherever a model must be personalised from a few examples: user-specific keyboards and speech models, per-patient adaptation in medical imaging, per-robot calibration. In those settings the "task" is a user or a device, the support set is their history, and the meta-learner provides the initialisation that a few gradient steps can specialise.

    Reptile and first-order MAML are the practical choices for large backbones because they avoid the Hessian. Metric methods are popular in retrieval and open-set recognition, where the number of classes is not known in advance and a classifier head cannot be fixed. Relation modules appear in Siamese architectures for verification, where the learned similarity is the product.

    Outside supervised learning, the same episodic objective underlies meta-RL, where the inner loop is a policy update, and gradient-based hyperparameter search, where the inner loop is training and the outer loop scores validation loss — the connection to chapter 13's hyperparameter_search.
