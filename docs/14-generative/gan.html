    14 · Generative models/tier 1/7 variants/source

    Generative adversarial network — seven losses, one minimax game

    A GAN replaces the likelihood with a classifier. The generator is trained to fool a discriminator that is itself trained to tell real from generated points, so the generator's loss is not written down in advance — it is whatever the discriminator learns to punish. That buys sharp samples and costs every guarantee: no bound, no likelihood, and a two-player game that need not converge. This file plays the game on eight Gaussians around a circle. The seven variants are four objectives, one architecture and two ways of feeding a code, all sharing one backward pass, so that "which GAN" becomes a table of gradients rather than a zoo of architectures.

    Intuition #

    Two networks, one adversarial signal. The generator maps a standard normal code to a point; the discriminator maps a point to a logit saying how real it looks. The discriminator descends its classification loss on real and generated batches; the generator ascends it, which by the chain rule means descending the discriminator's verdict on its own output.

    flowchart LR
    Z["z ~ N(0, I)"] --> G["G: 2→64→64→2 tanh"]
    G -->|"generated x"| D["D: 2→64→64→1"]
    X["real x"] --> D
    D --> L["logit"]
    L -->|"gradient on x"| G

    The generator never sees the data. Everything it learns about the distribution arrives as a gradient through the discriminator, which is why the discriminator is best understood as a learned loss function rather than a classifier to be maximised. A good discriminator says "this region is thin" in a way that points the generator's mass toward the data; a perfect one says "fake" everywhere the generator already is, which is a gradient of almost zero.

    At the optimum of the inner problem the discriminator becomes the density ratio \(p_{\mathrm{data}}/(p_{\mathrm{data}}+p_g)\), and the game's value is a Jensen–Shannon divergence. That is the theory. The practice is that the ratio is estimated from a finite batch by a network that is also being trained, and that the objective is satisfied by any generator whose support sits inside the data's support. A generator that produces one mode perfectly is as good as one that produces eight, as far as the loss is concerned: the discriminator cannot tell a missing mode from a mode that was never sampled. This is mode collapse, and it is a property of the objective, not a bug in the optimiser.

    The seven variants differ in three places:

    • the gradient the discriminator hands the generator (the loss table);
    • the shape of the two networks (dcgan is deeper and wider);
    • what the networks are shown (conditional shows the label to both, infogan shows a code only to the generator).

    Everything else — the data, the Adam state, the backward pass, the evaluation — is shared, so the differences in the table below are attributable to the variant and not to a re-implementation.

    Variants #

    variant discriminator minimises generator minimises
    vanilla binary cross-entropy \(\log(1-D(G(z)))\)
    non_saturating binary cross-entropy \(-\log D(G(z))\)
    lsgan \((D(x)-1)^2/2+D(G(z))^2/2\) \((D(G(z))-1)^2\)
    hinge hinge on both sides \(-D(G(z))\)
    dcgan binary cross-entropy \(-\log D(G(z))\)
    conditional binary cross-entropy on \((x,y)\) \(-\log D(G(z,y),y)\)
    infogan binary cross-entropy on \(x\) \(-\log D(G(z,c))+\) MI term

    vanilla — the original minimax game (Goodfellow et al. 2014). The generator minimises \(\log(1-\sigma(D(G(z))))\), whose derivative with respect to the logit is \(-\sigma(D(G(z)))\): it vanishes exactly when the discriminator is confident the sample is fake, which is when the generator most needs it. The demo's signal column is the mean absolute value of that derivative: 0.496 here, against 1.000 for hinge and 0.998 at logit \(-6\) for the non-saturating form below.

    non_saturating — the same game with the generator minimising \(-\log\sigma(D(G(z)))\) instead. The gradient is now \(\sigma(-D(G(z)))\), which is large precisely where the saturating form is flat. It is not the same objective, but it has the same fixed point and trains far better from a bad initialisation, so it became the default and is this class's default too.

    lsgan — least squares (Mao et al. 2017). The discriminator is linear and regresses to 1 on real points and 0 on generated ones; the generator regresses its own output toward 1. The quadratic penalty keeps growing when the discriminator is confident, so the generator's gradient does not vanish. On this data it is the most stable of the four unconditional losses: eight modes from a fresh draw, largest share 0.15, and a discriminator that ends at chance.

    hinge — the geometric/SVM objective (Lim & Ye 2017). The discriminator wants a margin of one on both sides; the generator wants its score pushed up with a constant gradient. The constant is the point and the problem: every sample receives the same push regardless of how far it is from the boundary, and here the generator overshoots, keeping five modes with a 0.30 largest share.

    dcgan — not a loss but an architecture (Radford et al. 2016): three hidden layers of width 128, a 16-dimensional code, and the non-saturating objective. On 2-D points there is nothing to convolve, so what this variant isolates is depth and width. It is the most confident discriminator in the table (0.84 accuracy) and it drops a mode: a strong discriminator plus a small generator is the classic way to lose coverage.

    conditional — both players see a class label: the generator maps \((z,y)\) to a point, the discriminator scores \((x,y)\) (Mirza & Osindero 2014). The game factorises over classes, so each class gets its own ratio and its own optimum. The demo draws 64 points for each of the eight labels: all 512 land nearest their own centre, at mean distance 0.072.

    infogan — only the generator sees the code; the discriminator does not (Chen et al. 2016). An auxiliary head \(Q\) reads the discriminator's features and predicts the code from the generated point, and its cross-entropy is added to the generator's loss. The generator is therefore rewarded for making the code recoverable, which is a lower bound on \(I(c;G(z,c))\). Here the bound is tight: 1.000 consistency and 8/8 codes resolved, against 0.521 and 2/8 with the MI weight set to zero.

    Derivation #

    The game #

    Write \(D\) for the probability the discriminator assigns to "real" and \(G(z)\) for a generated point. The minimax objective is

    \[\min_G\max_D\;V(D,G)= \mathbb E_{x\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}\bigl[\log D(x)\bigr]+ \mathbb E_{z\sim p_z}\bigl[\log\bigl(1-D(G(z))\bigr)\bigr]. \qquad (1)\]

    The code works with logits and applies \(\sigma\) inside the loss table, but the mathematics is clearest with probabilities, so this section uses \(D\) as a probability and \(a\) for the logit when the distinction matters.

    The optimal discriminator #

    For fixed \(G\), the second expectation is an expectation over the pushforward \(p_g\), so (1) is a pointwise problem in \(D(x)\):

    \[V=\int\Bigl[p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)\log D(x)+p_g(x)\log\bigl(1-D(x)\bigr)\Bigr]dx.\]

    Differentiate the integrand with respect to \(D\) and set it to zero:

    \[\frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}}{D}-\frac{p_g}{1-D}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad D^*(x)=\frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)}{p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)+p_g(x)}. \qquad (2)\]

    Substituting (2) back gives the value of the game. Let \(m=(p_{\mathrm{data}}+p_g)/2\); then

    \[V(D^*,G)=2\,\mathrm{JSD}\bigl(p_{\mathrm{data}}\,\|\,p_g\bigr)-\log 4, \qquad (3)\]

    where the Jensen–Shannon divergence is \(\tfrac12\mathrm{KL}(p_{\mathrm{data}}\|m)+\tfrac12\mathrm{KL}(p_g\|m)\). It is non-negative and zero only when the two distributions agree, so the game's minimum is \(-\log 4\) at \(p_g=p_{\mathrm{data}}\), where \(D^*=\tfrac12\) everywhere.

    This is the most misread number in the subject. A discriminator at 50% accuracy is not broken; at the equilibrium of the training distribution it is exactly right. The demo's vanilla, lsgan, non_saturating and conditional models all report 0.48–0.50, and they are the ones that cover all eight modes. The dcgan model reports 0.84 and covers seven.

    Saturating and non-saturating #

    Let \(a(x)=D(G(z))\) be the discriminator's logit on a generated point. With \(\sigma'(a)=\sigma(a)(1-\sigma(a))\),

    \[\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\log\bigl(1-\sigma(a)\bigr)=-\sigma(a), \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial a}\bigl(-\log\sigma(a)\bigr)=-\sigma(-a). \qquad (4)\]

    The first row is the saturating objective: when the discriminator is confident the sample is fake, \(a\ll 0\) and \(\sigma(a)\to 0\), so the generator's gradient disappears. The demo evaluates both at five logits:

    logit \(a\) \(-6\) \(-4\) \(-2\) \(0\) \(2\)
    \(\lvert\partial\log(1-\sigma)\rvert\) 0.002 0.018 0.119 0.500 0.881
    \(\lvert\partial(-\log\sigma)\rvert\) 0.998 0.982 0.881 0.500 0.119

    At logit \(-6\) the saturating signal is 0.002 of the other: two thousand times weaker exactly where a bad generator starts. The two objectives are related by the identity

    \[-\log\sigma(a)=-\log\bigl(1-\sigma(a)\bigr)-a,\]

    so the non-saturating loss is the negative of the saturating one shifted by \(-a\). At the equilibrium \(a=0\) both have derivative \(-\tfrac12\), which is why they share a fixed point; away from it the shift is what keeps the gradient alive, and that is the whole reason the heuristic works.

    Least squares #

    LSGAN drops the sigmoid and the logarithm. With targets 1 for real, 0 for generated and 1 for the generator's output, the discriminator solves

    \[D^*=\arg\min_D\;\tfrac12\mathbb E_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}\bigl[(D-1)^2\bigr] +\tfrac12\mathbb E_{p_g}\bigl[D^2\bigr] =\frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}}{p_{\mathrm{data}}+p_g}, \qquad (5)\]

    the same ratio as (2), obtained by a linear rather than a logistic regression. At that \(D^*\) the generator's objective is exactly

    \[\mathbb E_{p_g}\bigl[(D^*-1)^2\bigr] =\int\frac{p_g^3}{(p_{\mathrm{data}}+p_g)^2}\,dx . \qquad (6)\]

    Because the discriminator is linear, a confident verdict does not saturate: the squared error at \(D(G(z))\) far from 1 is far from zero, and its gradient is proportional to the distance. That is why this variant holds all eight modes here while the log-based ones are more delicate.

    Hinge #

    The hinge objective gives the discriminator a margin and the generator a constant push:

    \[\mathcal L_D=\mathbb E_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}\bigl[\min(0,D(x)-1)\bigr] +\mathbb E_{p_g}\bigl[\min(0,-D(x)-1)\bigr], \qquad \mathcal L_G=-\mathbb E_{p_g}\bigl[D(x)\bigr]. \qquad (7)\]

    The discriminator's per-sample gradients are \(-1\) while a real point is inside the margin and \(+1\) while a generated one is, and zero outside; the generator's is \(-1\) always. It is a support vector machine in which the boundary is learned adversarially, and it is the only row of the table whose generator signal is constant — 1.000 in the demo. Constant does not mean good: the push carries no information about distance, and this variant is the least stable of the four.

    Conditioning #

    A conditional GAN makes both players functions of the label:

    \[\mathcal L=\mathbb E_{p_{\mathrm{data}}(x,y)}\bigl[\log D(x,y)\bigr] +\mathbb E_{p_g}\bigl[\log\bigl(1-D(G(z,y),y)\bigr)\bigr]. \qquad (8)\]

    For each \(y\) the inner problem is (1) restricted to that class, so \(D^*(x,y)=p_{\mathrm{data}}(x\mid y)/(p_{\mathrm{data}}(x\mid y)+p_g(x\mid y))\) provided both sides use the same distribution of labels — which they do, because the demo draws the fake label uniformly. The label is one-hot concatenated to the input, eight extra columns on both networks; the cost is \(8\times64+8\) parameters on the discriminator and the same on the generator, and the benefit is that the model no longer has to spend capacity on class identity.

    The InfoGAN bound #

    InfoGAN wants the code to control the output without telling the discriminator which code was used. The quantity to maximise is the mutual information

    \[I(c;G(z,c))=H(c)-H(c\mid G(z,c)) =H(c)+\mathbb E_{x=G(z,c)}\bigl[\log p(c\mid x)\bigr], \qquad (9)\]

    and \(p(c\mid x)\) is intractable because it requires the generator's posterior over codes. Introduce any distribution \(Q(c\mid x)\) and use \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|Q)\ge 0\):

    \[\mathbb E\bigl[\log p(c\mid x)\bigr] =\mathbb E\bigl[\log Q(c\mid x)\bigr] +\mathrm{KL}\bigl(p(c\mid x)\,\|\,Q(c\mid x)\bigr) \ge\mathbb E\bigl[\log Q(c\mid x)\bigr].\]

    Combining with (9),

    \[I(c;G(z,c))\ge H(c)+\mathbb E_{z,c}\bigl[\log Q(c\mid G(z,c))\bigr]. \qquad (10)\]

    With uniform codes \(H(c)=\log 8\) is a constant, so the practical objective is to make an auxiliary classifier \(Q\), reading the discriminator's features, good at naming the code of a generated point:

    \[\max_{G,Q}\;\mathbb E_{z,c}\bigl[\log Q(c\mid G(z,c))\bigr]. \qquad (11)\]

    \(Q\) is trained by cross-entropy on the generated batch; the same cross-entropy's gradient is passed to the generator. With \(\mathcal L_G\) the adversarial loss and \(\mathrm{CE}\) the auxiliary cross-entropy, the generator's update is

    \[\theta_G\leftarrow\theta_G-\eta\Bigl( \nabla_{\theta_G}\mathcal L_G +\lambda\,\nabla_{\theta_G}\mathrm{CE}\Bigr). \qquad (12)\]

    The sign in (12) is the whole variant. The first version of this file subtracted the auxiliary gradient, and the run looked healthy: the adversarial loss went down and mode coverage stayed 8/8, because the generator can ignore the code and still produce eight modes. What collapsed was the code — 0.22 consistency, below the 0.52 that the code channel reaches with no MI term at all, because the generator was being trained to make the code unpredictable. A wrong sign in an auxiliary term is invisible in the adversarial loss; only the auxiliary metric catches it.

    A discriminator with a constant output #

    The simplest failure to diagnose is a discriminator that outputs the same logit for everything. Fix a base rate \(\pi\) of positives and a constant logit \(c\); the binary cross-entropy is

    \[\mathcal L(c)=-\pi\log\sigma(c)-(1-\pi)\log\bigl(1-\sigma(c)\bigr),\]

    whose derivative is \(\sigma(c)-\pi\). Hence

    \[\hat c=\log\frac{\pi}{1-\pi}, \qquad \mathcal L(\hat c)=\mathrm H(\pi), \qquad (13)\]

    the entropy of the label. The demo sweeps the logit on a grid for three base rates and recovers the entropy to within 0.002 (the grid step): 0.325 nats at \(\pi=0.1\), 0.693 at \(\pi=0.5\), 0.325 at \(\pi=0.9\). A discriminator stuck at a constant has not learned nothing; it has learned the marginal and nothing else, which is precisely what a generator that matched the marginal would leave it. The number says which aggregate matched, never which modes are missing.

    Code walkthrough #

    The data first. Eight centres on a circle of radius 0.8, isotropic noise of 0.07, 256 points each. The radius matters: the generator ends in tanh, so anything outside \([-1,1]\) is unreachable and the model would pile up on the boundary.

    def centres() -> np.ndarray:
    """The eight mode centres, (8, 2)."""
    angle = 2.0 * np.pi * np.arange(MODES) / MODES
    return RADIUS * np.stack([np.cos(angle), np.sin(angle)], axis=1)

    def make_data(seed: int, per_mode: int = 256) -> tuple:
    """Return (x, y) with `per_mode` points around each of the eight centres."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x = np.vstack([c + rng.normal(0.0, SPREAD, (per_mode, 2))
                   for c in centres()])
    y = np.repeat(np.arange(MODES), per_mode)
    order = rng.permutation(len(y))
    return x[order], y[order]

    Coverage is measured against those centres, not against a loss. A mode counts when it wins more than 3% of 1024 draws, so a collapsed generator scores one and a uniform one scores eight; the 3% floor is a choice, and every number in this file inherits it.

    def mode_stats(samples: np.ndarray) -> tuple:
    """Return (modes covered, largest share, mean distance to nearest centre).

    A mode counts as covered when it wins more than 3% of the samples, so a
    collapsed generator scores one and a uniform one scores eight.
    """
    s = np.asarray(samples, dtype=float)
    d = np.linalg.norm(s[:, None, :] - centres()[None], axis=2)
    share = np.bincount(d.argmin(axis=1), minlength=MODES) / len(s)
    return int(np.sum(share > 0.03)), float(share.max()), float(d.min(1).mean())

    Both networks are stacks of dense layers with one forward cache per layer. The generator's last layer is tanh; the discriminator's is linear, and the sigmoid lives in the loss table so that the hinge and least-squares variants can omit it.

    def _forward(layers: list, x: np.ndarray, leak: float = 0.0,
             tanh_last: bool = False) -> tuple:
    """Run the stack; returns (output, cache) with one entry per layer."""
    cache = []
    h = x
    last = len(layers) - 1
    for i, layer in enumerate(layers):
        pre = h @ layer["W"] + layer["b"]
        if i == last:
            out = np.tanh(pre) if tanh_last else pre
        else:
            out = np.where(pre > 0.0, pre, leak * pre)
        cache.append((h, pre, out))
        h = out
    return h, cache

    The backward pass returns both the parameter gradients and the gradient with respect to its input, because the generator needs the latter: the chain rule through the discriminator is the generator's loss. dout already carries the \(1/n\) of a batch mean, so the two share one scale.

    def _backward(layers: list, cache: list, dout: np.ndarray, leak: float = 0.0,
              tanh_last: bool = False) -> tuple:
    """Return (per-layer grads, gradient wrt the input).

    `dout` must already carry the 1/n of a batch mean, so that the parameter
    gradients and the input gradient share one scale.
    """
    grads = [None] * len(layers)
    d = dout
    last = len(layers) - 1
    for i in range(last, -1, -1):
        h, pre, out = cache[i]
        if i == last:
            if tanh_last:
                d = d * (1.0 - out ** 2)
        else:
            d = d * np.where(pre > 0.0, 1.0, leak)
        grads[i] = {"W": h.T @ d, "b": d.sum(axis=0)}
        d = d @ layers[i]["W"].T
    return grads, d

    The seven variants are one function. Each row is a derivative with respect to the discriminator logit; the discriminator descends the first two, the generator descends the third. The least-squares rows are linear because \(D\) is linear; the hinge rows are indicators; the log rows use _sig.

    def loss_grads(variant: str, real: np.ndarray, fake: np.ndarray) -> tuple:
    """Return (dD/d real, dD/d fake, dG/d fake), one entry per sample.

    The discriminator descends its two rows; the generator descends the third.
    Keeping all three here is what makes the variants comparable: they differ
    in this table, in the network shapes, and in what the networks are shown.
    """
    if variant == "vanilla":
        return _sig(real) - 1.0, _sig(fake), -_sig(fake)
    if variant in ("dcgan", "non_saturating", "conditional", "infogan"):
        return _sig(real) - 1.0, _sig(fake), _sig(fake) - 1.0
    if variant == "lsgan":
        return real - 1.0, fake, fake - 1.0
    if variant == "hinge":
        return (-(real < 1.0).astype(float),
                (fake > -1.0).astype(float), -1.0)

    Network shapes are a function of the variant. conditional grows both inputs by the eight one-hot columns; infogan grows only the generator's; dcgan adds a layer and doubles the width.

        def _sizes(self) -> tuple:
        d_in = 2 + (CODE if self.conditional else 0)
        g_in = self.z_dim + (CODE if self.conditional or self.infogan else 0)
        depth = 3 if self.variant == "dcgan" else 2
        return ([d_in] + [self.hidden] * depth + [1],
                [g_in] + [self.hidden] * depth + [2])

    That asymmetry is the whole of InfoGAN. The discriminator is shown a point and nothing else, so the only way the code can become recoverable is through \(Q\) and the generator's auxiliary gradient.

        def _d_input(self, x: np.ndarray, c: np.ndarray | None) -> np.ndarray:
        # Only the conditional variant shows the code to the discriminator;
        # InfoGAN's whole point is that it does not.
        onehot = self._onehot(c) if self.conditional else None
        return x if onehot is None else np.concatenate([x, onehot], axis=1)

    One training step is two discriminator passes (real, fake), one accumulator addition, then a fresh discriminator pass on the same generated batch for the generator. The generator is updated against the discriminator it just changed, which is the standard simultaneous update; no extra inner loop.

                              _col(kdr, self.batch) / self.batch, LEAK)
            gf, _ = _backward(params["D"], df_cache,

            grads_d = _add(gr, gf)
            self.history.append(float(np.mean(np.abs(kgen))))

            d_fake, df_cache = _forward(

            if self.infogan:

                                     d_logits @ params["Q"]["W"].T, LEAK)

                # so add its gradient instead of subtracting it.
                dx = dx + self.mi_weight * dx_mi
            if self.conditional or self.infogan:
                dx = dx[:, :self.z_dim]
            grads_g, _ = _backward(params["G"], g_cache, dx, tanh_last=True)
            opt_g.step(params["G"], grads_g)

    The InfoGAN block fits \(Q\) on the generated batch only — the quantity being bounded is \(I(c;G(z,c))\), so real points are not evidence about the code — and then pushes the same cross-entropy back through the discriminator's features into the generator. The Q update and the D update use separate Adam states, but the feature layers are shared, so grads_d receives the auxiliary gradient too.

    Finally the test. The code need not equal the mode's number: InfoGAN has no reason to use the same labelling, and forcing it would measure the indexing convention rather than the information. What matters is that each code lands on one mode.

            hits = 0
        resolved = 0

            if not len(mine):
                continue
            top = int(np.bincount(mine, minlength=MODES).max())

            resolved += int(top >= 0.6 * len(mine))
        return hits / n, resolved

    With no MI term the code channel still gives 0.521 consistency and 2 of 8 codes resolved; the generator uses the code as a private random seed even when nothing asks it to. With the term, both are 1.000 and 8. That gap — not the coverage gap, which is zero either way — is what InfoGAN buys.

    Complexity #

    The standard pair has 4482 generator parameters and 4417 discriminator parameters: two hidden layers of width 64, plus the input and output. The conditional model adds \(8\times64+8=520\) parameters to each network, because eight one-hot columns enter each first layer. InfoGAN adds 520 to the generator and a 520-parameter \(Q\) head, but not to the discriminator. The dcgan variant is the outlier: 35,458 and 33,537, roughly eight times the standard pair, for one more layer and double the width.

    One step touches each generated batch three times — once through \(G\), and twice through \(D\) (real and fake) plus a second fake pass after the update. At batch 128 that is about 5M multiply-adds per step for the standard pair, so 20G over the demo's 4000 steps. dcgan has eight times the parameters, costs about 35M per step, and reaches 140G over the same run. The demo trains eight models (seven variants plus the zero-MI ablation), which is 32,000 adversarial steps; the cost is linear in the number of variants and quadratic in the width, and there is no epoch structure to tune.

    Memory is the parameter count plus the largest activation cache: batch times width per layer, doubled by Adam's two moment estimates. At this size everything fits in L2; at image scale the discriminator's activations, not its weights, are the constraint, and gradient checkpointing in \(D\) is the usual answer.

    Failure modes #

    Mode collapse is the objective, not a bug. Any generator whose support is inside the data's support can drive the discriminator to a constant on that support; the missing modes are simply never sampled, so they contribute nothing to either expectation. The demo's hinge run keeps five modes with a 0.30 largest share. Remedies change the objective (Wasserstein, which compares distributions rather than classifying points), the batch (minibatch discrimination), or the update (unrolled GANs); none of them is a proof.

    The saturating gradient vanishes at exactly the wrong time. At logit \(-6\) the saturating signal is 0.002 of the non-saturating one. The non-saturating form is a heuristic, not an equivalent objective — the identity above shows the extra \(-a\) term — and it is the reason every practical GAN writes \(-\log D\) and calls the original "vanilla".

    An auxiliary term's sign is invisible in the main loss. Adding instead of subtracting the InfoGAN cross-entropy gave a healthy adversarial loss, full mode coverage and a code that was anti-learned: 0.22 consistency against a 0.53 baseline. The fix is one character, and only the auxiliary metric can see it.

    A discriminator that is too good starves the generator. dcgan reaches 0.84 accuracy and drops a mode; the generator is chasing a boundary that has already moved. One-sided label smoothing, input noise, a smaller \(D\), or a slower \(D\) learning rate all trade some discrimination for signal. The opposite failure — a discriminator that never learns — leaves the generator with a constant loss and produces blobs.

    Batch normalisation in the discriminator leaks across the batch. If real and fake points share a normalisation batch, each fake's score depends on the real points beside it, and the generator can exploit the coupling. The usual fix is to normalise each batch separately or to use layer normalisation; this file uses no normalisation, which is feasible only because the data is two dimensional and standardised by construction.

    The output range is a hard constraint. The generator ends in tanh, so a mode at radius 1.2 would be unreachable and all mass would collect on the unit circle's boundary in that direction. The data here sits at 0.8 for exactly this reason. At image scale the same mistake appears as clipped highlights and saturated colours.

    Seed sensitivity is large enough to change the verdict. hinge keeps five modes in the table and six on a fresh draw; vanilla keeps eight either time. A single seed is an anecdote, and a table of single seeds is a table of anecdotes. The demo's thresholds are deliberately loose for this reason: it asserts that the stable losses keep at least seven modes and that hinge keeps fewer than vanilla, not that any particular count is exact.

    The loss is not a quality metric. The signal column ranges from 0.496 to 1.000 across variants that produce equally good samples, and no row's loss is comparable to another's. Coverage, distance to the nearest centre and precision/recall against a held-out set are the honest measures; the 3% coverage threshold is itself a choice that a different threshold would move.

    Applications #

    Image synthesis. The reason the field exists: from DCGAN's 64×64 bedrooms to StyleGAN's photorealistic faces, the generator is a sampler from a distribution that was only ever given as examples. The engineering that matters is architectural (progressive growing, style modulation) and the loss is often the least interesting part.

    Conditional generation and translation. pix2pix and CycleGAN make the label or the other domain part of the input; the same conditional game in (8) is the whole of it. Super-resolution, colourisation, inpainting and style transfer are all conditional GANs with different conditioning.

    As a loss function. A discriminator trained on real data is a learned perceptual metric, and it is used as an auxiliary loss in compression, speech enhancement and video prediction long after the generator is gone. This is the most durable use: adversarial training as a regulariser, not as a sampler.

    Synthetic data and augmentation. A GAN can produce more examples of a rare class than were measured. The caveat is the one from the failure section: the generator drops modes and memorises training points, so downstream models inherit both biases. Differential-privacy-flavoured GANs exist but do not make this safe by construction.

    Adversarial robustness. The same saddle point, read the other way: perturb an input to maximise the loss of a fixed classifier. Fast gradient sign and PGD are the inner maximisation; adversarial training is the outer minimisation. The GAN view explains why the perturbation transfers between models.

    Inverse problems. Given a forward operator and a measurement, sample the posterior by optimising a latent code so that \(A(G(z))\) matches the measurement, with the GAN prior standing in for a likelihood. Compressed sensing with generative priors (Bora et al. 2017) is this recipe.
