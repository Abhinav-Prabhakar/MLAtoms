    14 · Generative models/tier 2/6 variants/source

    Normalising flows — one invertible map, one exact likelihood

    A normalising flow is a diffeomorphism \(f\) trained to push a standard normal through itself onto the data. Because \(f\) is invertible and its Jacobian determinant is cheap to compute, the model has a closed-form density at every point, not just a sampler. That single property separates this atom from the rest of the chapter: the generator in gan.py can be judged only by the statistics of what it emits, while here the loss itself is the quantity of interest, and the gap between the fitted density and the true one is a number you can print.

    The six variants are six answers to "which invertible map". They share the same data, the same optimiser and the same budget, so the table in the demo is a comparison of architectures at fixed compute, and the spread is large: the best variant is 0.42 nats closer to the truth than the worst while using less than half the parameters of the most expensive one.

    Intuition #

    Picture the data as eight tight blobs arranged on a circle of radius 0.8. A flow does not draw new blobs; it learns to unbend the plane so that those blobs become the eight mild bumps of a standard normal, then samples the normal and replays the bend backwards.

    flowchart LR
    X["x, eight blobs"] --> B1["actnorm"]
    B1 --> B2["1x1 mix"]
    B2 --> B3["affine coupling"]
    B3 --> B4["... repeated"]
    B4 --> Z["z, one bump"]
    Z -.->|"inverse"| X

    Every block must be invertible, and every block must report the volume change it caused, because the density transforms by that volume. The whole design problem is: what invertible map has a cheap Jacobian determinant? A general matrix costs \(O(d^3)\) to invert and does not adapt. A coupling layer restricts the map so that one coordinate is copied and the other is an affine function of the copy — and then the Jacobian is triangular, with one diagonal entry equal to 1 and the other equal to a learned scale. That is the trick everything else builds on.

          x1 ────────────────► y1 = x1
       │
       ├─ s(x1) ─┐
       └─ t(x1) ─┴────────► y2 = (x2 - t(x1)) · e^{-s(x1)}

    Two things follow, and both are visible in the numbers.

    The first is that a coupling layer is only half a transform. Coordinate 1 passes through untouched, so a single layer cannot move the data in both directions; it can only shear. Stacking layers with the roles alternated is what buys a real map, and the depth sweep makes this quantitative: one layer reaches 1.785 nats, two 0.568, three 0.341, six 0.171. More than half of the total improvement comes from the second layer, because that is the first one that can move both coordinates.

    The second is that the likelihood is a volume bookkeeping exercise. Nothing in the loss asks the map to place the blobs anywhere in particular; the density term \(-\log|\det J|\) is what forces the map to expand exactly where the data is dense. A model that expands too much gets a high likelihood everywhere and is punished on the test set; one that expands too little leaves the blobs tight and pays a large negative log-likelihood. That tension is present in every variant, and it is why the exact mixture's \(-0.395\) nats is a floor and not a target.

    Variants #

    variant nll gap params modes share dist radius

    continuous_flow 0.390 0.786 1250 8/8 0.140 0.179 0.809
    autoregressive_flow 0.442 0.837 2376 8/8 0.188 0.199 0.786
    spline_flow 0.500 0.895 5536 8/8 0.179 0.204 0.793

    Every variant is trained on the same 2048 points — eight Gaussians of radius 0.8 and spread 0.07 at angles \(22.5^\circ + 45^\circ k\) — for 3000 Adam steps on batches of 256, with two hidden layers of 32 SiLU units per conditioner. nll and gap are nats per sample on a held-out 2048; the exact mixture scores \(-0.395\) and a standard normal 2.161. dist is the mean distance to the nearest mode centre, against a floor of 0.088 — the mean distance from a sample of the true mixture to its own centre.

    coupling — six affine coupling layers with the split reversed on alternate layers. This is the reference: 1186 parameters per layer, one conditioner evaluation per direction, and a Jacobian that is triangular by construction. It reaches 0.171 nats and every sample lands near a mode.

    realnvp — the same stack with an activation normalisation in front of each coupling layer and with two conditioners per layer, one for the scale and one for the shift, as in the original paper. The extra network doubles the parameter count to 14256 and makes the fit worse (0.376 against 0.171). On two dimensions the shared trunk was acting as a regulariser: forcing \(s\) and \(t\) to be linear functions of a common hidden layer is a constraint that this data likes. RealNVP's other contribution, the multi-scale factoring with squeeze operations, needs four or more dimensions to be meaningful and has no analogue here.

    glow — actnorm, a \(1\times1\) convolution and a coupling layer, five times. The convolution is the cheapest possible way to mix the two coordinates: a lower-triangular \(2\times2\) matrix with positive diagonal, stored as three numbers, whose determinant is the product of the diagonal. It beats the plain coupling stack (0.080 against 0.171) with fewer parameters (5965 against 7116), because it lets every layer mix without spending a whole extra layer on the alternating split.

    autoregressive_flow — a masked autoregressive flow: two layers, each transforming both coordinates with parameters that depend on the coordinate already fixed. The Jacobian is triangular by causality rather than by copies, so a single layer is a full transform. It is the smallest of the discrete models (2376 parameters) and reaches 0.442. The catch is sampling: the inverse needs coordinate 1 before it can compute coordinate 2, so generating \(d\) coordinates costs \(d\) sequential conditioner passes. In two dimensions that is two passes; for an image with 3072 pixels it is 3072, which is why autoregressive flows are used for density estimation of small vectors and not for image generation.

    spline_flow — four coupling layers where the affine map is replaced by a monotone piecewise-linear spline with 8 bins. The bin widths come from a softmax, so they are positive and sum to a fixed total range; the derivative is piecewise constant, so the Jacobian costs one lookup. It is the most expressive map per layer and it finishes last (0.500), because a piecewise-constant derivative means the fitted density is piecewise-constant in the same way: eight bins per dimension quantise the shape of each blob, and no amount of depth removes that floor. The learned widths are far from uniform — the demo prints per-block ratios from 5.15 to 14.15 — so the spline is using its capacity, just not on the thing that matters here.

    continuous_flow — a continuous normalising flow: an ODE \(\dot x = v_\theta(x,t)\) trained by flow matching, integrated with RK4 in 20 steps. It has no invertibility constraint at all (the velocity field is an arbitrary MLP), 1250 parameters, and reaches 0.390. It is simultaneously the smallest model in the table and the third best, and its two costs are visible: the round trip is only accurate to \(3.4\times10^{-6}\) because RK4 is a discretisation, and its density needs a divergence integral rather than a sum of log-determinants.

    Derivation #

    Change of variables #

    For a diffeomorphism \(f:\mathbb R^d \to \mathbb R^d\) with inverse \(g\), and a base density \(p_Z\),

    \[\log p_X(x) = \log p_Z\bigl(f(x)\bigr) + \log\left|\det \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x)\right| . \qquad (1)\]

    With \(p_Z = \mathcal N(0,I)\) the training objective is the mean negative log-likelihood

    \[\mathcal L = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[ \frac12\lVert f(x_i)\rVert^2 + \frac d2\log 2\pi - \log\left|\det J_{f}(x_i)\right| \right]. \qquad (2)\]

    Only the last term is hard, and every variant below is a different way to make it cheap while keeping \(f\) expressive.

    Coupling #

    Split the input into two parts, here one coordinate each. Define

    \[y_1 = x_1, \qquad y_2 = \bigl(x_2 - t(x_1)\bigr)\,e^{-s(x_1)} . \qquad (3)\]

    The Jacobian is triangular with diagonal \((1, e^{-s})\), so

    \[\log\left|\det J\right| = -s(x_1), \qquad x_2 = y_2\,e^{s(x_1)} + t(x_1) . \qquad (4)\]

    The inverse needs no series expansion and no iterative solve: it recomputes the same conditioner and undoes the affine map. That is the whole attraction. Reversing the roles on alternate layers gives the map access to both coordinates, and the log-determinants add because determinants multiply along a composition:

    \[\log\left|\det J_{f_N\circ\cdots\circ f_1}\right| = \sum_{k=1}^{N}\log\left|\det J_{f_k}\right| . \qquad (5)\]

    The scale is bounded, \(s = r\tanh(u/2)\) with \(r=2\), which keeps \(e^{-s}\in[e^{-2},e^{2}]\) and stops a single layer from collapsing or exploding the volume. Without a bound the network can drive \(s\to\infty\) on a region with no data and buy an unbounded likelihood there.

    Actnorm and mixing #

    Activation normalisation is an affine map with two \(d\)-vectors:

    \[y = (x - \mu)\odot e^{-\sigma}, \qquad \log\left|\det J\right| = -\sum_{k}\sigma_k . \qquad (6)\]

    It is initialised from the first batch so that its output has zero mean and unit variance, \(\mu_k = \bar x_k\) and \(\sigma_k = \log(\mathrm{sd}_k x + 10^{-6})\); after that it trains like any other parameter. The point of the data-dependent init is that the first update is not spent undoing a scale mismatch.

    The \(1\times1\) convolution is a general linear map with a cheap determinant. Parameterising it as lower-triangular with positive diagonal,

    \[W = \begin{pmatrix} e^{a} & 0 \\ b & e^{d}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \log\left|\det W\right| = a + d , \qquad y = Wx . \qquad (7)\]

    Two of the three parameters are log-scales, so \(W\) is invertible by construction, and mixing costs one matrix-vector product with no decomposition.

    Autoregressive #

    An autoregressive flow conditions each coordinate on the ones before it:

    \[y_i = \bigl(x_i - t_i(x_{

    The Jacobian is triangular because \(y_i\) does not depend on \(x_{>i}\), so again the determinant is the product of the diagonal. Generation is the mirror image and is sequential: recovering \(x_i\) needs \(x_{, so the \(d\) coordinates come out one at a time. In two dimensions the file implements (8) with a single conditioner per layer that emits \(s_2, t_2\) from \(x_1\) and a pair of scalars \(\mu, \sigma\) for \(x_1\) itself; the alternating split makes the two layers cover both directions.

    Splines #

    On \([-B,B]\) with \(K\) bins, let \(w = \mathrm{softmax}(u)\) sum to one. The knots are offsets inside a fixed total span,

    \[k_b = -B + 2B\sum_{j

    and the map and its log-determinant are

    \[y = k_b + K w_b\,(x + B - b\,\delta), \qquad \log\left|\det J\right| = \begin{cases} \log (K w_b), & x\in(-B,B),\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases} \qquad (10)\]

    with \(\delta = 2B/K\). Outside the range the map is the identity, so the model assigns exactly standard-normal density there — a real limitation, discussed below. Because the input partition is uniform and the output partition is learned, the forward direction finds its bin by arithmetic while the inverse has to search the knots:

    \[b = \#\{j : k_j < y\}, \qquad x = -B + b\delta + \frac{y - k_b}{K w_b} . \qquad (11)\]

    Differentiating through the softmax uses its Jacobian \(\partial w_j/\partial u_i = w_j(\delta_{ij}-w_i)\), which collapses to

    \[\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial u} = w \odot \left(g - (g\cdot w)\right), \qquad g = \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial w} . \qquad (12)\]

    Continuous flows #

    A continuous flow replaces the stack with an ODE. For \(\dot x_t = v_\theta(x_t,t)\) the density obeys the instantaneous change of variables

    \[\frac{d}{dt}\log p_t(x_t) = -\nabla\cdot v_\theta(x_t,t), \qquad \log p_1(x) = \log p_0(z) + \int_{1}^{0}\nabla\cdot v_\theta(x_t,t)\,dt , \qquad (13)\]

    where \(z\) is the solution at \(t=0\) of the backward integration from \(x\) at \(t=1\). The divergence is the trace of the Jacobian, and for the two-layer MLP used here it is an explicit product of three matrices:

    \[\nabla\cdot v = \mathrm{tr}\left( \mathrm{diag}(\sigma'(h_1))W_1\, \mathrm{diag}(\sigma'(h_2))W_2\,W_3 \right), \qquad (14)\]

    with \(W_1\) the \(3\times 32\) input layer, whose first two rows are the spatial coordinates and whose third is time — so the trace runs over those two rows. Training does not need the density at all: flow matching regresses the velocity field on straight-line interpolants,

    \[x_t = (1-t)x_0 + t x_1, \qquad \mathcal L = \mathbb E\left\lVert v_\theta(x_t,t) - (x_1-x_0)\right\rVert^2 , \qquad (15)\]

    which is a plain regression and needs no ODE solve and no divergence. The density is only computed when someone asks for it.

    What the gap means #

    The gap column is not noise. For the true density \(p\) and a model \(q\),

    \[\mathbb E_p[-\log q] - \mathbb E_p[-\log p] = \mathrm{KL}(p\Vert q) \ge 0 , \qquad (16)\]

    so no flow can beat \(-0.395\) in expectation, and the observed gap is the test estimate of the KL divergence. A gap of 0.475 nats is a fit that has placed the mass almost right; 0.895 nats is a fit that has the shape wrong somewhere. The ordering of the six variants is therefore a statement about densities, which is something the adversarial atoms of this chapter cannot produce.

    Code walkthrough #

    The conditioner is the only learned object in a coupling layer, and the only subtlety is the bounded scale. raw_s is the network's first output; passing it through \(r\tanh(u/2)\) keeps the log-scale in \([-r,r]\) and is invertible as a function of \(u\), which matters because the same expression is differentiated on the way back:

    def _cond(params: list, block: dict, x1: np.ndarray) -> tuple:
    """Conditioner: x1 -> (bounded log-scale, shift) plus its cache."""
    q = block["p"]
    outs = []
    for k in range(2 if block["split"] else 1):
        sub = [params[i] for i in q[6 * k:6 * k + 6]]
        outs.append(_mlp_forward(sub, x1[:, None]))
    if len(outs) == 1:
        raw_s, raw_t = outs[0][0][:, 0], outs[0][0][:, 1]
    else:
        raw_s, raw_t = outs[0][0][:, 0], outs[1][0][:, 0]
    s = block["scale_range"] * np.tanh(0.5 * raw_s)
    return s, raw_t, (outs, raw_s)

    The split flag is the difference between the two conditioner styles: two networks of 1186 parameters each instead of one. realnvp stacks those behind an actnorm and finishes 0.2 nats worse than coupling (0.376 against 0.171). The block parameter indices are a flat slice of the module's parameter list, so a block never holds weights directly and the optimiser sees one flat vector.

    Forward is (3) and (4). The flip is applied to the input and then to the output, so every layer sees the coordinates in the same order and only the conditioner's meaning alternates:

        x1, x2 = x[:, 0], x[:, 1]
    s, t, cache = _cond(params, block, x1)
    if block["scale_first"]:
        mu, log_s = params[block["p"][6]], params[block["p"][7]]
        y1 = (x1 - mu) * np.exp(-log_s)
    else:
        y1, log_s = x1, None
    y2 = (x2 - t) * np.exp(-s)
    y = np.stack([y1, y2], axis=1)
    ld = -s - (np.full(len(x), log_s[0]) if log_s is not None else 0.0)

    The 1.0 in the backward pass is the log-determinant. It looks like a stray constant and it is not: the loss contains \(-ld = +s\), so every sample contributes \(+1\) to \(\partial \mathcal L/\partial s\) regardless of where it sits. Drop it and the scale still trains — the fit degrades quietly, which is the worst kind of bug:

        e = np.exp(-s)
    dx2 = d2 * e
    ds = 1.0 - d2 * (x2 - t) * e
    dt = -d2 * e

    d is a summed gradient throughout the file; the optimiser divides by the batch size once, at the step. That convention is what makes the 1.0 literal, and mixing the two conventions is the easiest way to train a flow that never converges — the demo's lr 2e-2 row is the mild version of that mistake.

    Actnorm's initialisation runs a forward pass through the blocks built so far, because each actnorm normalises the output of its predecessors, not the raw data. This is why it cannot be done in the constructor:

        def _init_actnorm(self, x: np.ndarray) -> None:
        """Data-dependent init: the first batch maps to zero mean unit var."""
        h = x
        for block in self.blocks:
            if block["kind"] == "actnorm":
                shift = self.params[block["p"][0]]
                log_scale = self.params[block["p"][1]]
                shift[...] = h.mean(axis=0)
                log_scale[...] = np.log(h.std(axis=0) + 1e-6)
            h = self._block_forward(block, h)[0]

    Glow's mixing layer is three scalars and one matrix product. The lower-triangular form is what makes the determinant free and the inverse trivial:

    def _mix_matrix(params: list, block: dict) -> np.ndarray:
    """Lower-triangular 1x1 convolution: [[e^a, 0], [b, e^d]]."""
    a, b, d = (params[i][0] for i in block["p"])
    return np.array([[np.exp(a), 0.0], [b, np.exp(d)]])

    The spline is (9)–(11) in three lines of array arithmetic. Note the two different bin searches: floor on the input here, and a comparison count on the output in _spline_inverse:

        w = _spline_weights(u)
    knots = _spline_knots(w)
    slope = SPLINE_BINS * w
    width = 2.0 * SPLINE_RANGE / SPLINE_BINS
    b = np.clip(np.floor((x2 + SPLINE_RANGE) / width).astype(int),
                0, SPLINE_BINS - 1)
    inside = (x2 > -SPLINE_RANGE) & (x2 < SPLINE_RANGE)
    rows = np.arange(len(x2))
    y2 = np.where(inside, knots[rows, b] + slope[rows, b]
                  * (x2 + SPLINE_RANGE - b * width), x2)
    ld = np.where(inside, np.log(slope[rows, b]), 0.0)

    Its gradient is the softmax Jacobian (12) plus the two ways a knot position affects the loss: through the bin's starting height, which depends on all widths below it, and through the slope itself. The below mask is the first of those; the g[rows, b] += line adds the second and the log-determinant term:

        below = (np.arange(SPLINE_BINS)[None, :] < b[:, None]).astype(float)
    g = inside[:, None] * 2.0 * SPLINE_RANGE * (d2[:, None] * below)
    g[rows, b] += inside * (SPLINE_BINS * (x2 + SPLINE_RANGE - b * width) * d2
                            - 1.0 / np.maximum(w[rows, b], 1e-9))
    du = w * (g - (g * w).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True))

    For the continuous flow, the divergence is a single einsum over the three-matrix product in (14). Building the two diagonal Jacobians as (n, in, out) arrays and chaining them gives the trace in one pass, with no loop over dimensions and no finite differences:

            A = P[0][None] * _dsilu(h1)[:, None, :]
        B = P[2][None] * _dsilu(h2)[:, None, :]
        J = np.einsum("nij,njk,kl->nil", A, B, P[4])
        return v, J[:, 0, 0] + J[:, 1, 1]

    The demo checks that expression against central differences and gets \(1.4\times10^{-9}\). The integrator then accumulates the trace along the path. RK4 evaluates the velocity four times per step, and in the density case each evaluation also carries its divergence, so a 20-step solve costs 80 network calls forward plus 80 divergence traces:

            for i in range(RK4_STEPS):
            t = np.full(len(x), t0 + i * h)
            if want_div:
                k1, d1 = self._velocity_div(x, t)
                k2, d2 = self._velocity_div(x + 0.5 * h * k1, t + 0.5 * h)
                k3, d3 = self._velocity_div(x + 0.5 * h * k2, t + 0.5 * h)
                k4, d4 = self._velocity_div(x + h * k3, t + h)
                acc = acc + h / 6.0 * (d1 + 2.0 * d2 + 2.0 * d3 + d4)

    The sign convention is (13) with the integral running from 1 down to 0, which is why log_prob returns base + acc and not base - acc. Getting that wrong produces a plausible-looking density that integrates to something other than one; the demo's learned density integrates to 0.9998 is the check that catches it.

    Complexity #

    Let \(d\) be the dimension, \(n\) the batch, \(h\) the hidden width and \(N\) the number of layers. Every coupling layer costs one conditioner forward and one backward, so training is \(O(nNdh^2)\) per step and the constant is the same for all the discrete variants.

    In this atom \(d=2\), \(h=32\) and a conditioner is 1186 multiply-adds per sample, so a six-layer forward pass over 2048 test points is 14.6M multiply-adds. The whole demo is dominated by training: 3000 steps of batch 256 through 7116 parameters, forward and backward, is roughly 16G multiply-adds for coupling, and the six fits plus the depth sweep and the learning-rate row come to about a minute.

    The costs that differ are in sampling and in density:

    variant parameters per-sample sampling work per-sample density work
    coupling 7116 6 conditioners 6 conditioners
    realnvp 14256 12 conditioners 12 conditioners
    glow 5965 5 conditioners, 5 mixes same
    autoregressive_flow 2376 2 sequential conditioners 2 conditioners
    spline_flow 5536 4 conditioners, 4 knot scans same
    continuous_flow 1250 80 velocity calls 80 calls plus 80 traces

    The continuous flow's column is the honest one: it uses a sixth of the parameters of coupling and pays thirteen times its sampling cost. That trade is why CNFs are used where the density or the velocity field is the product — likelihood-based inference, importance sampling, ODE-based editing — and coupling stacks are used where sampling speed dominates.

    Memory is the batch of activations plus the caches. Each block caches the conditioner's hidden layers, so a six-layer flow holds twelve hidden arrays per sample; this is the usual backpropagation cost and it is why flows are trained with smaller batches than GANs of comparable size.

    Failure modes #

    A learning rate written for the wrong convention. The file averages gradients over the batch and divides once. A reader who copies a per-sample scale of \(2\times10^{-3}\) from a summed-gradient implementation and uses it here gets a step 256 times too small, and the result is worse than doing nothing: 3.002 nats after the same 3000 steps, against 2.161 for the standard normal it never left. The map has drifted just enough to distort the base density without learning the blobs — seven modes covered, mean radius 1.219. The other direction is milder but still costly: ten times the correct step reaches 0.894 against 0.171. Adam is scale-free only asymptotically; the first thousand steps are not.

    An unbounded scale. Without the \(\tanh\) in (3), the network finds that driving \(s\to\infty\) on an empty region of the plane gives an unbounded likelihood there. The training loss falls while the samples collapse. Bounding the scale to \([-2,2]\) is not a detail; it is the constraint that keeps the map a diffeomorphism onto a sensible region.

    A coupling layer that cannot rotate. One layer leaves \(x_1\) untouched, so a dataset rotated by \(45^\circ\) relative to the axes cannot be captured — the map can shear but not turn. The depth sweep is the measurement: 1.785 at one layer, 0.568 at two. If a single-layer flow fits well, the data was already aligned with the split; if it plateaus, add layers or add Glow's mixing.

    Splines and their support. Outside \([-B,B]\) the spline map is the identity and the density is exactly standard normal, so a mode placed at radius 4 or an outlier at radius 10 is modelled as noise. Inside, the piecewise-constant derivative quantises the density, which is why spline_flow lands at 0.500 with 8 bins. The fix is more bins or a rational-quadratic spline with continuous derivatives; both cost per-bin work in the inverse.

    Autoregressive sampling is sequential. The inverse of (8) needs coordinate 1 before coordinate 2. In two dimensions that is two passes; for a 3072-pixel image it is 3072, each of which is a full network evaluation. The usual escape is to make the forward direction (training) sequential and the inverse cheap — which is exactly the trade an inverse autoregressive flow makes, at the cost of losing the exact likelihood.

    A density that integrates to the wrong number. The continuous flow's sign convention in (13) is easy to flip, and the resulting density looks reasonable pointwise while integrating to 2 or 0.5. The check that catches it costs one grid evaluation: learned density integrates to 0.9998. The same check on a discrete flow is exact by construction (1.0000 here) and therefore says nothing about the fit — only that the Jacobian bookkeeping is right.

    Silent gradient errors. The input gradient of the conditioner is used only by the block behind it, so a missing @ W.T in the first layer's backward pass leaves the last block's gradients exact and corrupts everything below it. Building this file, the check reported \(4.3\times10^{-1}\) on the first block and \(7\times10^{-10}\) on the last, which located the missing transpose in a minute. Check every block, not the one you happen to look at.

    A regime that is too easy. With eight blobs of spread 0.07 on a circle of radius 0.8, adjacent centres are 0.61 apart — nearly nine standard deviations. All six variants cover 8/8 and none merges neighbours, so the table above separates the architectures by how well they shape each blob, not by whether they can find them. Bring the radius down or widen the blobs and the ordering would change: at three or four standard deviations apart the coupling stack has to spend depth on separating modes instead of sharpening them, and the spline's 8-bin quantisation stops being the limiting factor.

    Applications #

    Density estimation and anomaly detection. The exact likelihood is the product: score a new point, threshold it, and you have a detector whose calibration is a real likelihood rather than a discriminator score. This is the main use of flows in tabular and scientific settings, where \(d\) is small enough for the sequential-inverse cost of autoregressive variants not to matter.

    Variational inference. A flow is the standard way to make a variational posterior flexible while keeping the ELBO a single expectation: the change of variables term is analytic, so the objective stays low-variance. Planar and radial flows were introduced for exactly this, and every modern normalising-flow posterior inherits the construction.

    Image and audio generation. Glow generates images at 256×256 with a coupling stack, and WaveGlow does the same for raw audio. Both rely on actnorm and the \(1\times1\) convolution from this file; the multi-scale factoring that makes them practical needs 3-D tensors and is the part that does not appear here.

    Boltzmann generators and lattice field theory. Sampling a Boltzmann distribution at a fixed temperature is a density-modelling problem with a known unnormalised target, and a flow trained by maximum likelihood on samples is the standard tool. The exactness of the likelihood is what makes the acceptance ratio computable.

    Continuous flows in diffusion. The probability-flow ODE of a diffusion model is a continuous normalising flow whose velocity is the score, so score_sde and this atom are two views of one object. Sampling from the ODE is deterministic and invertible, which is what makes image editing by inversion possible — the ddim_inversion variant in ddim.py is the discrete cousin.

    Importance sampling and resampling. A flow trained on a proposal distribution gives exact importance weights, because both the proposal and the target densities are available in closed form. This is used to correct a cheap model's samples into an expensive model's distribution without retraining the sampler.
