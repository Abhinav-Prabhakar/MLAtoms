    Wasserstein GAN — the earth-mover distance and four Lipschitz constraints

    A GAN's discriminator is a classifier, and a classifier's gradient is only useful while it is uncertain. When the model distribution and the data are far apart, a perfect discriminator separates them with probability one, its output saturates, and the generator receives a gradient of zero. The Wasserstein GAN replaces the classifier with a critic: a function trained to measure how much mass has to move to turn one distribution into the other. That quantity has a gradient everywhere, including when the distributions do not overlap — which is the situation at the start of every training run. The price is a constraint the critic must satisfy: it has to be 1-Lipschitz. This file compares the four ways of imposing that constraint that are in common use, on eight Gaussians, with the critic's shape measured rather than assumed.

    Intuition #

    The Wasserstein-1 distance between two distributions is the cheapest way to transport one onto the other when moving a unit of mass a unit of distance costs one:

    flowchart LR
    P["p: real"] -->|"move mass, cost = distance"| Q["q: model"]
    P --> D["critic f (1-Lipschitz)"]
    Q --> D
    D -->|"E_p f - E_q f"| V["W1 estimate"]

    You cannot compute the infimum over transport plans directly. The Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality turns it into a supremum over functions: the best 1-Lipschitz \(f\) gives \(W_1\) as the difference of its expectations. So the critic is not asked "is this real?" — it is asked "how high can you score the real data relative to the fake data, without ever rising faster than slope one?" The answer is a number whose units are distance, not a probability.

    That change of question fixes the vanishing gradient. Two disjoint point masses at distance \(d\) have \(W_1=d\) and a critic with slope one between them, so the generator's gradient is proportional to \(d\) from the first step. With a classifier the same configuration has a constant loss and no gradient at all.

    It also moves the difficulty. A classifier can be arbitrarily sharp; a critic may not. Every variant in this file is a different answer to one question: how do you keep a neural network 1-Lipschitz?

    • Clip the weights. The original answer. After each critic step, clamp every weight to \([-c,c]\). Simple, and it works, but the clamp is a blunt instrument: it also crushes the critic's overall scale, so the signal the generator needs shrinks with it.
    • Penalise the slope, two-sided. Add \(\lambda(\lVert\nabla f\rVert-1)^2\) at points interpolated between real and fake. This is WGAN-GP, and it is the default in most code you will read.
    • Penalise the slope, one-sided. Same term, but only where the slope exceeds one. WGAN-LP. The extra freedom matters exactly where a critic wants to be flat: at a maximum.
    • Normalise the weights spectrally. Divide each matrix by its top singular value after each step, so the product of layer norms is one.

    The last two sentences are the whole result of this file. On data with eight separated modes, the optimal critic is a potential that peaks at each mode and slopes down between them. A peak has zero gradient. A two-sided penalty forbids zero, so it forbids peaks, so the critic cannot separate the modes; the one-sided penalty allows plateaus and recovers the shape. The demo measures the critic's value and slope at a mode centre and halfway between two modes, and the table below is that measurement, not an opinion.

    There is a second, quieter theme: the Wasserstein estimate itself. It is a difference of two sample means, and with a powerful critic on a finite batch it is biased upward — the critic can separate the batch even when it cannot separate the distributions. A negative estimate after training is normal and is not a bug (the two-sided variant here ends at \(-0.071\)).

    Variants #

    variant constraint modes share distance \(\hat W\) \(\lVert\nabla D\rVert\)
    wgan clip \(c=0.01\) 8/8 0.15 0.237 +0.000 0.000
    wgan_gp two-sided, \(\lambda=10\) 5/8 0.31 0.425 −0.071 0.993
    wgan_lp one-sided, \(\lambda=10\) 8/8 0.14 0.100 +0.015 0.457
    spectral_norm power iteration 8/8 0.19 0.262 +0.019 0.165

    All four share the data, the architecture, the optimiser and the budget: 2048 points from eight Gaussians of radius 0.8 and spread 0.07; a critic \(2\to64\to64\to1\) with leaky ReLU (4417 parameters); a generator \(2\to64\to64\to2\) with ReLU and a tanh output (4482 parameters); 3000 steps of Adam at \(10^{-4}\) with \(\beta_1=0.5\); four critic updates per generator update; batches of 128. The metrics are computed on 1024 fresh samples: modes won, the largest mode's share, the mean distance to the nearest centre, the Wasserstein estimate on fresh data, and the mean critic slope at the test points.

    wgan — the original (Arjovsky, Chintala & Bottou 2017). Weight clipping is the cheapest constraint to implement and the most destructive: after clipping, the critic's slope is bounded by the product of its layers' Frobenius norms, about \(0.0058\) for these dimensions at \(c=0.01\), and the measured value is \(2.0\times10^{-4}\): across the whole test set the critic's value moves by \(4\times10^{-4}\). The critic is effectively constant, so the generator is from almost nothing — yet it covers all eight modes at distance 0.237, because the critic was informative for the first few hundred steps, before the clamp wore it down. Coverage alone would call this row a success.

    wgan_gp — the gradient penalty (Gulrajani et al. 2017), two-sided with the paper's \(\lambda=10\). It reaches five modes. The penalty is satisfied — the slope at a mode centre is 0.983 and between modes 1.072 — and that is exactly the problem: the critic is forced to climb at unit rate through the point where a separating critic needs to turn over. Its value across a mode and its neighbouring gap differs by less than 0.1, so it cannot tell them apart. The demo repeats the run at \(\lambda=1\) and gets all eight modes at distance 0.146, with slopes 0.736 and 0.843: the same penalty, relaxed enough to allow a rounded peak. This is the file's main finding.

    wgan_lp — the one-sided penalty (Petzka, Fischer & Lukovnicov 2017). The threshold at one leaves the critic free below it, and it uses that freedom exactly as theory says it should: slope 0.127 at the mode centres and 0.474 between them, with the critic's value \(+0.12\) on the modes and \(-0.08\) in the gaps. Flat plateaus on the data, steep valleys between — a critic that measures transport cost. Best sample distance of the four (0.100).

    spectral_norm — normalise each weight matrix by its top singular value (Miyato et al. 2018), one power iteration per step, carrying the vectors between steps. No penalty, no clipping, and the Lipschitz constant of the whole critic is at most one by construction. All eight modes at 0.262. The critic is flatter than the one-sided penalty's (0.166 in both places), so the generator's signal is weaker and the samples are looser; the advantage is that the constraint never has to be tuned.

    The \(\lambda=1\) diagnostic — all eight modes with a rounded peak — is the shape to look for. The two-sided row at \(\lambda=10\) is the shape to be suspicious of.

    Derivation #

    The distance and its dual #

    Let \(\Pi(p,q)\) be the set of couplings: joint distributions with marginals \(p\) and \(q\). The Wasserstein-1 distance is

    \[W_1(p,q)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(p,q)} \mathbb E_{(x,y)\sim\gamma}\bigl[\lVert x-y\rVert\bigr]. \qquad (1)\]

    The infimum is over an infinite-dimensional set of plans, which is not something to optimise with a neural network. Kantorovich duality exchanges it for a supremum over functions:

    \[W_1(p,q)=\sup_{\lVert f\rVert_L\le 1} \Bigl(\mathbb E_{x\sim p}f(x)-\mathbb E_{y\sim q}f(y)\Bigr), \qquad (2)\]

    where \(\lVert f\rVert_L\) is the Lipschitz constant of \(f\). Two facts make the dual useful. First, the supremum is attained by \(f(x)=\mathrm{dist}(x,A)\) when \(p\) is supported on a set \(A\) and \(q\) elsewhere — a distance function, not a classifier. Second, the bound is sharp: if you can find any 1-Lipschitz \(f\), its objective is a lower bound on \(W_1\), so maximising it is a well-posed problem even when you cannot solve it exactly.

    The game #

    Write the critic \(f_\theta\) and the generator \(G_\phi\). The WGAN objective is

    \[\max_{\theta}\;\mathbb E_{x\sim p}f_\theta(x) -\mathbb E_{z}f_\theta(G_\phi(z)), \qquad \min_{\phi}\;-\mathbb E_{z}f_\theta(G_\phi(z)), \qquad (3)\]

    under a constraint on \(\lVert f_\theta\rVert_L\) that the four variants implement differently. Note what is absent: a sigmoid, a log, a ratio. Both expectations are of the critic's raw output, and the generator's loss is the negative of its own expectation. The critic is not trained to output one for real data and zero for fake; only the difference matters, and the scale is set by the constraint.

    The gradients follow directly:

    \[\nabla_\theta\Bigl(\mathbb E_p f_\theta-\mathbb E_q f_\theta\Bigr) =\mathbb E_p\nabla_\theta f_\theta-\mathbb E_q\nabla_\theta f_\theta, \qquad (4)\]

    which is two backward passes with opposite signs. Getting that sign wrong — descending on the objective instead of ascending — trains the critic to prefer fakes, and the generator follows it into a single blob. This is not a hypothetical: it is the first bug this file hit.

    The generator's gradient is the critic's slope at the generated points, propagated through \(G\):

    \[\nabla_\phi\,\mathbb E_z f_\theta(G_\phi(z)) =\mathbb E_z\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{\partial G_\phi(z)}{\partial\phi}\Bigr)^{\mathsf T} \nabla_x f_\theta(G_\phi(z))\Bigr]. \qquad (5)\]

    Equation (5) is where the Lipschitz constraint pays off. The signal is \(\nabla_x f\), whose norm is bounded below by the constraint only indirectly — but unlike a classifier's sigmoid derivative, it does not vanish when the two distributions are far apart: a 1-Lipschitz critic that separates distant modes must have slope near one somewhere between them.

    Clipping #

    With every weight in \([-c,c]\), each layer's spectral norm is at most its Frobenius norm, so

    \[\lVert f_\theta\rVert_L\le\prod_{\ell=1}^{3}c\sqrt{m_\ell n_\ell} =0.01\sqrt{2\cdot 64}\;\cdot\;0.01\sqrt{64\cdot 64}\;\cdot\; 0.01\sqrt{64}\approx0.0058 . \qquad (6)\]

    The bound is loose but the message is exact: clipping does not merely bound the slope, it bounds the whole function's scale, and with three layers the bound compounds. The measured slope is \(0.0004\) — three orders of magnitude below the unit slope the dual would like, and below the resolution of the demo's table. The weights are also clipped after every critic step, so the effective step size shrinks as the weights pile up against the box.

    The two-sided penalty #

    WGAN-GP drops the hard constraint for a soft one. Sample a point on the segment between a real and a generated point and penalise deviation of the slope from one:

    \[\mathcal L_{\mathrm{GP}}=\mathbb E_{\hat x} \bigl(\lVert\nabla_{\hat x}f_\theta(\hat x)\rVert-1\bigr)^2, \qquad \hat x=\epsilon x+(1-\epsilon)\tilde x, \quad\epsilon\sim U(0,1). \qquad (7)\]

    The segment is the important part. The critic only needs unit slope where it is being asked to separate, which is between the two distributions; penalising everywhere would be wasteful and would fight the data's own geometry. The penalty enters the critic's loss as \(\lambda\mathcal L_{\mathrm{GP}}\), and \(\lambda=10\) is the paper's default.

    Computing (7) requires differentiating the critic's gradient — a double backward pass. For the three-layer critic, with masks \(m_1,m_2\in\{1,\text{slope}\}\) at the two hidden layers and \(w_3\) the last weight column,

    \[\nabla_x f=W_1^{\mathsf T}\,\mathrm{diag}(m_1)\,W_2^{\mathsf T}\, \mathrm{diag}(m_2)\,w_3 . \qquad (8)\]

    The critic is piecewise linear, so its second derivative is zero almost everywhere and the masks can be treated as constants. Writing \(v=m_2\odot w_3\), \(t=m_1\odot(W_2^{\mathsf T}v)\), \(n=\lVert W_1^{\mathsf T}t\rVert\), \(c=2(n-1)\) and \(\hat g=W_1^{\mathsf T}t/n\) per sample, the parameter gradients are three matrix products:

    \[\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial W_1}=\frac1N\sum_n c_n\,\hat g_n\,t_n^{\mathsf T}, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial W_2}=\frac1N\sum_n c_n\, \bigl(m_1\odot(W_1\hat g_n)\bigr)v_n^{\mathsf T}, \qquad (9)\]
    \[\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial w_3}=\frac1N\sum_n c_n\, m_2\odot\bigl(W_2\,(m_1\odot(W_1\hat g_n))\bigr). \qquad (10)\]

    Two details in (9)–(10) are easy to get wrong and are worth checking numerically. The \(1/N\) is there because the penalty is a mean; without it the penalty is \(N\) times too strong. And \(c_n\) already contains the \(1/n\) from differentiating \(\lVert\cdot\rVert\), so \(\hat g_n\) must be divided by \(n\) exactly once. The demo checks the whole gradient against central differences and gets a maximum error of \(2.7\times10^{-10}\).

    The one-sided penalty #

    The relaxation is one line:

    \[\mathcal L_{\mathrm{LP}}=\mathbb E_{\hat x} \bigl(\max(0,\lVert\nabla_{\hat x}f_\theta(\hat x)\rVert-1)\bigr)^2 . \qquad (11)\]

    Slopes below one cost nothing, so the critic may be flat. That sounds like a weaker guarantee — and it is, in the sense that the critic is no longer forced to be exactly 1-Lipschitz — but the inequality \(\lVert f\rVert_L\le 1\) is all the dual needs, and flat regions are consistent with it. What (11) buys is the freedom to have a maximum.

    Why a maximum is non-negotiable here: a separating critic on eight point masses is essentially \(\min_k\lVert x-c_k\rVert\)-like, which has a cusp at each mode and slope one on the Voronoi boundaries. A cusp is not differentiable, so in practice the critic approximates it with a rounded peak, whose slope at the top is below one. The two-sided penalty charges for that rounding; the one-sided penalty does not. The measurements are the signature: wgan_lp has slope 0.127 at the centres and 0.474 between them, while wgan_gp has 0.983 and 1.072 — unit slope straight through the peak.

    Spectral normalisation #

    Take the largest singular value of each weight matrix and divide:

    \[v\leftarrow\frac{W^{\mathsf T}u}{\lVert W^{\mathsf T}u\rVert}, \quad u\leftarrow\frac{Wv}{\lVert Wv\rVert}, \quad W\leftarrow\frac{W}{\sigma_1(W)}, \quad \sigma_1(W)\approx\lVert Wv\rVert. \qquad (12)\]

    The power iteration estimates \(\sigma_1\) from a pair of vectors carried between steps; one iteration per training step is enough because \(W\) changes slowly. Since the spectral norm is sub-multiplicative, normalising every layer bounds the critic by one:

    \[\lVert f\rVert_L\le\prod_{\ell}\sigma_1(W_\ell)=1 . \qquad (13)\]

    Unlike clipping, this does not shrink the function's scale: a matrix with all its singular values equal to one is an isometry, which can move mass as far as the data requires. The constraint is enforced by direction rather than by magnitude, and there is nothing to tune.

    The estimate #

    For reporting, the dual is evaluated on samples:

    \[\hat W=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m f(x_i)-\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m f(G(z_j)). \qquad (14)\]

    \(\hat W\) is a lower bound on \(W_1\) for a fixed critic, and it is maximised over the critic, so it inherits the critic's overfitting to the batch. On 1024 points it is noisy, and after the generator has adapted to the critic it can go negative: that means the critic scores this batch of fakes above this batch of reals, which is a statement about the two samples, not about the distributions. The table reports it because it is the quantity the critic is trained on, not because it ranks the variants.

    Code walkthrough #

    The critic's input gradient is the whole of the gradient penalty's inner computation, and it is also the diagnostic the demo uses. It is written as the product of the layer matrices with the activation masks taken at x:

    def _input_grad(params: list, x: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """The critic's gradient with respect to its input, (n, DIM).

    The critic is piecewise linear, so the gradient is a product of the
    layer matrices with the activation masks taken at `x`.
    """
    _, cache = _forward(params, x, LEAK, "linear")
    w = params[-1]["W"][:, 0]
    d = np.broadcast_to(w, (len(x), len(w)))
    for i in range(len(params) - 2, -1, -1):
        pre = cache[i][1]
        d = (d * np.where(pre > 0.0, 1.0, LEAK)) @ params[i]["W"].T
    return d

    The penalty reuses that product but keeps the intermediate vectors, because differentiating through it needs them. The masks come from the same forward pass:

        _, cache = _forward(params, xh, LEAK, "linear")
    m1 = np.where(cache[0][1] > 0.0, 1.0, LEAK)
    m2 = np.where(cache[1][1] > 0.0, 1.0, LEAK)
    w1, w2, w3 = params[0]["W"], params[1]["W"], params[2]["W"][:, 0]
    v = m2 * w3
    t = m1 * (v @ w2.T)
    g = t @ w1.T
    n = np.linalg.norm(g, axis=1)
    coef = 2.0 * (n - 1.0)
    if one_sided:
        coef = np.where(n > 1.0, coef, 0.0)
    coef = coef / len(xh)
    ghat = g / np.maximum(n, 1e-12)[:, None]

    coef is \(c_n/N\) and ghat is \(\hat g_n\) from (9)–(10). The three parameter gradients are then three einsums, each one a sum over the batch of an outer product:

        gw1 = np.einsum("n,nd,nh->dh", coef, ghat, t)
    w1g = ghat @ w1
    gw2 = np.einsum("n,nh,nk->hk", coef, w1g * m1, v)
    p = (m1 * w1g) @ w2
    gw3 = np.einsum("n,nh->h", coef, m2 * p)
    return float(np.mean(n)), [
        {"W": gw1, "b": np.zeros_like(params[0]["b"])},
        {"W": gw2, "b": np.zeros_like(params[1]["b"])},
        {"W": gw3[:, None], "b": np.zeros_like(params[2]["b"])},
    ]

    Biases do not appear in the penalty at all: adding a constant to the critic changes its value but not its slope, so the gradient with respect to every bias is exactly zero, and the code returns zeros rather than omitting the keys.

    The spectral update divides by the estimate from the carried vectors. After the division the ratio measured with the same vector is one exactly, which is why the demo can assert it to \(10^{-5}\):

        for layer, u in zip(params, us, strict=True):
        w = layer["W"]
        v = w.T @ u
        v /= np.linalg.norm(v) + 1e-12
        u[...] = w @ v
        u /= np.linalg.norm(u) + 1e-12
        layer["W"] = w / (np.linalg.norm(w @ v) + 1e-12)

    The critic step is two backward passes with opposite signs — equation (4) — followed by whichever constraint the variant selects. The schema is the same for all four, and only the tail differs:

                    gr, _ = _backward(d_params, cr,
                                  np.full((self.batch, 1), -1.0 / self.batch),
                                  LEAK, "linear")
                gf, _ = _backward(d_params, cf,
                                  np.full((self.batch, 1), 1.0 / self.batch),
                                  LEAK, "linear")
                grads = [{key: gr[i][key] + gf[i][key] for key in gr[i]}
                         for i in range(len(d_params))]

    The penalty branch interpolates a real point and a fake one, calls _penalty, and adds \(\lambda\) times its gradient. The other two branches clamp or normalise after the update:

                    if self.constraint in ("two_sided", "one_sided"):
                    eps = rng.random((self.batch, 1))
                    xh = eps * xr + (1.0 - eps) * fake
                    _, pg = _penalty(d_params, xh,
                                     self.constraint == "one_sided")
                    grads = [{key: grads[i][key] + self.lam * pg[i][key]
                              for key in grads[i]}
                             for i in range(len(d_params))]
                opt_d.step(d_params, grads)
                if self.constraint == "clip":
                    for L in d_params:
                        for key in L:
                            np.clip(L[key], -self.clip, self.clip, out=L[key])
                elif self.constraint == "spectral":
                    _spectral(d_params, us)

    The generator step goes through the critic once more: one forward pass at the current fakes, one backward pass to get \(\nabla_x f\), and one backward pass through \(G\) with that as the upstream gradient. This is equation (5) in code, and it is the only place where the generator sees the critic:

                z = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, (self.batch, LATENT))
            fake, cg = _forward(g_params, z, 0.0, "tanh")
            _, cf = _forward(d_params, fake, LEAK, "linear")
            _, dx = _backward(d_params, cf,
                              np.full((self.batch, 1), -1.0 / self.batch),
                              LEAK, "linear")
            gg, _ = _backward(g_params, cg, dx, 0.0, "tanh")
            opt_g.step(g_params, gg)

    One implementation note that the earlier atoms share: _backward always propagates through the first matrix, so its second return value is the gradient with respect to the input, not to the first hidden activation. The critic hands that value straight to the generator. A version that stops after the first layer returns a vector in the wrong space, and the shapes fail loudly — which is how the bug was found here.

    Complexity #

    The critic has 4417 parameters and the generator 4482: two hidden layers of 64, so almost all of the weight is in the \(64\times64\) matrices. A forward pass on a batch of 128 is therefore about 0.57M multiply-adds, and a backward pass about twice that.

    A step runs four critic updates and one generator update. Without a penalty that is \(4\times\) (two forwards plus two backwards) plus (one forward through \(G\), one through \(D\), and two backwards), roughly 17M multiply-adds. The gradient penalty adds one forward and one double backward per critic update — the double backward is two more matrix products — about 1.7M each, for roughly 24M per step with a penalty. Over 3000 steps that is about 70G multiply-adds.

    The comparison with a plain GAN is instructive: there the discriminator does one forward and one backward, and the ratio of critic to generator work is one to one. WGAN spends four to five times more on the critic, because the dual requires an approximately optimal critic for the generator's gradient to mean anything. That is the real cost of the Wasserstein objective, and it is the reason WGAN was displaced in practice by objectives that need less inner optimisation.

    Sampling is a single forward pass through \(G\) — 4482 multiply-adds per point, independent of the number of training steps. Nothing here is sequential, unlike diffusion.

    Memory is dominated by Adam's two moments per parameter: 3× the parameter count per network, plus the activations of one batch per layer (128×64 numbers per hidden layer). The double backward needs the critic's cached activations, not a second graph, because the masks are all it uses from the forward pass.

    Failure modes #

    A critic crushed by clipping. The bound in (6) compounds over layers, and three layers at \(c=0.01\) leave a slope of \(2\times10^{-4}\) — the critic is constant, the generator's signal is gone, and the samples are inherited from the first few hundred steps. A larger clip (\(0.05\), \(0.1\)) restores the slope, but now the constraint is looser and the whole point of the method is diluted. Weight clipping is a constraint you set by hand, and the useful range is narrow.

    A two-sided penalty that forbids the optimum. This is the file's headline. The penalty is satisfied to 0.02 and the critic still cannot separate five of the eight modes, because a separating critic needs a maximum at each mode and a maximum needs a zero slope. \(\lambda=10\) is strong enough that the critic spends its capacity satisfying the constraint rather than separating; at \(\lambda=1\) the same code finds all eight modes. The lesson is not "GP is broken" — it is that the penalty constant is a real modelling choice, and that a satisfied penalty is not evidence of a good critic.

    Reading \(\hat W\) as a score. The estimate is a difference of sample means with an optimised critic, and it goes negative after the generator has adapted (wgan_gp ends at −0.071 while covering five modes). Its scale is not comparable across variants with different constraints, because the constraints set different function scales. It is a training diagnostic — is the critic separating this batch? — not a metric.

    A penalty that is not a mean. Omitting the \(1/N\), or dividing by \(\lVert g\rVert\) twice, changes the effective \(\lambda\) by a factor of the batch size or of the slope, and the symptom is a critic that either ignores the penalty or is frozen by it. The numerical gradient check in the demo is three lines of central differences and catches both; it is the cheapest test in the file and the one that found the actual bug.

    One power iteration per step. Spectral normalisation assumes the top singular vector changes slowly. It does here, but a large learning rate or a rapidly changing critic makes the estimate lag, and the true top singular value drifts above one — the constraint is then violated by an amount the code cannot see. The demo measures the true value with an SVD after training and finds it within \(2.4\times10^{-7}\) of one; a production model with a larger learning rate would want two or three iterations.

    Mode coverage without quality. wgan covers all eight modes and has the second-worst distance, because coverage counts which centre wins each sample, not how tightly the samples cluster. wgan_lp wins on distance with the same coverage. A single number is never enough: the demo reports coverage, share, distance, slope and critic shape for this reason.

    A critic that is too strong or too weak. Four critic updates per generator update is a compromise. Fewer and the dual is not estimated; more and the critic overfits the batch, \(\hat W\) inflates, and the generator chases a critic that no longer reflects the data. The WGAN paper's advice — keep the critic near its optimum without letting it memorise — has no closed form, which is the honest summary of GAN tuning.

    Applications #

    Image generation, 2017–2019. WGAN-GP was the standard recipe for high-resolution image synthesis before style-based generators, and the one-sided variant and spectral normalisation are still used as regularisers in modern discriminators. BigGAN's discriminator is spectrally normalised, and the constraint is applied per layer exactly as in (12).

    Generative models with a meaningful loss. Because \(\hat W\) approximates a distance, it can be monitored during training in a way that a classifier's accuracy cannot. That is useful for debugging and for model selection when the downstream metric is expensive.

    Optimal transport and domain adaptation. The dual in (2) is the same object used to learn transport maps between domains, to match distributions in representation learning, and to define Wasserstein autoencoders. The critic is the Kantorovich potential, and its gradient is the transport direction.

    Inverse problems and physics. A Wasserstein critic is a loss for matching distributions of fields, particles or spectra where per-sample losses are meaningless — for example matching the distribution of simulated turbulence to measurements. The Lipschitz constraint is what makes the loss a metric rather than a classifier's confidence.

    Evaluating and compressing generators. Distances between generated and real feature distributions (FID and its relatives) are Wasserstein-2-like quantities computed in a fixed feature space; WGAN-style critics are the trainable version of the same idea. GAN compression and distillation use a critic as the teacher's signal for the same reason.

    Anywhere a classifier saturates. Setting up a GAN on a new modality is easier when the loss is a distance: the failure mode is "the critic is not Lipschitz enough", which has a diagnostic (the slope), rather than "the discriminator is too good", which has none. That is the practical argument for the Wasserstein objective even when a plain GAN would train.
