    14 · Generative models/tier 1/6 variants/source

    Diffusion models — a fixed noising process and a learned reverse

    A diffusion model is a denoiser run backwards. The forward process is not learned: it adds Gaussian noise on a fixed schedule until the data is indistinguishable from a standard normal. The reverse is learned, one small denoising step at a time, and sampling is that loop run from noise. There is no adversary, no encoder and no likelihood network — only regression on noise, which is why diffusion trains more reliably than a GAN and samples more slowly than a VAE. This file runs the process on eight Gaussians whose circle is isometrically embedded in eight dimensions, so that the difference between the ambient space and the data's own two-dimensional plane can be measured rather than asserted.

    Intuition #

    Fix a schedule \(\beta_1,\dots,\beta_T\) with \(\beta_t\) small. One forward step shrinks the data by \(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\) and adds noise of variance \(\beta_t\):

    flowchart LR
    X0["x0 (data)"] -->|"add noise"| X1["x1"] -->|"..."| XT["xT ~ N(0, I)"]
    XT -->|"denoise"| XT1["xT-1"] -->|"..."| X0b["x0 (sample)"]

    The forward chain has a closed form: after \(t\) steps the point is \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon\) with \(\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s\le t}(1-\beta_s)\). At \(t=T\) the signal fraction \(\bar\alpha_T\) is tiny — \(4\times10^{-5}\) for the 1000-step schedule here — so \(x_T\) is essentially noise and can be replaced by a standard normal draw without measurable loss.

    The reverse step is the hard direction, and it is learned. The model is shown \((x_t,t)\) and asked to predict the noise \(\epsilon\) that was added. That target is available for free during training — draw a clean point, draw a noise level, draw the noise, and the label is the noise you drew. The model never has to imagine a distribution; it has to regress a vector the training loop can see.

    Everything else follows from that. Sampling starts at \(x_T\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) and applies the model's estimate of \(\epsilon\) at each step, subtracting a fraction of it and adding a smaller amount of fresh noise. Each step is a denoising; the composition is a sampler. Two consequences are worth stating at the start:

    • The model must be told \(t\). One network is asked to denoise at every noise level from nearly clean to nearly pure noise. Without a time embedding it can only learn the average, which is a blur.
    • The chain is sequential. Sampling costs \(T\) forward passes and cannot be parallelised across steps. That is the price of the reliability; DDIM and distillation are the ways to pay less.

    The three axes this file varies are the schedule (where the noise is placed), the target (\(\epsilon\) or the velocity \(v\)), and the space (ambient or a learned latent). They are independent, and the demo arranges the six variants as three controlled pairs so each axis is isolated.

    Variants #

    variant T schedule target space distance
    ddpm 1000 linear \(10^{-4}\)–\(0.02\) \(\epsilon\) ambient 0.308
    linear_schedule 200 linear \(10^{-3}\)–\(0.05\) \(\epsilon\) ambient 0.280
    cosine_schedule 200 cosine \(\epsilon\) ambient 0.254
    epsilon_prediction 100 cosine \(\epsilon\) ambient 0.241
    v_prediction 100 cosine \(v\) ambient 0.334
    latent_diffusion 100 cosine \(v\) 2-D latent 0.175

    ddpm — the reference configuration (Ho, Jain & Abbeel 2020): 1000 steps, the linear \(\beta\) schedule from \(10^{-4}\) to \(0.02\), \(\epsilon\)-prediction, the ancestral sampler. It has the best per-step denoising in the table (0.0019 mean squared error at \(t=10\)) because a thousand rungs make each step tiny, and the worst sample distance (0.308) because those thousand steps also compound a thousand small errors — and 3000 updates is a thin budget for a thousand-rung chain.

    linear_schedule — the same schedule rescaled to 200 steps, from \(10^{-3}\) to \(0.05\). The rescaling is the point: the paper's numbers assume 1000 steps, and using them with 200 leaves \(\bar\alpha_T=0.13\), so \(x_T\) still contains an eighth of the data's signal and the sampler starts from the wrong distribution. With the rescaled schedule \(\bar\alpha_T=5.6\times10^{-3}\) and the distance improves from 0.308 to 0.280.

    cosine_schedule — the cosine schedule of Nichol & Dhariwal (2021), which places \(\bar\alpha_t\) on a cosine curve instead of letting it decay linearly in \(t\). It keeps more signal in the early steps and destroys it faster at the end: \(\bar\alpha_T=6.1\times10^{-8}\) against \(5.6\times10^{-3}\), and the midpoint \(\bar\alpha_t=0.5\) lands exactly at \(t=T/2\) by construction, against \(t=72\) of 200 for the linear one. Same cost, same sampler, distance 0.254.

    epsilon_prediction — \(\epsilon\)-prediction at 100 steps on the cosine schedule. It is the bridge between the schedule ladder and the target ladder: it is the 200-step cosine model with half the steps, and it has the best ambient sample distance (0.241). Fewer steps does not hurt here, because 100 updates per sample is already generous for a two-dimensional manifold.

    v_prediction — the same 100 steps and schedule, predicting the velocity \(v=\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,\epsilon-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}\,x_0\) instead of the noise (Salimans & Ho 2022). Its samples are slightly worse (0.334), and its point is not sample quality: at the last rung its implied clean point has error 0.340, against 78,097 for \(\epsilon\)-prediction, because \(v\) never divides by \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_T}\). Anything that needs \(\hat x_0\) — clipping, guidance, deterministic samplers — inherits that difference.

    latent_diffusion — \(v\)-prediction at 100 steps in the two-dimensional code \(z=xB^{\mathsf T}\), decoded by \(zB\) (Rombach et al. 2022). The embedding is isometric, so the round trip is exact (\(1.3\times10^{-33}\)) and the encoder costs nothing; the model is 4738 parameters against 5512, and its samples land 0.175 from the nearest centre against 0.334. The ambient model has to spend part of every step re-learning which six directions carry no signal; the latent model never sees them.

    Derivation #

    The forward process #

    One step is a Gaussian kernel that shrinks and noises:

    \[q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})=\mathcal N\bigl(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1},\,\beta_t I\bigr). \qquad (1)\]

    Composing \(t\) of them gives the closed form used in training:

    \[q(x_t\mid x_0)=\mathcal N\bigl(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\, (1-\bar\alpha_t)I\bigr), \qquad x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon, \qquad (2)\]

    with \(\alpha_t=1-\beta_t\) and \(\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s\le t}\alpha_s\). The composition is exact because a sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian; no approximation is involved in the forward direction at all.

    The reverse is Gaussian too, given the clean point #

    The true reverse \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\) is not Gaussian. But conditioned on \(x_0\) it is, by Bayes' rule for Gaussians:

    \[q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)=\mathcal N\bigl(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0),\, \tilde\beta_t I\bigr), \qquad \tilde\beta_t=\frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\beta_t, \qquad (3)\]
    \[\tilde\mu_t=\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 +\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t .\]

    This is the quantity the network is asked to imitate. Since the mean is affine in \(x_0\), and \(x_0\) can be written in terms of \(x_t\) and \(\epsilon\) by (2), predicting \(\epsilon\) is the same as predicting the posterior mean: the two differ by a fixed affine map at each \(t\).

    The ELBO #

    Maximise a variational bound on the data likelihood, with the forward chain as the variational distribution:

    \[\mathcal L=\mathbb E_q\Bigl[-\log p(x_T) -\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)}{q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})}\Bigr]. \qquad (4)\]

    Rearranging into a prior term, a sum of per-step KLs and a decoder term:

    \[\mathcal L=\mathcal L_T+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\mathcal L_{t-1}+\mathcal L_0, \qquad \mathcal L_{t-1}=\mathrm{KL}\bigl(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\,\|\, p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\bigr). \qquad (5)\]

    \(\mathcal L_T\) has no parameters — the forward chain is fixed and \(p(x_T)\) is standard normal. Take \(p_\theta\) to be Gaussian with the posterior variance \(\tilde\beta_t\) and the network's mean, and the KL becomes a scaled squared error. Substituting the network's \(\epsilon\)-prediction for its mean:

    \[\mathcal L_{t-1}=\mathbb E\Bigl[ \frac{\beta_t}{2\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})} \bigl\|\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\bigr\|^2\Bigr]+C . \qquad (6)\]

    For the 1000-step linear schedule the coefficient runs from 0.600 at \(t=2\) down to 0.0102 at \(t=T\): the bound weights the near-clean steps sixty times more heavily than the noisiest one. That is statistically correct — a small error at low noise is very visible — and it is not what produces good samples.

    The simplified objective #

    DDPM drops the coefficient:

    \[\mathcal L_{\mathrm{simple}}=\mathbb E_{t,x_0,\epsilon}\Bigl[ \bigl\|\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta\bigl( \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,\,t\bigr)\bigr\|^2 \Bigr], \qquad (7)\]

    with \(t\) uniform over \(1,\dots,T\). This is the objective every implementation actually minimises, and it is a reweighting of the bound toward high noise, not an approximation to it. The demo trains on (7) everywhere; the difference from the bound is why the reported loss is a mean squared error and not a log-likelihood.

    Velocity prediction #

    The \(\epsilon\)-target is nearly the input at high noise: \(x_t\) is dominated by \(\epsilon\), so predicting \(\epsilon\) means "copy \(x_t\) and correct a little". The velocity target rotates the problem so that both components stay order one:

    \[v=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0, \qquad (8)\]

    and, inverting (2) and (8),

    \[x_0=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,v, \qquad \epsilon=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,v+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t . \qquad (9)\]

    The reason to care is conditioning. Recovering the clean point from an \(\epsilon\)-prediction divides by the signal fraction:

    \[\frac{\partial\hat x_0}{\partial\hat\epsilon} =-\frac{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}},\qquad \bigl\|\delta\hat x_0\bigr\|= \sqrt{\frac{1-\bar\alpha_t}{\bar\alpha_t}}\;\|\delta\epsilon\|. \qquad (10)\]

    For the cosine schedule at \(T=200\), \(\bar\alpha_T=6.1\times10^{-8}\), so the amplification is 4058: a noise estimate off by half a unit becomes a clean-point estimate off by two thousand. The demo measures exactly this: the implied \(\hat x_0\) has mean squared error \(328{,}000\) at the last rung under \(\epsilon\)-prediction and \(0.340\) under \(v\)-prediction. With \(v\) the map in (9) has coefficients at most one and no division.

    The ancestral update #

    Given \(\hat\epsilon\), the posterior mean (3) becomes

    \[x_{t-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Bigl( x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\hat\epsilon\Bigr) +\sigma_t z, \qquad \sigma_t^2=\tilde\beta_t, \qquad (11)\]

    with \(z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) for \(t>1\) and no noise at \(t=1\). If \(\hat\epsilon\) is the true noise and no noise is injected, (11) is exact: substituting \(x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\epsilon\) gives \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\epsilon\), the same functional form one rung down. Iterating from \(x_T\) therefore returns \(x_0\) with no error at all. The demo runs that oracle through the real update and gets a maximum deviation of \(0.0\): the arithmetic of the sampler is exact, so every error in a real sample belongs to the model.

    Schedules #

    The linear schedule interpolates the per-step variance:

    \[\beta_t=\beta_1+\frac{t-1}{T-1}\bigl(\beta_T-\beta_1\bigr). \qquad (12)\]

    The cosine schedule instead places the cumulative signal on a cosine:

    \[\bar\alpha_t=\frac{\cos^2\bigl(\frac{t/T+s}{1+s}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\bigr)} {\cos^2\bigl(\frac{s}{1+s}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\bigr)}, \qquad s=0.008, \qquad (13)\]

    and recovers \(\beta_t=1-\bar\alpha_t/\bar\alpha_{t-1}\), clipped at \(0.999\). The \(s\) offset keeps \(\beta_t\) from vanishing at \(t=0\). Because \(\cos^2(\pi/4)=\tfrac12\), this schedule is defined so that half the signal is gone at the midpoint: the demo's crossover step is 100 of 200, against 72 for the linear one.

    Latent diffusion #

    Let \(B\) be a \(2\times8\) matrix with orthonormal rows, and make every data point \(B^{\mathsf T}\) times a point on the circle. Then

    \[z=Bx\in\mathbb R^2, \qquad \hat x=B^{\mathsf T}z, \qquad \hat x=B^{\mathsf T}Bx=x \qquad (14)\]

    for every point in the data's plane, so the autoencoder is exact here. Diffusion runs on \(z\); the sampler decodes once at the end. The point is not compression for its own sake — it is that the model is never asked to represent the six directions in which the data has no variance.

    Code walkthrough #

    The data: a circle in two dimensions, lifted into eight by an isometric basis. encode is a projection and decode its transpose, and on this data the two compose to the identity.

    def _basis() -> np.ndarray:
    """The (2, 8) matrix whose rows span the data's plane."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(BASIS_SEED)
    q, _ = np.linalg.qr(rng.normal(size=(DIM, LATENT)))
    return q.T

    def encode(x: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Project ambient points onto the two-dimensional data plane."""
    return np.asarray(x, dtype=float) @ BASIS.T

    def decode(z: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Lift latent codes back into the ambient space, exactly."""
    return np.asarray(z, dtype=float) @ BASIS

    The schedule is a pure function of the step count and the kind, so the two schedules are compared on identical arithmetic. The linear one is rescaled for short chains; the cosine one is inverted from a cosine curve and clipped.

    def make_schedule(steps: int, kind: str) -> tuple:
    """Return (beta, alpha, abar), each of length `steps`.

    `linear` interpolates the per-step variance from LINEAR_LO to LINEAR_HI;
    `cosine` places the cumulative signal on a cosine curve (Nichol & Dhariwal
    2021), which keeps more signal at small t and destroys it faster at the end.
    """
    if kind == "linear":
        lo, hi = (LINEAR_LO, LINEAR_HI) if steps >= 500 else (SHORT_LO, SHORT_HI)
        beta = np.linspace(lo, hi, steps)
    elif kind == "cosine":
        t = np.arange(steps + 1) / steps
        f = np.cos((t + COSINE_S) / (1.0 + COSINE_S) * np.pi / 2.0) ** 2
        abar = f / f[0]
        beta = np.clip(1.0 - abar[1:] / abar[:-1], 1e-5, 0.999)
    else:
        raise ValueError(f"unknown schedule {kind!r}")
    alpha = 1.0 - beta
    return beta, alpha, np.cumprod(alpha)

    The network needs the step number, and one scalar is not enough: a single monotone input makes the high-noise regime a thin slice of the domain. Four Fourier features give the model a periodic code in which nearby noise levels are nearby inputs and the two ends of the schedule are far apart.

    def _time_features(t: np.ndarray, steps: int) -> np.ndarray:
    """Four features: sin and cos at one and two cycles over the schedule."""
    phase = (np.asarray(t, dtype=float) / steps) * np.pi
    return np.stack([np.sin(phase), np.cos(phase),
                     np.sin(2.0 * phase), np.cos(2.0 * phase)], axis=1)

    The stack is a two-hidden-layer MLP with SiLU. The last layer is initialised to zero, so the model starts by predicting no noise and learns the scale before it learns the shape.

    def _forward(params: list, x: np.ndarray, t: np.ndarray,
             steps: int) -> tuple:
    """Run the stack; returns (prediction, cache) with one entry per layer."""
    h = np.concatenate([x, _time_features(t, steps)], axis=1)
    cache = []
    last = len(params) - 1
    for i, layer in enumerate(params):
        pre = h @ layer["W"] + layer["b"]
        out = pre if i == last else pre * _sig(pre)
        cache.append((h, pre, out))
        h = out
    return h, cache

    SiLU's derivative is \(\sigma(z)+z\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))\), which is why the backward pass recomputes the sigmoid rather than storing it: the cache is already three arrays per layer, and memory is what limits diffusion models at scale.

    def _backward(params: list, cache: list, dout: np.ndarray) -> list:
    """Return parameter gradients; `dout` carries the 1/(n*d) of a mean."""
    grads = [None] * len(params)
    d = dout
    last = len(params) - 1
    for i in range(last, -1, -1):
        h, pre, out = cache[i]
        if i != last:
            s = _sig(pre)
            d = d * (s + pre * s * (1.0 - s))
        grads[i] = {"W": h.T @ d, "b": d.sum(axis=0)}
        if i:
            d = d @ params[i]["W"].T
    return grads

    The two parameterisations are one branch each. _target builds the regression label during training; _split inverts a prediction at sampling time.

    def _target(x0: np.ndarray, eps: np.ndarray, abar: np.ndarray,
            kind: str) -> np.ndarray:
    """The regression target at the batch's own noise levels."""
    if kind == "eps":
        return eps
    return np.sqrt(abar) * eps - np.sqrt(1.0 - abar) * x0

    def _split(out: np.ndarray, xt: np.ndarray, abar: np.ndarray,
           kind: str) -> tuple:
    """Turn a prediction into (x_hat_0, eps_hat) at one noise level."""
    if kind == "eps":
        eps = out
        return (xt - np.sqrt(1.0 - abar) * out) / np.sqrt(abar), eps
    x0 = np.sqrt(abar) * xt - np.sqrt(1.0 - abar) * out
    eps = np.sqrt(abar) * out + np.sqrt(1.0 - abar) * xt
    return x0, eps

    Training samples one noise level per point, not a trajectory: the forward chain is simulated in closed form by (2), so a step costs one forward and one backward pass regardless of \(T\). The abar broadcast is the only place the schedule enters the loop.

            for _ in range(self.train_steps):
            idx = rng.integers(0, len(z), self.batch)

            out, cache = _forward(params, xt, t, self.steps)

            opt.step(params, _backward(params, cache, dout))

    Sampling is the same network called \(T\) times, with the noise level decreasing by one each call. The model's prediction is converted to \(\hat\epsilon\) by _split, which is the only line that differs between the two targets.

            x = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, (n, self.dim))
        for t in range(self.steps, 0, -1):
            out, _ = _forward(self.params, x, np.full(n, t), self.steps)
            _, eps = _split(out, x, self.abar[t - 1], self.target)
            noise = (rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, (n, self.dim))
                     if t > 1 else None)
            x = self._step(x, t, eps, noise)

    The update itself is written once and used by both the sampler and the oracle check. noise=None makes it deterministic, which is how the demo proves the arithmetic: fed the true noise, the loop must return the point it started from.

        def _step(self, x: np.ndarray, t: int, eps: np.ndarray,
              noise: np.ndarray | None) -> np.ndarray:
        """One ancestral update; `noise=None` makes it deterministic."""
        abar = self.abar[t - 1]
        mean = (x - self.beta[t - 1] / np.sqrt(1.0 - abar) * eps)
        mean = mean / np.sqrt(self.alpha[t - 1])
        if noise is None or t == 1:
            return mean
        var = self.beta[t - 1] * (1.0 - self.abar[t - 2]) / (1.0 - abar)
        return mean + np.sqrt(var) * noise

    The diagnostic projects every model's implied clean point onto the data's plane before scoring it. Without that projection the ambient model would be flattered by six coordinates whose true value is zero, and the comparison with the latent model would be meaningless.

    Complexity #

    The ambient model has 5512 parameters and the latent one 4738: the input is 12 numbers (8 ambient plus 4 time features) against 6, and the output 8 against 2, so the saving is confined to the first and last layers — 14% here, and more when the ambient dimension is large. The hidden layers, which dominate both counts, are identical.

    A training step is one forward and one backward pass on a batch of 256, about 4M multiply-adds, and 3000 steps is roughly 13G. Sampling is \(T\) forward passes; at 1024 draws that is 5.6G for the 1000-step model and 0.56G for a 100-step one, so the reference configuration is ten times the sampling cost of the short ones for a slightly worse distance. The sequential loop is the bottleneck no batched implementation can remove; it is why step distillation and DDIM exist.

    Memory is the parameter count plus the activations of one batch per layer, doubled for Adam. Nothing here needs checkpointing; a 512-channel image model at 256×256 does, and the same code shape applies.

    Failure modes #

    \(\bar\alpha_T\) that is not small enough. The linear schedule's constants are tied to \(T=1000\). Run them at 200 steps and \(\bar\alpha_T=0.56\): \(x_T\) still carries more than half the signal, the sampler starts from a distribution that is not noise, and no amount of training fixes it. The check is one number, and the rescaling is one line.

    \(\epsilon\)-prediction's implied clean point is ill-conditioned at high noise. The amplification is \(\sqrt{(1-\bar\alpha_T)/\bar\alpha_T}\) — 4058 for the cosine schedule at 200 steps. The sampler is safe because it uses \(\hat\epsilon\), not \(\hat x_0\); everything downstream that needs the clean point (clipping, guidance, inversion) is not. The demo's 328,000 against 0.340 is that difference in one row.

    More steps are not free quality. The 1000-step model has the best per-step denoising (0.0019 at \(t=10\)) and the worst sample distance (0.308) under a fixed update budget, because a thousand rungs need more than 3000 updates to learn. Steps, capacity and training budget trade against each other; the schedule is not a dial that improves monotonically.

    The time embedding is not optional. Without \(t\) the network sees a mixture of all noise levels and can only return their average, which samples as a blur. A single scalar input is enough in principle and thin in practice; Fourier features are the cheap fix. The related bug is an off-by-one between \(t\) and the array index: this file stores \(\bar\alpha_{t}\) at abar[t - 1] and reads abar[t - 2] for the previous rung, and the oracle check is what catches a mistake there.

    An ELBO weight that is not a good training weight. For the linear schedule the bound's coefficient falls from 0.600 at \(t=2\) to 0.0102 at \(T\); for the cosine schedule the clipped last step \(\beta_T\approx1\) makes its coefficient about 500, so the weighted bound is dominated by the final rung. The simplified objective drops all of this on purpose. Reporting "the ELBO" from a model trained on the simplified loss is a category error.

    A lossy encoder caps the sampler. Latent diffusion is only as good as the autoencoder: here the round trip is exact because the data lies in the encoded plane, so the latent model has no reconstruction error to inherit. A convolutional autoencoder at image scale does not have that property, and its reconstruction artefacts are the floor under every sample.

    Mode collapse is not the failure to watch for. All six variants cover all eight modes; diffusion's failure mode is distance, off-manifold mass and over-smoothing, not a generator that picks one mode. A coverage table alone would call every row a success.

    Evaluating with the loss at \(t=T\). The implied clean point at maximum noise is meaningless for \(\epsilon\)-prediction by construction (it divides by \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_T}\)), so a large number there is arithmetic, not a bad model. The meaningful diagnostics are the error at low and middle noise and the distribution of final samples.

    Applications #

    Image, audio and video generation. The reason the field moved: DALL·E 2, Imagen and Stable Diffusion are diffusion models (with a text conditioner), and audio and video followed the same recipe. The training stability relative to GANs is the main reason.

    Conditional restoration. Super-resolution, inpainting, deblurring and colouring are diffusion with the known pixels clamped at every step. The same ancestral loop runs, with the observed region overwritten between steps; no retraining is needed for a new mask.

    Science and engineering. Molecular conformation, protein backbone design and weather downscaling all use the same denoiser-plus-sampler template, often with equivariance built into the network. The data's structure lives in the architecture; the process is unchanged.

    Planning and control. A trajectory is a point in a high-dimensional space, and a diffusion model over trajectories is a policy (Diffuser and successors). The classifier or cost function enters as a guidance term on \(\hat x_0\), which is exactly where the conditioning problem from the failure section bites.

    As a sampler for an unnormalised density. The score-based reading (Song & Ermon) makes diffusion a way to sample from \(p(x)\propto e^{-U(x)}\) by estimating \(\nabla\log p\) at every noise level. The \(\epsilon\)-network and the score network differ by a factor of \(\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\).

    Latent diffusion as the default. For anything larger than a toy, running the process in a compressed space is the difference between feasible and not: Stable Diffusion's autoencoder reduces 512×512×3 to 64×64×4 before the chain starts, a 48-fold reduction in the sequence the model has to denoise.
