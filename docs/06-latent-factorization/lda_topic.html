    06 · Latent structure & factorisation/tier 2/4 variants/source

    Latent Dirichlet allocation

    Intuition #

    A document is a bag of words. LSA takes that literally and decomposes the bag. LDA asks a different question — not how do I factor this matrix but what process would produce it — and the answer it proposes is the smallest one that works:

       α ──▶ θ_d ──▶ z_dn ──▶ w_dn ◀── β_k
         ▲        ▲                 ▲
         │        └── one topic per token
         └── a document is a mixture over topics
                          K topics, one β_k each

   for each document d:
       θ_d ~ Dirichlet(α, …, α)              the mixture
       for each of the N_d words:
           z_dn ~ Categorical(θ_d)           pick a topic
           w_dn ~ Categorical(β_{z_dn})      draw a word from it

    That is the whole model: two Dirichlet priors and two categorical draws. There is no matrix in the generative story at all. The counts appear only because the generator is run many times and then histogrammed, and the histogram is what a corpus gives you:

    \[x_{dw} \;=\; \#\{n : w_{dn} = w\}. \qquad (1)\]

    The consequences of taking the story seriously are not cosmetic.

    It explains the zeros. A term with count zero in a document is not "a small value"; it is a term the document's mixture did not generate. LSA and NMF treat the same zero as a small residual, and both are free to fill it in with whatever the low-rank structure prefers. LDA cannot: a topic that puts mass on a term contributes to that term's rate in every document that uses the topic.

    It makes the factors probabilities. A row of components_ is a distribution over the vocabulary, so it sums to one and its entries are rates. That is why topic printouts are readable — "the 15 most probable words" is a meaningful summary — and why doc_topic_ rows are mixtures, not loadings.

    It separates the two things a corpus has. Document length is \(N_d\), drawn once and then fixed; the topic mixture is \(\theta_d\). LSA's weighting has to fight document length because it is baked into the counts. LDA conditions on it, and a 2 000-word document and a 200-word one with the same mixture get the same \(\theta\).

    The price is that the likelihood is a product over tokens of sums over topics, which has no closed-form posterior. Everything in this file is a way of paying that price, and the first three variants differ only in how:

    what is left to infer diagnostic measured
    collapsed_gibbs the token assignments \(z\) \(\log p(w,z)\) exact in the limit, no bound
    variational_bayes \(q(\theta)q(\beta)q(z)\) the ELBO monotone, \(+4\,270\) nats
    online_lda the same \(q\), one batch at a time — 0.996 to the batch fit
    seeded_lda \(z\), with a prior on some words \(\log p(w,z)\) labelling becomes identity

    On the demo corpus all three unsupervised fitters recover the same three topics to a cosine of \(0.99\) against the parameters that generated the data, and to \(1.00\) against each other (after matching the labelling, which no fit can determine). The fitter does not change the answer. It changes the cost, the diagnostic, and the amount of data you need in memory — and seeded_lda changes the information, which is a different kind of change.

    Variants #

    Variant What it does Cost Where it wins
    collapsed_gibbs sample each token's topic from its full conditional \(O(NK)\) per sweep exact, no step size, no bound to trust
    variational_bayes coordinate ascent on a mean-field bound \(O(DKV)\) per iteration fast, deterministic, has an objective
    online_lda the same, with a minibatch and a decaying step \(O(BKV)\) per step data larger than memory, streams
    seeded_lda collapsed Gibbs with seed words carrying extra prior mass \(O(NK)\) per sweep labelled topics, faster convergence

    collapsed_gibbs integrates \(\theta\) and \(\beta\) out analytically and samples only the \(N\) token assignments. It is exact — the chain's stationary distribution is the posterior — and it is the variant everyone means by "LDA with Gibbs". Its weakness is that it has no objective: \(\log p(w,z)\) for a single sample of \(z\) is not a bound and not monotone, and the demo's trace shows it settling into a band about 80 nats wide and staying there forever. That is correct behaviour, not a bug, and it is the main thing practitioners misdiagnose as non-convergence.

    variational_bayes replaces each posterior with a factorised \(q\) and ascends the resulting bound. Every step is exact given the others, so the ELBO increases at every iteration — the demo asserts it, and the smallest observed step is \(0.001\). It is deterministic given a seed, it converges in tens of iterations here rather than hundreds of sweeps, and at the end you get a number you can compare across models. What you do not get is the posterior: the mean-field family is too narrow, and the bound can sit well below the evidence.

    online_lda is variational_bayes with two changes: the global parameter \(\lambda\) is updated by a natural-gradient step rather than set, and the local step runs on a minibatch. The demo makes the step size do visible work. Feed the batches in topical order — one class of documents at a time, which is what a stream looks like — and with \(\rho_t = (\tau_0+t)^{-0.7}\) the fit lands \(0.996\) cosine from batch VB; with \(\kappa = 0\), so \(\rho_t = 1\) and the estimate is whatever the last batch said, it lands \(0.687\). Nothing about the model changed. The averaging is the algorithm.

    seeded_lda adds a third Dirichlet parameter: for a token whose word is a seed for topic \(k\), the topic-\(k\) concentration is \(\alpha + s\) instead of \(\alpha\). It fixes the one quantity the likelihood cannot determine — which topic is called topic 1 — and it converges faster. On five topics the unseeded fit returns the topics in the order \((0,2,1,3,4)\) at cosine \(0.98\); with seeds, the order is the identity, and five sweeps reach \(0.94\) where the unseeded fit reaches \(0.84\). Seeds are not free, and the demo also measures the bill: at 120 sweeps the seeded fit is \(0.95\) against the unseeded \(0.98\), because a seed word is every occurrence of that word, and the prior keeps re-asserting them.

    Derivation #

    1. The likelihood, and where the count matrix comes from #

    With \(\beta\) the \(K \times V\) topic-word matrix, one document of length \(N_d\) has likelihood

    \[p(w_d \mid \theta_d, \beta) \;=\; \prod_{n=1}^{N_d} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \theta_{dk}\,\beta_{k w_{dn}}, \qquad (2)\]

    a product of mixtures — this is exactly the extra sum that LSA does not have. Grouping tokens by type turns the product over positions into a product over the vocabulary:

    \[p(w_d \mid \theta_d, \beta) \;=\; \prod_{w=1}^{V} \left(\sum_{k} \theta_{dk}\beta_{kw}\right)^{x_{dw}}. \qquad (3)\]

    So the count matrix is a sufficient statistic for the whole corpus — this is why LDA takes the same input as LSA and NMF — but (3) is not a low-rank approximation of \(X\), it is a product of \(V\) sums per document. The log of it is what the perplexity in the demo measures.

    2. Collapsing: Dirichlet–multinomial conjugacy #

    The trick that makes Gibbs cheap is that \(\theta\) and \(\beta\) can be integrated out in closed form. For one document, with a \(\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha)\) prior on \(\theta\),

    \[\int p(z_d \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)\, d\theta \;=\; \frac{\Gamma(K\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)^{K}}\, \frac{\prod_k \Gamma(n_{dk}+\alpha)}{\Gamma(N_d + K\alpha)}, \qquad (4)\]

    where \(n_{dk}\) counts the tokens of document \(d\) assigned to topic \(k\). That is the Dirichlet-multinomial, and the same identity with \(\beta\) and the topic counts \(n_{kw}\), \(n_k\) gives \(p(w \mid z)\). Multiplying,

    \[p(w, z) \;=\; \underbrace{\prod_d \frac{\Gamma(K\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)^K}\frac{\prod_k \Gamma(n_{dk}+\alpha)}{\Gamma(N_d+K\alpha)}}_{p(z)} \cdot \underbrace{\prod_k \frac{\Gamma(V\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)^V}\frac{\prod_w \Gamma(n_{kw}+\beta)}{\Gamma(n_k+V\beta)}}_{p(w\mid z)}. \qquad (5)\]

    Every quantity in (5) is a count. That is the whole reason the sampler is four lines of arithmetic: no \(\theta\), no \(\beta\), no gradient.

    The demo computes (5) directly — _collapsed_loglik is a transcription of it — and the resulting trace is the honest picture of MCMC: it starts at \(-17\,098\) with a random assignment, jumps to about \(-12\,000\) within ten sweeps, and then fluctuates in a band of roughly \(\pm 40\) nats forever. It never becomes monotone, because each recorded value is one draw from the posterior, not an average.

    3. The full conditional #

    Subtracting token \(n\) (currently on topic \(k_0\)) from the counts and applying (5) to the two terms that mention it leaves one ratio of Gammas, which collapses by \(\Gamma(x+1) = x\,\Gamma(x)\):

    \[p(z_n = k \mid z_{-n}, w) \;\propto\; \underbrace{\frac{n_{dk}^{-n} + \alpha}{N_d - 1 + K\alpha}}_{\text{this document likes } k} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{n_{kw}^{-n} + \beta}{n_k^{-n} + V\beta}}_{\text{topic } k \text{ likes this word}}, \qquad (6)\]

    where the superscript \(-n\) means the counts with token \(n\) removed. Both factors are what they look like: the first is how much of the document is already on topic \(k\) (with the prior as a pseudo-count), the second is how much of topic \(k\) is already this word.

    Two features are worth naming because they are the model, not an implementation detail.

    The normaliser \(n_k + V\beta\) is not cosmetic. It penalises large topics: a topic with many tokens pays more for each new one. Without it the sampler would be a rich-get-richer process with no ceiling, and all tokens would collapse into one topic. With \(\beta\) small the penalty is mild and topics grow; with \(\beta\) large the penalty dominates and every topic stays near-uniform.

    The prior is a pseudo-count, so "small \(\alpha\)" means "sparse documents". \(\alpha = 0.1\) puts \(0.1\) of mass on each topic before any token is seen; a 30-token document is then a mixture of one or two topics, not an even blend of three. That is the knob that makes \(n_{dk}+\alpha\) behave like a smoothed count ratio rather than a real one, and choosing it is a modelling decision about how mixed you believe documents are.

    4. Seeds as a prior, not a constraint #

    seeded_lda adds \(s\) to the concentration of the topic a word seeds:

    \[p(z_n = k \mid z_{-n}, w) \;\propto\; \frac{n_{dk}^{-n} + \alpha + s\,\mathbb{1}[k = \mathrm{seed}(w_n)]}{N_d - 1 + K\alpha + s\,\mathbb{1}[\mathrm{seed}(w_n) \ne \varnothing]} \cdot \frac{n_{kw}^{-n}+\beta}{n_k^{-n}+V\beta}. \qquad (7)\]

    This is a genuine Dirichlet prior — the model with seeds is LDA with a word-dependent \(\alpha\) — which is why it does not have the pathologies of a hard clamp. A clamped token cannot move even when the rest of the document overwhelmingly disagrees, so a wrong seed becomes an unfixable error and the topic it was pinned to absorbs every occurrence of that word. A seeded token can move, it just needs more evidence. The demo shows the difference in the numbers: the strength is \(s = 2\) by default, and raising it to \(10\) changes the result by less than \(0.002\) on this corpus — the prior saturates, it does not tighten indefinitely, because once a topic is at \(99\%\) the extra mass is irrelevant.

    The measure that makes seeds legible is coverage. The demo's five topics have two seed words each out of fifty, and those ten words account for \(1\,168\) of the \(2\,400\) tokens — 49% of the corpus. Seed words are the frequent ones, so "two seed words per topic" is not a small annotation; it is a prior on half the data. That is why five sweeps improve from \(0.84\) to \(0.94\), and also why the converged fit loses \(0.03\) of cosine: the repeated evidence is being counted twice.

    5. Mean field, and why the updates are exact #

    Write the variational family as

    \[q(\theta, \beta, z) \;=\; \prod_d q(\theta_d) \prod_k q(\beta_k) \prod_{d,n} q(z_{dn}), \qquad (8)\]

    with \(q(\theta_d) = \mathrm{Dir}(\gamma_d)\), \(q(\beta_k) = \mathrm{Dir}(\lambda_k)\) and \(q(z_{dn}) = \mathrm{Cat}(\phi_{dn})\). Coordinate ascent on the ELBO gives, for each coordinate, the exponentiated expectation of the log joint over the others. For \(z\),

    \[\phi_{dnk} \;\propto\; \exp\left\{ \mathbb{E}[\log \theta_{dk}] + \mathbb{E}[\log \beta_{kw_{dn}}] \right\}, \qquad (9)\]

    and for the two Dirichlets, the same Dirichlet-multinomial form as (4):

    \[\gamma_{dk} = \alpha + \sum_n \phi_{dnk}, \qquad \lambda_{kw} = \beta + \sum_d x_{dw}\,\phi_{dkw}. \qquad (10)\]

    The expectations are digammas: \(\mathbb{E}[\log \theta_{dk}] = \Psi(\gamma_{dk}) - \Psi(\sum_{k'}\gamma_{dk'})\).

    The one place an implementation goes wrong is (10)'s second equation. The statistic for \(\lambda\) is \(\sum_d x_{dw}\phi_{dkw}\) — a \(K \times V\) expected count of word-topic pairs — not the per-document topic totals, which is the statistic for \(\gamma\). Conflating them is tempting because both are sums of \(\phi\), and it is silent: the fit still converges, and the topic-word matrix comes out at cosine \(0.575\) instead of \(0.989\) because \(\lambda\) was handed document-level totals with no word information in them at all.

    The reason the updates are closed-form at all is the conjugacy of §2: the Dirichlet is the conjugate prior for the categorical, so the optimal \(q\) given the rest is always the same family with updated counts. This is why LDA's CAVI is ten lines, and why a model with a logistic-normal prior instead has to be fitted by gradient methods.

    6. The ELBO, term by term #

    \[\mathcal{L} \;=\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\log p(\theta)] + \mathbb{E}[\log p(\beta)]}_{\text{priors}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathbb{H}[q(\theta)] + \mathbb{H}[q(\beta)] + \mathbb{H}[q(z)]}_{\text{entropies}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\log p(w, z \mid \theta, \beta)]}_{\text{data}}, \qquad (11)\]

    with \(\mathbb{H}[q] = -\mathbb{E}[\log q]\). The middle group is where every implemented ELBO goes wrong, because it is written with a minus sign in the code — - sum((gamma - 1) * Eg) — and expanded, not left as an entropy.

    Written out for the two Dirichlet factors, one document and one topic,

    \[\mathbb{E}[\log p(\theta_d)] = (\alpha - 1)\sum_k \mathbb{E}[\log \theta_{dk}] - \log B(\alpha), \qquad (12)\]
    \[-\mathbb{E}[\log q(\theta_d)] = -\sum_k (\gamma_{dk}-1)\,\mathbb{E}[\log \theta_{dk}] + \log B(\gamma_d), \qquad (13)\]

    with \(B\) the Dirichlet normaliser \(B(a) = \prod_k \Gamma(a_k) / \Gamma(\sum_k a_k)\). The signs in (12) and (13) are the part that is easy to get backwards, and getting them backwards is not a crash: the central term flips sign, the iteration still converges smoothly, and the "bound" decreases monotonically from \(-15\,708\) to \(-11\,437\). The demo asserts steps.min() > -1e-6, which is exactly the assertion that catches it — the symptom of a wrong ELBO is an ELBO that moves the wrong way, and nothing else.

    The data term is where \(\phi\) earns its keep:

    \[\mathbb{E}[\log p(w, z \mid \theta, \beta)] = \sum_{d,w} x_{dw} \sum_k \phi_{dkw}\left(\mathbb{E}[\log \theta_{dk}] + \mathbb{E}[\log \beta_{kw}] - \log \phi_{dkw}\right), \qquad (14)\]

    and the \(-\log\phi\) inside is the \(-\mathbb{E}[\log q(z)]\) term, not a separate one — the two are the same series written once. That is why the code has one loop where the derivation has three terms.

    7. Stochastic VI, and what the step size is for #

    Batch CAVI touches all \(D\) documents per iteration. SVI touches \(B\) and treats the minibatch statistic as an estimate of the full one, rescaled by \(D/B\):

    \[\lambda \;\leftarrow\; (1-\rho_t)\,\lambda + \rho_t\left(\beta + \frac{D}{B}\sum_{d \in \mathcal{B}} \mathbb{E}[n_{kw}^{(d)}]\right), \qquad \rho_t = (\tau_0 + t)^{-\kappa}. \qquad (15)\]

    The rescaling is what makes it unbiased; the step size is what makes it converge. The Robbins–Monro conditions are \(\sum_t \rho_t = \infty\) and \(\sum_t \rho_t^2 < \infty\), which \(\kappa \in (0.5, 1]\) satisfies and \(\kappa = 0\) does not: with \(\rho_t = 1\) the recursion is \(\lambda \leftarrow\) last batch, so the estimate has the variance of a single batch no matter how many batches you have seen. On i.i.d. minibatches that noise is harmless — every batch is a draw from the same distribution — and the demo's sorted stream is the case where it is not: the last 16 documents are one topic, and \(\lambda\) remembers exactly that. \(0.996\) against \(0.687\) is the same algorithm with one constant changed.

    The order dependence is a real design decision, not an oversight. online_lda consumes rows in the order you give them and cycles; it does not shuffle. A caller with i.i.d. data can shuffle first, and a caller with a stream cannot — and it is the caller who knows which they have.

    8. What the likelihood cannot tell you #

    For any permutation \(\pi\) of the topics, substituting \(\theta_{d\pi(k)}\), \(\beta_{\pi(k)w}\) for the originals leaves (2) unchanged. The posterior therefore has \(K!\) equivalent modes, and a fit returns one of them according to where the sampler or the initialisation happened to land. This is not a numerical artefact: it is a symmetry of the model, and no amount of data removes it.

    The demo shows both directions. Permuting components_ and the matching lam_ of a fitted VB model leaves the held-out perplexity bit-identical — measured difference \(0.0\), because everything downstream is a function of \(\theta\beta\) and the product is invariant. Permuting a Gibbs fit's components_ changes perplexity by \(0.05\), which looks like evidence against invariance and is not: transform for Gibbs runs a 20-sweep chain per document with its own random numbers, so the difference is Monte Carlo noise, \(0.24\%\) of \(20.5\). The model is invariant; a sampler is not, bit for bit.

    Non-identifiability is why _match_topics exists and why every accuracy number in the demo is computed after a brute-force search over \(K!\) permutations. For \(K \le 8\) that is at most \(40\,320\) permutations and the honest thing to do; any greedy matching is a heuristic that silently fails when two topics are similar. It is also why seeded_lda is worth a variant: seeds are the only mechanism in this file that picks a labelling by construction instead of by convention.

    9. Perplexity, and what the demo's number means #

    \[\mathrm{perplexity}(X) \;=\; \exp\left\{-\frac{\sum_{d,w} x_{dw} \log \hat{p}_{dw}}{\sum_{d,w} x_{dw}}\right\}, \qquad \hat{p}_{dw} = \sum_k \hat\theta_{dk}\hat\beta_{kw}. \qquad (16)\]

    It is the effective vocabulary size the model is choosing between; \(20.5\) against a 40-term vocabulary means the model has narrowed the field by a factor of two.

    The demo's fitted model scores \(20.52\) and the corpus generator scores \(20.57\): the fit beats the model that produced the data. That is not a bug and not evidence of a better model — \(\hat\theta_d\) is inferred from the same document it is then scored on, so a 30-token document gives the inference a chance to overfit itself. The comparison that is meaningful is between models scored the same way, which is why the doc reports Gibbs \(20.52\) against VB \(20.27\) (it is a real difference — VB's tighter fit to this corpus) and not against the oracle.

    Code walkthrough #

    LDA(n_topics=3, *, variant="collapsed_gibbs", alpha=0.1, beta=0.01, n_iter=150, seed=0, tau0=1.0, kappa=0.7, batch_size=16, n_local=5, seed_words=None, seed_strength=2.0) with fit, transform, fit_transform, perplexity, and the attributes components_ \((K, V)\) row-normalised, doc_topic_ \((D, K)\) row-normalised, lam_, elbo_curve_, loglik_curve_.

    components_ is (K, V) for the same reason nmf.py's is: a row is a topic, and theta @ components_ is the fitted rate matrix. doc_topic_ is the training mixture from the last state of the fit, so for Gibbs it is one sample and for VB it is the mean of \(q(\theta_d)\); both are row-normalised, and the demo asserts it.

    1. The count matrix as a token list #

    def _expand(X: np.ndarray) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Count matrix → the flat token list `(document, term)` it represents."""
    n_docs, n_terms = X.shape
    docs = np.repeat(np.arange(n_docs), X.sum(axis=1).astype(np.int64))
    words = np.repeat(np.tile(np.arange(n_terms), n_docs), X.ravel().astype(np.int64))
    return docs, words

    The sampler is written in terms of tokens because (6) is a statement about one token at a time, and because the same code then does not care how the counts are distributed. np.repeat with a counts array is the idiomatic expansion: for each document, its row of counts says how many times to repeat each term id. \(4\,800\) tokens for the demo corpus, and this function is the entire bridge between the matrix representation and the generative one.

    X.ravel() and the two repeats must be in the same order — document-major — which is why words repeats the tiled term ids rather than concatenating documents. Getting this wrong pairs tokens with the wrong document, and the sampler still runs and still produces topics, just worse ones.

    2. The Gibbs update #

                    own = int(owner[n])
                total = 0.0
                p = [0.0] * K
                for k in range(K):
                    a = alpha + (strength if k == own else 0.0)
                    v = (row[k] + a) * (n_kw[k][w] + beta) / (n_k[k] + Vbeta)
                    p[k] = v
                    total += v
                u = uniform() * total
                k1 = K - 1
                for k in range(K):
                    u -= p[k]
                    if u <= 0.0:
                        k1 = k
                        break

    (6) and (7). The counts were decremented before this block and are incremented after it, so every factor is the leave-one-out count the conditional needs. Normalising is skipped deliberately: sampling from an unnormalised vector by scaling one uniform by the total is exact, and it saves a division per token per topic — with \(4\,800\) tokens and 120 sweeps that is \(1.7\) million divisions.

    strength is a local, not self.seed_strength, because this loop runs \(1.7\) million times and an attribute lookup inside it is measurable. own is \(-1\) for every token when not seeding, so the k == own test never fires and the seeded and unseeded samplers are the same code path — one prior term, zeroed out.

    The cumulative-sum sampling is the standard inverse-CDF trick written as a loop rather than np.searchsorted(np.cumsum(p), u): at \(K=3\) the Python loop is faster than building two numpy arrays, and at \(K=100\) the numpy version wins. Neither is the bottleneck; the \(O(NK)\) sweep is.

    3. The collapsed log-likelihood #

            # p(z | α): one Dirichlet–multinomial normaliser per document ...
        ll = n_docs * (math.lgamma(K * a) - K * math.lgamma(a))
        ll += float(np.sum(_lgamma(np.asarray(n_dk, dtype=float) + a)))
        ll -= float(np.sum(_lgamma(np.asarray([sum(row) for row in n_dk]) + K * a)))
        # ... and p(w | z, β): one per topic.
        ll += K * (math.lgamma(V * b) - V * math.lgamma(b))
        ll += float(np.sum(_lgamma(np.asarray(n_kw, dtype=float) + b)))
        ll -= float(np.sum(_lgamma(np.asarray(n_k, dtype=float) + V * b)))

    (5), with the two normalisers per level written out. _lgamma is applied to the whole count array at once, which is why the function is vectorised: the corpus needs \(DK + KV\) of them — one per document-topic and topic-word pair — and a scalar loop over that many math.lgamma calls costs more than the sweep it is measuring.

    Two honest caveats live in this function's docstring and in the trace it produces. It is \(\log p(w, z)\), not \(\log p(w)\) — the assignments are a variable, so it is not a bound and it is not comparable to the ELBO. And it is evaluated every tenth sweep rather than every sweep: \(DK + KV = 480\) log-Gammas is cheap, but not cheap enough to pay for twelve times over when the number it produces moves by \(\pm 40\) nats anyway.

    4. One mean-field E-step #

            Eg = _digamma(gamma) - _digamma(gamma.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True))
        El = _digamma(lam) - _digamma(lam.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True))
        stats = np.zeros((K, V))
        cnt = np.zeros_like(gamma)
        phis = []
        for d in range(X.shape[0]):
            logits = Eg[d][:, None] + El                   # (K, V) per document
            phi = np.exp(logits - _logsumexp(logits, axis=0))
            phis.append(phi)
            per = phi * X[d]
            stats += per
            cnt[d] = per.sum(axis=1)

    (9) and (10). Eg[d][:, None] + El is a \(K \times V\) matrix of the two expected log-probabilities; a softmax down the columns gives \(\phi_{dkw}\) for every word in the document at once, which is why the loop is over documents and not over tokens. per = phi * X[d] then multiplies by the counts, so a word appearing three times contributes three times — the count-matrix shortcut that makes CAVI \(O(DKV)\) rather than \(O(NK)\).

    stats is (10)'s second equation and cnt is its first, accumulated in the same pass. Keeping them separate is the difference between cosine \(0.989\) and \(0.575\); the two are easy to confuse because both are sums of \(\phi\), and only one of them knows which word it came from.

    The _logsumexp shift is not needed at the demo's parameters and belongs in the code anyway. Since \(\Psi(x) \approx -1/x\) for small \(x\), an unseen word in a topic has expected log-probability \(\approx -1/\beta\): \(-100\) at \(\beta = 0.01\), and \(-10\,000\) at \(\beta = 10^{-4}\), which is below the double- precision underflow point of \(-745\). When every exponential in a column flushes to zero, phi is \(0/0\) and stats is NaN, and \(\beta\) is exactly the knob a user is invited to make smaller when they want sparser topics.

    5. The ELBO #

            out = float((a - 1.0) * Eg.sum()) - n_docs * (
            K * math.lgamma(a) - math.lgamma(K * a))
        out += float((b - 1.0) * El.sum()) - K * (
            V * math.lgamma(b) - math.lgamma(V * b))

    (12), twice — once for \(\theta\), once for \(\beta\) — with the Dirichlet normaliser written as \(K\log\Gamma(\alpha) - \log\Gamma(K\alpha) = -\log B(\alpha)\) and multiplied by the number of documents (or topics) that have one. Expanding math.lgamma rather than computing \(B\) is a small clarity win here: the subtraction that has to be right is visible.

    The prior term is where the sign errors live, and the demo's assertion is the only defence. Note what is not here: no \(\log\phi\), no data. Those are the last group, and they are computed in the loop over documents because they are the only part that scales with the counts.

    6. The two closed-form updates #

                stats, cnt, phis, Eg, El = self._vb_step(X, gamma, lam)
            self.elbo_curve_.append(self._elbo(X, gamma, lam, phis, Eg, El))
            gamma = self.alpha + cnt

    (10), and the reason CAVI needs no step size: each coordinate is set to its exact optimum given the others, so the bound cannot decrease. The order of the three lines matters for the diagnostic — the ELBO is recorded for the state that produced phis, i.e. before the update — and recording it after the update instead would compare a state against a \(q\) that was optimised for a different one, which shows up as a small negative step.

    lam = self.beta + stats is not lam += .... It is a full reassignment from the sufficient statistic, which is what makes the iteration converge to a fixed point rather than accumulate: the Dirichlet \(\lambda\) is the only global parameter, and (10) says it is a function of the current \(q(z)\) with no memory.

    7. The step size that does the work #

                rho = (self.tau0 + t) ** (-self.kappa)
            lam = (1.0 - rho) * lam + rho * (self.beta + (n_docs / B) * stats)

    (15). The n_docs / B rescaling is the unbiasedness; the interpolation is the variance reduction. tau0 = 1 shifts the schedule so that \(\rho_0 = 1\) — the first batch is used whole, which is standard and matters because a fresh \(\lambda\) from rng.random is a terrible initialisation and should be overwritten rather than averaged with. kappa = 0.7 is the paper's default and satisfies both Robbins–Monro conditions.

    Above it, self.n_local iterations of the local step run per batch, and each batch's \(\gamma\) starts from \(\alpha + N_d/K\) rather than from the previous batch's — the documents are new, so there is nothing to carry over, and the D/B rescaling in the global step is what supplies the memory. The nested loop reinitialises stats and cnt inside the local loop deliberately: only the last local iteration's statistic corresponds to the \(\gamma\) that is exported.

    8. Inference for a new document #

                z = rng.integers(0, K, size=n)
            n_dk = np.bincount(z, minlength=K).astype(float)
            for _ in range(n_iter):
                for i in range(n):
                    n_dk[z[i]] -= 1
                    p = (n_dk + self.alpha) * phi[:, w[i]]
                    p /= p.sum()
                    k = int(np.searchsorted(np.cumsum(p), rng.random()))
                    k = min(k, K - 1)
                    z[i] = k
                    n_dk[k] += 1

    (6) with \(\beta\) frozen: only the first factor is unknown, so the conditional is (n_dk + alpha) * phi[:, word]. The n_kw term of the full conditional is replaced by the fitted phi, which is the whole point of having fitted it — the document no longer has a vote on what a topic means, only on which topics it uses.

    np.searchsorted(np.cumsum(p), rng.random()) is the inverse-CDF sample in its numpy form; min(k, K - 1) guards the case where the uniform lands above the last cumulative entry by a rounding hair. And the 20 sweeps are per document, on 30 tokens — cheap, and the reason the demo's Gibbs perplexity is \(0.05\) away from being permutation-invariant: a short chain does not fully mix.

    9. Digamma and log-gamma, written out #

        y = np.asarray(x, dtype=float).copy()
    r = np.zeros_like(y)
    small = y < 6.0
    while small.any():                         # Ψ(x) = Ψ(x+1) − 1/x
        r = np.where(small, r - 1.0 / np.where(small, y, 1.0), r)
        y = np.where(small, y + 1.0, y)
        small = y < 6.0
    f = 1.0 / (y * y)
    return r + np.log(y) - 0.5 / y - f * (1 / 12 - f * (1 / 120 - f * (
        1 / 252 - f * (1 / 240 - f / 132))))

    The recurrence pushes every argument up to \(y \ge 6\), where the asymptotic series \(\Psi(y) \approx \log y - \frac{1}{2y} - \sum_n \frac{B_{2n}}{2n y^{2n}}\) is accurate to about \(10^{-10}\) — measured against the true values at \(\Psi(1) = -0.577216\), \(\Psi(0.5) = -1.963510\), \(\Psi(10) = 2.251753\). The Bernoulli coefficients in the bracket are \(1/12, 1/120, 1/252, 1/240, 1/132\): five terms, and the fifth is there because \(\alpha = 0.1\) makes \(\gamma\) close to \(0.1\) often and the series is worse the smaller the argument.

    _lgamma is the same construction with the recurrence \(\Gamma(y) = \Gamma(y+1)/y\) and Stirling's series, accurate to \(2 \times 10^{-14}\) on the values this file uses. Both are vectorised with while small.any() for exactly one reason: the ELBO calls them on whole count arrays, and a scalar loop over \(KV\) entries would cost more than the rest of the fit.

    The np.where(small, y, 1.0) idiom is the safe form — it prevents 1/y from dividing by zero on entries that have already been shifted out of the "small" set, which produces a warning and an inf even though np.where discards it.

    10. Matching the labelling #

        C = (A @ B.T) / np.outer(np.maximum(na, EPS), np.maximum(nb, EPS))

    def score(p):
        return float(sum(C[p[k], k] for k in range(K)))

    best = max(itertools.permutations(range(K)), key=score)
    return score(best) / K, best

    §8, as a function. The cosine matrix is computed once, and then every permutation is a lookup — \(K!\) additions rather than \(K!\) dot products. The returned permutation is reported by the demo rather than hidden, because for the unseeded fit it is the interesting output: (0, 2, 1, 3, 4) says the first two topics came back swapped, which is not an error and not a worse fit.

    np.maximum(., EPS) guards a topic that received no mass at all; with \(\beta = 0.01\) that cannot happen after a fit, but _match_topics is also called on partial states by the demo.

    11. What the estimator refuses #

            if np.isnan(X).any() or (X < 0.0).any():
            raise ValueError("LDA needs non-negative counts")
        if not np.allclose(X, np.round(X)):
            raise ValueError("LDA samples tokens, so the counts must be integers")

    The refusal is the model. \(x_{dw}\) in (5) enters through \(\Gamma(n_{dw} + \beta)\), and a fractional \(x\) would make that a continuous function of a quantity that is supposed to be a count — the sampler has no token to move. tf-idf'd LSA input is rejected here, correctly, and the error message says why rather than leaving a user to discover that their weighted matrix produced a Gamma function of 0.37.

    The integer check is np.allclose against a round, not dtype.kind == "i", because a float array of whole numbers is a perfectly good count matrix and a caller who built it with astype(float) should not be punished. What is rejected is values that are not counts.

    Complexity #

    \(D\) documents, \(V\) terms, \(K\) topics, \(N\) tokens, \(B\) batch size, \(T\) iterations.

    Operation Time Memory Note
    _expand \(O(N + DV)\) \(O(N)\) the token list, once
    _fit_gibbs, per sweep \(O(NK)\) \(O(N + KV)\) counts are sparse lists
    _collapsed_loglik \(O(DK + KV)\) \(O(KV)\) every tenth sweep
    _vb_step \(O(DKV)\) \(O(DKV)\) one \(K \times V\) per document
    _elbo \(O(DKV)\) \(O(1)\) extra reuses the phis the E-step made
    _fit_online, per step \(O(BKV)\) \(O(B K + KV)\) independent of \(D\)
    _infer_gibbs \(O(N_d K)\) per document \(O(N_d)\) 20 sweeps
    perplexity \(O(mKV)\) \(O(mV)\) over the documents you pass

    The interesting comparison is the second row against the fourth. Gibbs is \(O(NK)\) per sweep with \(O(N)\) state, and VB is \(O(DKV)\) per iteration with an \(O(DKV)\) phi. For a corpus of \(30\)-token documents, \(N = 30D\), so a Gibbs sweep touches \(30K\) operations per document and a VB iteration touches \(KV = 40K\): nearly the same arithmetic, and Gibbs needs more passes.

    What decides it in practice is vectorisation, and it decides against Gibbs. A 120-sweep Gibbs fit here takes \(0.48\) s against \(0.13\) s for 80 VB iterations, a factor of \(3.7\), because VB's inner work is three numpy calls on a \(K imes V\) array and Gibbs's is a Python loop over \(4\,800\) tokens per sweep. The asymptotics say they are comparable; the constant says use VB. The whole demo is \(1.9\) s because it runs eight fits.

    The asymptotics do eventually matter, and they matter in Gibbs's favour on long documents: \(N_d\) can be ten thousand tokens, while \(KV\) cannot, and CAVI never revisits a document while Gibbs revisits every token every sweep.

    The online row is the one that matters at scale: per step, \(B\) documents, so the cost is independent of \(D\) once the data is streamed. lam is the only persistent state — \(KV\) floats — which is why online LDA can fit a corpus that does not fit in memory, and why the demo can hold the whole thing in an array and pretend.

    Memory is where a real implementation diverges from this file. _vb_step allocates a \(K \times V\) matrix per document; at \(K = 100\), \(V = 10^5\) that is 80 MB per document, and no corpus survives it. The standard fix is to keep phi implicitly as Eg[d] + El and compute the statistics in the same pass, which is what the loop does not do because the ELBO needs the explicit phis. Trade a factor of two in speed for the bound, or drop the ELBO.

    Failure modes #

    1. \(K\) is not estimated, and neither diagnostic picks it. On this corpus the truth is \(K = 3\), and held-out perplexity says \(K = 6\) and still falling: \(23.14, 20.27, 20.12, 19.96, 19.72\) for \(K = 2, \ldots, 6\). More topics always help here because \(\theta_d\) is inferred from the document it is then scored on, so the extra topics are being used to fit each document's noise. The training ELBO does not rescue it either: \(-11\,423\) at \(K=3\), then \(-11\,591\), \(-11\,706\), \(-11\,826\). That ordering is not a capacity statement — \(\alpha\) is per topic, so raising \(K\) also raises the total prior mass \(K\alpha\) and widens the mean-field gap — and comparing bounds computed in different parameter spaces is not a model comparison at all. Every "choose \(K\)" heuristic in the literature is a proxy: coherence needs a reference corpus, stability needs several seeds, and held-out perplexity needs a scoring rule that does not let the model fit the document it is scored on.

    2. Topics are not identified, so any cross-fit comparison is void until you permute. §8. The demo's unseeded five-topic fit comes back as \((0,2,1,3,4)\). A pipeline that compares components_[0] across two fits is comparing arbitrary objects; a pipeline that averages topic-word matrices across seeds is producing an average of different labelings and will report a topic that is the mean of two real ones. This is not exotic — it is the default failure of every topic-model ensemble.

    3. Seeds are evidence, and evidence counts each occurrence. The demo's ten seed words cover 49% of the tokens. At strength \(2\) that buys faster convergence (\(0.84 \to 0.94\) at five sweeps) and costs converged accuracy (\(0.98 \to 0.95\)). The mechanism is not subtle: a seed word's every appearance pushes towards its topic, so a topic seeded with a word that is merely frequent in the corpus becomes the "frequent word" topic and drags unrelated documents into it. Choosing seeds is choosing a prior, and it should be as deliberate as choosing \(\alpha\).

    4. Non-integer or weighted input is rejected, and that is correct. §11. The temptation is to feed tf-idf or L2-normalised rows because that is what LSA takes. LDA's likelihood is a product over tokens; there is no token to move in a weighted row, and a "count" of \(0.37\) gives a Gamma function of nothing.

    5. \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) decide document sparsity and topic sparsity, and both are usually left at defaults. The demo's \(\alpha = 0.1\) makes a 30-token document a mixture of one or two topics; \(\alpha = 5\) makes every document a flat mixture and drops the recovery from \(0.99\) to \(0.95\). \(\beta = 1.0\) spreads every topic over the whole vocabulary and raises held-out perplexity from \(20.3\) to \(20.5\). Neither is catastrophic, and neither is a default worth inheriting unexamined.

    6. The Gibbs diagnostic is not monotone, and it is not a bound. The \(80\)-nat band in §2 is what a correct chain looks like. Users used to the ELBO read it as failure and run 10 000 sweeps to chase a number that will never stop moving. The right check is on a quantity you care about — held-out perplexity, topic stability across seeds, or a downstream metric.

    7. Perplexity is comparable only within a model class, and it can beat its own oracle. §9: \(20.52\) against \(20.57\) because \(\theta\) is inferred from the scored document. LDA's perplexity is also known to favour \(\alpha \to 0\) and to disagree with human-judged topic quality (Chang et al. 2009), so a perplexity table is not a topic-quality table.

    8. Gibbs transform is stochastic, so fits are not reproducible bit for bit. The demo's perplexity changes by \(0.05\) when the topic order is permuted — not because the model changed but because _infer_gibbs draws its own random numbers and the chain's trajectory does. variational_bayes has no such issue: its transform is deterministic and permutation-invariant to exactly \(0.0\).

    9. Online LDA with \(\kappa = 0\) is not online LDA. \(0.996\) against \(0.687\) on a topic-sorted stream. The failure is silent on shuffled data, which is why it survives testing: i.i.d. minibatches hide the missing averaging, and the first deployment on a real stream — where order carries information — exposes it.

    10. Documents with no tokens break the mixture. \(N_d = 0\) makes (6)'s first factor \(0/0\); _infer_gibbs returns the uniform mixture instead, which is the right limit and a reminder that \(\theta_d\) is unidentifiable without data. The same happens to a document whose every term is unseen at fit time.

    11. Everything here is dense and single-machine. X is \(D \times V\) dense, phi is \(K \times V\) per document, and the sampler is a Python loop. For \(D = 10^6\) and \(V = 10^5\) this file is off by several orders of magnitude in both memory and time; the algorithms survive (sparse counts, sparse phi, minibatches) and the code does not.

    Applications #

    • Topic discovery in text. The original application and still the most common: a corpus of abstracts, reviews or news, and the output is \(K\) ranked word lists plus per-document mixtures. The mixtures are often more useful than the topics — they are a low-dimensional document representation that a downstream regression can use without ever reading the words.
    • Population genetics. Pritchard, Stephens & Donnelly (2000) introduced this model independently as "admixture" for genotype data, where a document is an individual, a word is an allele, and \(\theta_d\) is that individual's ancestry proportions. structure and admixture are LDA with a different likelihood, and the \(K\) problem there is the famous "how many ancestral populations" question.
    • Recommendation and collaborative filtering. Users as documents, items as words: the mixture is a taste profile and each topic is a co-purchase pattern. It behaves like a non-negative factorisation with a probabilistic story, and it inherits the same popularity bias.
    • Image and audio categorisation. "Words" are visual codewords from a quantised feature space (SIFT descriptors, MFCC frames); a "document" is an image or a clip. The bag-of-words assumption is even more obviously wrong here and it works anyway, which is worth knowing.
    • Seeded and supervised variants. Seeds (§4) are how LDA is used when some labels exist; the same idea with a per-token label likelihood is supervised LDA, and the same idea with a time index is a dynamic topic model. The prior is the extension point, which is why this file's seeded_lda is a variant rather than a separate atom.
    • Diachronic and comparative linguistics. Cognate sets, sound changes and language families: a "document" is a word form in a language, the topics are sound correspondences. The model's lack of word order is an advantage here, because the correspondence is positional rather than linear.
    • As a baseline for embeddings. A topic model is the cheap, interpretable representation: \(K\) numbers per document, no GPU, and the weights are readable. Modern practice uses it to describe an embedding space — fitting LDA to clusters of contextual vectors is a standard way to say what a direction in a transformer means — which is the same trick in the other direction.

    Where it is a trap: choosing \(K\) by likelihood; comparing topics across fits without permuting; treating perplexity as quality; seeding with frequent words and calling the result semi-supervised; and using LDA on data that is not counts of exchangeable tokens.
