    Independent component analysis

    Intuition #

    PCA and factor analysis find directions in which the data is uncorrelated. ICA finds directions in which it is independent:

    \[x = A s, \qquad s_1, \ldots, s_d \text{ independent}, \qquad (1)\]

    with no assumption that \(A\) is orthogonal, and no assumption about the shape of the sources beyond "at most one of them is Gaussian". Independence is a statement about the whole joint distribution, so it is far stronger than zero covariance — and, remarkably, it is a statement strong enough to determine \(A\).

    The demo's first line is the argument for why second moments are not enough. Whiten the mixture and every rotation is invisible to covariance:

    \[\mathrm{cov}(Z) = I \quad\text{to } 1.3 \times 10^{-15}, \qquad (2)\]

    and yet the same projections still have squared values correlated at \(0.30\). They are uncorrelated and they are obviously not independent — the correlation of \(y_1^2\) with \(y_2^2\) is a fourth moment, and nothing in the covariance can see it. Whitening is a coordinate change that removes all the second-order information, which is exactly why the remaining problem is a search over rotations.

          sources                     mixture x = A s                whitened Z
   s2 │  ·  ·  ·                 x2 │   ╱                        z2 │    ●
      │ ·  ·  ·                    │  ╱                            │  ●   ●
      │  ·  ·  ·                   │ ╱                             │ ●      ●
      └──────── s1                 └──────── x1                    └──────── z1
    independent                correlated, sheared              uncorrelated:
    (separable)                (a direction of maximum           every rotation
                                variance is not a source)        looks the same

    The three panels are the whole story. In the left panel the density factorises: knowing \(s_1\) tells you nothing about \(s_2\). In the middle, \(A\) has sheared and rotated the cloud, so the axes of the ellipse are not the sources. In the right, whitening has undone the ellipse — and produced something that looks identical from every direction. The information that identifies the sources is not in the shape of the cloud but in the shapes of the marginals, and whitening has deliberately thrown away the axes that would tell you where they are.

    PCA Factor analysis ICA
    model \(x = W^\top f + \mu\) \(x = \Lambda f + \mu + \varepsilon\) \(x = As\)
    fitted by variance likelihood, \(\Phi = I\) non-Gaussianity
    moments used second second third and higher
    mixing identified up to rotation up to rotation up to permutation and scale
    what breaks it nothing nothing two Gaussian sources

    The last two rows are the trade. PCA's answer is arbitrary inside the retained subspace and that is fine, because it is not claiming to be the true generating directions. ICA claims exactly that, and pays for the claim with an assumption about the source distributions that can be checked but not assumed away.

    Variants #

    Variant Objective Algorithm What it is for
    whitening none one eigendecomposition the baseline, and the necessary first step
    fastica_deflation negentropy of one projection fixed point, then Gram–Schmidt \(k < d\) components, in order
    fastica_symmetric negentropy of all projections fixed point, then symmetric decorrelation all components at once
    infomax logistic likelihood natural gradient, switching score a likelihood, and mutual information
    jade fourth cumulant eigenmatrices, then Jacobi exact, and cheap for small \(d\)
    kernel_ica kernel alignment coordinate descent over Givens angles dependence that is not a moment

    whitening is the coordinate change \(Z = (x - \mu) W_w^\top\) with \(\mathrm{cov}(Z) = I\). It is a variant for a reason: it is what the other five all start with, it is a legitimate answer to "decorrelate my data", and it makes the size of the remaining problem visible. In whitened coordinates the mixture is \(z = B s\) with \(B = W_w A\) orthogonal, because

    \[B B^\top = W_w A A^\top W_w^\top = W_w \Sigma_x W_w^\top = I. \qquad (3)\]

    So after whitening there are only \(d(d-1)/2\) numbers left to find, and the whole of ICA is a search over the orthogonal group. The demo measures what skipping that search costs: the whitened basis is \(67°\) away from the true one.

    fastica_deflation finds one source at a time. Each row is the fixed point of a negentropy contrast, then projected off the rows already found, so the components come out in order of non-Gaussianity. It is the right variant when you want \(k < d\) of them, or when the sources are known to be ordered, and it is the wrong one when you want all of them: the deflation error accumulates, because row \(k\) is estimated in the complement of estimates that already have error.

    fastica_symmetric updates all rows at once and repairs orthogonality with one matrix square root per step,

    \[W \leftarrow \left(W W^\top\right)^{-1/2} W, \qquad (4)\]

    which is the orthogonal polar factor of \(W\) and hence the nearest orthogonal matrix to it. No row is favoured, the errors do not compound, and the iteration is the same fixed point as the deflation form. The demo measures them agreeing to \(1.0°\) after 6 and 5 iterations respectively.

    infomax comes at the same problem from a likelihood. Model each source with a density \(p_i\), so that \(y = Wz\) has

    \[\log p(x) = \log \lvert \det W \rvert + \sum_i \log p_i(w_i^\top z) + \text{const}, \qquad (5)\]

    and ascend it with the natural gradient, which is what makes this practical: the ordinary gradient's scale depends on \(W^\top W\) and the step size then has to be retuned as the matrix grows or shrinks. The variant's cost is the density choice. A logistic density assumes a super-Gaussian source; the extended form reads the sign of each source's kurtosis from the data and uses a symmetric sub-Gaussian density for the ones that need it. On the demo's three sources — Laplace, uniform, and a heavy-tailed product — the difference is the difference between recovering them to \(1.3°\) and not recovering them at all: forced to be super-Gaussian, the uniform source is fitted as a mixture and the answer is \(48°\) wrong.

    jade uses the fourth-order cumulant instead of optimising a marginal contrast. In whitened coordinates the cumulant tensor defines an operator on symmetric matrices whose eigenvectors are exactly the outer products of the source directions, with the kurtoses as eigenvalues. Diagonalising them jointly gives the unmixing in closed form — no step size, no learning rate, four Jacobi sweeps on the demo's data, and exact in the sense that no step size or stopping rule is involved. It is also the most brittle: a source with a kurtosis near zero is indistinguishable from the exact zeros that whitening puts in the other directions.

    kernel_ica does not use a moment at all. It embeds each component's samples in a Gaussian kernel's reproducing kernel Hilbert space and measures dependence by the normalised Hilbert–Schmidt inner product of the two embeddings — 0 exactly when the components are independent, for a characteristic kernel, and positive otherwise. The contrast is minimised over rotations by coordinate descent. It is the slowest variant per fit and the one that works when the non-Gaussianity is too weak for a cumulant: on a pair whose second source has excess kurtosis \(-0.08\) it recovers the mixing to \(3.8°\) where JADE is \(58°\) off.

    Derivation #

    1. Whitening, and the rotation that is left over #

    Let \(S = \frac{1}{n} X_c^\top X_c = V \Lambda V^\top\). Then

    \[W_w = \Lambda^{-1/2} V^\top, \qquad W_w S W_w^\top = I, \qquad (6)\]

    which is exactly what _whiten computes: eigenvectors descending, each divided by the square root of its eigenvalue. Any other whitening matrix is \(Q W_w\) for orthogonal \(Q\), so whitening itself has a rotation ambiguity of the same size as the problem it is supposed to simplify. The pair of facts together are the structure of ICA:

    • whitening fixes everything second-order, and leaves an orthogonal \(B\) (3);
    • the remaining ambiguity is a rotation, and independence is what picks it.

    Note what whitening is not: a projection. No dimension is dropped unless its variance is numerically zero, because dropping one would make \(B\) rectangular and the "rotation" a projection, which is a different (and much harder) problem. The component count is decided by the estimator, not by the whitening.

    2. Identifiability, in one polynomial identity #

    Write \(\kappa_x(u) = \mathrm{cum}(u^\top x, u^\top x, u^\top x, u^\top x)\), the fourth cumulant of a projection. Cumulants add over independent variables and are homogeneous of degree four, so under (1)

    \[\kappa_x(u) = \sum_{i=1}^{d} \kappa_i \left(a_i^\top u\right)^4, \qquad (7)\]

    where \(a_i\) is the \(i\)-th column of \(A\) and \(\kappa_i\) the kurtosis of \(s_i\). The left side is a quartic form in \(u\) that you can estimate from data; the right side is a decomposition of it into fourth powers of linear forms.

    The decomposition is unique up to permutation and scale provided at most one \(\kappa_i\) is zero. That is Comon's theorem, and (7) is the whole proof strategy: a quartic form has a finite set of rank-one decompositions, and the \(\kappa_i\) are what label them. Two corollaries the demo exercises:

    • the scale of each column of \(A\) is not identified, and neither is the order — a permutation of the columns and a rescaling of each one give the same \(x\);
    • if two sources are Gaussian, \(\kappa_i = 0\) for both, their two terms are missing from (7), and the corresponding \(2\times 2\) block of the decomposition is not determined at all. That is not a limitation of any algorithm; it is the distribution being invariant along that block.

    3. Negentropy, and a contrast that can be estimated #

    Entropy is maximised, for a fixed variance, by the Gaussian. So

    \[J(y) = H\!\left(y_{\text{Gauss}}\right) - H(y) \;\ge\; 0, \qquad (8)\]

    with equality exactly when \(y\) is Gaussian, is a natural measure of non-Gaussianity: it is non-negative, scale-invariant at fixed variance, and zero for the one case where ICA cannot work. It is also not a moment, which makes it hard to estimate directly. Truncating the Gram–Charlier expansion of the density gives the standard approximation

    \[J(y) \approx \frac{\kappa_3(y)^2}{12} + \frac{\kappa_4(y)^2}{48}, \qquad (9)\]

    for a unit-variance \(y\), and _negentropy uses the second term:

    \[J(y) \approx \frac{1}{48}\left(\mathbb{E}[y^4] - 3\right)^2. \qquad (10)\]

    Two things about (10) are worth stating because both were bugs in this file. First, the reference value is exactly 3 — a standard normal has \(\mathbb{E}[\nu^4] = 3\) — so no constant has to be estimated or calibrated, and the proxy is exactly zero for Gaussian data. An earlier version used a tanh-based proxy with a nonzero reference constant, and it was minimised by a non-Gaussian; carrying a wrong constant inside a contrast does not produce a slightly worse estimate, it produces a different criterion. Second, (10) is a function of \(y^2\), so it is invariant under \(y \mapsto -y\), as negentropy is. Any proxy has to be, since the sign of a source is not identified by (1).

    4. FastICA, as a Newton step #

    Maximise \(J(w^\top z) \approx \left(\mathbb{E}[G(w^\top z)] - \mathbb{E}[G(\nu)]\right)^2\) over \(\lVert w \rVert = 1\), with \(G\) a smooth even function — here \(G = \log\cosh\), so \(g = G' = \tanh\). At an optimum of the squared quantity, either \(\mathbb{E}[G(w^\top z)] - \mathbb{E}[G(\nu)]\) is zero (the Gaussian solution, which is what we are trying to avoid) or \(w\) is a stationary point of \(\mathbb{E}[G(w^\top z)]\) under the constraint:

    \[\mathbb{E}\left[z\, g(w^\top z)\right] - \beta w = 0, \qquad \beta = \mathbb{E}\left[g'(w^\top z)\right]. \qquad (11)\]

    Newton's method on (11) needs the Jacobian \(\mathbb{E}[zz^\top g'] - \beta I\), and the approximation that makes FastICA fast is to replace it by \(\mathbb{E}[g']I - \beta I \propto I\): the data is whitened, so \(zz^\top\) is the identity in expectation and the outer product contributes only its average. One Newton step is then

    \[w^+ = \mathbb{E}\left[z\, g(w^\top z)\right] - \mathbb{E}\left[g'(w^\top z)\right] w, \qquad (12)\]

    which is _fastica_step and it is three lines, because both expectations are just means over rows.

    The symmetric form applies (12) to every row and then repairs orthogonality. If \(W\) has orthonormal rows the fixed point is unchanged by (4): a symmetric decorrelation of an already orthogonal matrix is the identity. If it does not, (4) is the projection onto the orthogonal group in the Frobenius norm, so it is the smallest correction that restores the constraint. Deflation instead projects each new row off the previous ones — and does it twice, because one pass leaves a residue of the size of the projection error, which is enough to stop the iteration converging to its own tolerance.

    5. The natural gradient, and why the identity term is not a detail #

    Differentiate (5) with respect to \(W\):

    \[\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial W} = W^{-\top} + \mathbb{E}\left[\psi(y) z^\top\right], \qquad \psi(y) = -\frac{d}{dy}\log p(y). \qquad (13)\]

    The natural gradient preconditions by the Fisher information, which for this parameterisation is \(W^\top W\), so

    \[\Delta W = \left(W^{-\top} + \mathbb{E}[\psi z^\top]\right) W^\top W = \left(I + \mathbb{E}\left[\psi(y)y^\top\right]\right) W. \qquad (14)\]

    The identity term in (14) is not a convention: \(W^{-\top}W^\top W = W\), and it is what turns a gradient whose scale depends on \(W\) into a multiplicative update that is equivariant under linear changes of coordinates.

    Now choose the density. For a super-Gaussian source, \(p \propto 1/\cosh\), so \(\psi = -\tanh(y) - y\); for a sub-Gaussian one the signs of the odd part flip and \(\psi = +\tanh(y) - y\). Writing \(\psi = -K\tanh(y) - y\) with \(K = \pm 1\) per component and using \(\mathbb{E}[yy^\top] = I\) in (14),

    \[\Delta W = \left(I - K\,\mathbb{E}\left[\tanh(y)y^\top\right] - I\right)W = -K\,\mathbb{E}\left[\tanh(y)y^\top\right]W, \qquad (15)\]

    which is the form in the code. A fixed point of (14) satisfies \(\mathbb{E}[\psi(y)y^\top] = -I\), and score_residual_ is the max-abs of that residual — the demo measures \(8 \times 10^{-8}\) on a converged fit. It is a sharper check than the step size, because row scaling changes both \(y\) and \(\psi\): a stationary step and a stationary score are not the same statement.

    \(K\) is estimated once, from the kurtosis of the initial projections, and then frozen. The online extended-infomax update re-estimates it every step, which is fine when each sample updates the matrix once; in a batch iteration that switches the score function back and forth it oscillates between two fixed points. And a wrong \(K\) is not a small error: forcing \(K = +1\) on a uniform source asks the model to fit a sub-Gaussian marginal with a super-Gaussian density, and the demo's answer lands \(48°\) away with the source's apparent kurtosis pushed from \(-1.2\) to \(0.1\).

    6. JADE: the cumulant is an operator, and its eigenvectors are the sources #

    For whitened \(z\), define

    \[Q(M) = \mathbb{E}\left[z z^\top \left(z^\top M z\right)\right] - \mathrm{tr}(M) I - 2M. \qquad (16)\]

    The subtraction is forced by whiteness. Expanding the expectation in cumulants,

    \[\mathbb{E}\left[z_i z_j (z^\top M z)\right] = \kappa_{ijkl} M_{kl} + \delta_{ij}\,\mathrm{tr}(M) + 2M_{ij}, \qquad (17)\]

    so (16) is the fourth-cumulant part alone. It is a linear operator on symmetric matrices, self-adjoint in the Frobenius inner product.

    In the source basis \(z = Bs\) with \(B\) orthogonal, \(\kappa_{ijkl} = \sum_p \kappa_p B_{ip}B_{jp}B_{kp}B_{lp}\), and therefore

    \[Q\left(u_p u_p^\top\right) = \kappa_p\, u_p u_p^\top, \qquad Q\left(u_p u_q^\top + u_q u_p^\top\right) = 0 \ \ (p \ne q), \qquad (18)\]

    where \(u_p\) is the \(p\)-th column of \(B\). So the operator's eigenvectors are the rank-one matrices \(u_p u_p^\top\), its nonzero eigenvalues are the kurtoses, and everything else sits in an eigenvalue-zero subspace of dimension \(d(d-1)/2\). The code builds \(Q\) in an orthonormal basis of symmetric matrices, takes the \(d\) eigenvectors of largest \(\lvert \lambda \rvert\), and joint-diagonalises them.

    Joint diagonalisation means finding orthogonal \(V\) with \(V^\top M_l V\) diagonal for every \(l\). For a single \(l\) that is the symmetric eigenvalue problem; for a set, the Jacobi approach is to rotate one \((p, q)\) plane at a time to the angle that minimises that plane's off-diagonal contribution over all \(l\). Under a rotation by \(\theta\) of the plane, with \(a = M_{pp}\), \(d = M_{qq}\), \(b = M_{pq}\) and \(u = (a - d)/2\),

    \[M'_{pq} = b \cos 2\theta - u \sin 2\theta, \qquad (19)\]

    and summing the square over the set,

    \[\sum_l \left(M'_{pq}\right)^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\Sigma u^2 + \Sigma b^2\right) - \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\Sigma u^2 - \Sigma b^2\right)\cos 4\theta - \Sigma ub \sin 4\theta, \qquad (20)\]

    which is minimised at

    \[4\theta = \mathrm{atan2}\left(2\Sigma ub,\; \Sigma u^2 - \Sigma b^2\right). \qquad (21)\]

    The factor of four is the whole subtlety, and it cost this file a day. Because \(4\theta\) appears twice in (20), the criterion has period \(\pi/2\) in \(\theta\); a formula derived from the eigenvector of the quadratic form in \((\cos 2\theta, \sin 2\theta)\) gives \(\theta\) twice too large unless that is noticed, and the resulting rotation still decreases the criterion at every step. It converges monotonically, in a handful of sweeps, to a rotation that is \(10\)–\(20°\) short of the answer and looks entirely plausible. The symptom is not divergence; it is an off-diagonal mass that stops at 70% instead of 8%.

    7. Kernel ICA: dependence without moments #

    Let \(K\) be a Gaussian kernel matrix on one component's samples and \(L\) on another's, both centred in feature space by \(H = I - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top\) so that \(HKH\) has zero row and column means. The Hilbert–Schmidt inner product \(\langle HKH, HLH \rangle_F\) is the empirical HSIC of the pair, and HSIC is zero exactly when the two variables are independent — not merely uncorrelated — provided the kernel is characteristic, which the Gaussian one is. Normalising by the two Frobenius norms gives a bounded correlation,

    \[\rho = \frac{\langle HKH,\, HLH\rangle_F}{\lVert HKH \rVert_F \, \lVert HLH \rVert_F} \in [-1, 1], \qquad (22)\]

    and the contrast is \(\sum_{i, minimised over the rotation. For \(d = 2\) there is one pair and one angle, so a grid search is an exhaustive minimisation up to its resolution: the demo's fitted contrast, \(0.00655\), is the minimum of an independent 721-point scan to five decimals.

    Three implementation notes that are not incidental:

    • the bandwidth is one scalar, from the median off-diagonal pairwise distance of the whitened sample. Whitening has already fixed the scale, so one knob is right, but the median must exclude the diagonal: including \(n\) zeros in \(n^2\) entries halves the median and drives the kernel towards the identity;
    • the contrast is evaluated on a subsample (300 rows here), because each evaluation costs \(O(n^2 d)\) and a grid search needs dozens of them;
    • the search is coordinate descent over Givens angles, coarse-to-fine, not a gradient method. With \(d = 2\) it is exact by construction.

    8. The boundary: why two Gaussians cannot be separated #

    If \(s \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and \(z = Bs\) with \(B\) orthogonal, then \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, BB^\top) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) — the same distribution, for every orthogonal \(B\). The likelihood, the negentropy contrast, the cumulant tensor and the kernel contrast are all constant along the rotation group, so no method has a maximum to find. The demo measures the flatness directly: the negentropy proxy varies by \(10^{-4}\) over the whole circle, which is less than its own sampling noise, and six FastICA seeds stop at \(40.5°\) and \(49.5°\) — both "converged", both stationary, both meaningless.

    This is Comon's condition from §2 seen from the other side. With \(d = 2\) and both kurtoses zero, (7) is the zero polynomial and carries no information; with one Gaussian the polynomial still determines the other direction, which is why the demo's JADE recovers a mixture to \(2.8°\) even though it contains a nearly Gaussian source, and why it fails at \(58°\) when the only non-Gaussian source is that weak and \(d = 2\).

    Code walkthrough #

    ICA(n_components=None, *, variant=..., seed=..., extend=True, max_iter=..., tol=..., lr=...) with fit, transform, fit_transform, and the attributes components_ \((k, d)\), mixing_ \((d, k)\), whitening_, rotation_, contrast_curve_, n_iter_, converged_, plus the variant-specific bandwidth_, kernel_sample_, kurtosis_signs_, negentropy_ and score_residual_.

    Two conventions are load-bearing. components_ has the same orientation as sklearn's — one row per component, so transform(X) = (X - mean_) @ components_.T — and components_ = rotation_ @ whitening_ holds for every variant, which is what makes them comparable: they whiten identically and differ only in the orthogonal map. transform returns the sources with whitening's scaling, not the true sources' scaling, because the scaling is not identified in the first place.

    1. Whitening in six lines #

        mean = X.mean(axis=0)
    Xc = X - mean
    S = Xc.T @ Xc / X.shape[0]
    w, V = np.linalg.eigh(S)
    order = np.argsort(w)[::-1]
    w, V = w[order], V[:, order]
    keep = w > EPS * max(float(w[0]), 1.0)
    W_w = (V[:, keep] / np.sqrt(w[keep])).T          # (r, d), rows are directions
    return mean, W_w, Xc @ W_w.T

    (6), with the two decisions that matter made explicit. keep is a rank test, not a variance cut: the threshold is EPS times the largest eigenvalue, so a direction is discarded only when it is numerically indistinguishable from zero, never for being small. That is what keeps whitening a change of coordinates rather than a projection. Dividing eigenvectors by \(\sqrt{w}\) and transposing puts the directions in the rows, which is why Z = Xc @ W_w.T reads as a projection onto them.

    2. The fixed-point step #

            U = Z @ W.T
        G = np.tanh(U)                                # g = tanh, logcosh's derivative
        Gp = 1.0 - G * G                              # g'
        return (G.T @ Z) / Z.shape[0] - Gp.mean(axis=0)[:, None] * W

    (12), for all rows at once. G.T @ Z is \(\mathbb{E}[z g(w^\top z)]\) with the expectation taken over rows, and Gp.mean(axis=0) is \(\mathbb{E}[g'(w^\top z)]\) per row — one scalar per component, which is exactly the approximation that made the Newton step cheap. \(\tanh\) and \(1 - \tanh^2\) are the same two numbers, so Gp costs no second transcendental.

    3. Symmetric decorrelation #

                    W_new = self._fastica_step(Z, W)
                # symmetric decorrelation: (W⁺W⁺ᵀ)^{-1/2}W⁺ makes the rows
                # orthonormal without singling any of them out
                W_new = _sym_inv_sqrt(W_new @ W_new.T) @ W_new
                change = float(np.max(np.abs(np.abs(np.sum(W_new * W, axis=1)) - 1.0)))

    (4) in one line. The convergence test is the row-wise dot product with the previous iterate: at a fixed point the rows are unchanged up to sign, so \(\lvert \langle w_i^{\text{new}}, w_i \rangle \rvert \to 1\) and the max deviation from 1 is the natural residual. Comparing the matrices entrywise instead would fail, because a sign flip changes every entry of a row.

    4. Deflation's double projection #

                    w_new = Z.T @ g / n - gp.mean() * w
                # two Gram–Schmidt passes: one leaves a residue of the size of
                # the projection error, which is enough to spoil convergence
                for _ in range(2):
                    if i:
                        w_new -= (W[:i] @ w_new) @ W[:i]
                w_new /= max(np.linalg.norm(w_new), EPS)

    The same (12) without the matrix form, then the constraint: \(w\) must be orthogonal to the \(i\) rows already found. W[:i] @ w_new are the projections and the subtraction removes them; doing it twice is classical Gram–Schmidt reorthogonalisation, and one pass is not enough because the first row's error is amplified by the second projection. The final normalisation is what makes done = abs(w_new @ w) > 1 - tol a meaningful test.

    5. Infomax: the update and the score #

                for it in range(1, self.max_iter + 1):
                U = Z @ W.T
                psi = -K * np.tanh(U) - U
                # ΔW = lr (I + E[ψ(u)uᵀ]) W: the natural gradient, whose identity
                # part cancels the −u in ψ and leaves −K E[tanh(u)uᵀ]
                W_new = W + self.lr * ((np.eye(d) + psi.T @ U / n) @ W)

    (14) and (15) together. psi.T @ U / n is \(\mathbb{E}[\psi(u)u^\top]\), a \(d \times d\) matrix whose diagonal is the natural step size for each component and whose off-diagonals are the cross-talk that the natural gradient is removing. K is a vector of \(\pm 1\), one per component, frozen before the loop.

                U = Z @ W.T
            psi = -K * np.tanh(U) - U
            resid = float(np.max(np.abs(np.eye(d) + psi.T @ U / n)))

    The stationarity check, computed on the \(W\) at which the loop stopped — before the row normalisation, because normalising changes \(u\) and therefore \(\psi\). The demo's converged run gives \(8 \times 10^{-8}\); the stubborn one, which hits its iteration cap, gives \(2 \times 10^{-6}\) and reports converged_ = False.

    6. The cumulant operator #

            q = np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", Z, M, Z)
        return (Z * q[:, None]).T @ Z / Z.shape[0] - np.trace(M) * np.eye(len(M)) - 2.0 * M

    (16) literally. einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", Z, M, Z) is the row-wise quadratic form \(z_i^\top M z_i\) without materialising an \(n \times d \times d\) tensor, and the three terms are the expectation, the trace correction and the \(2M\) correction from (17). It is a static method precisely because it depends on nothing but the data.

    7. From the operator to the matrices to diagonalise #

            m = len(pairs)
        Op = np.zeros((m, m))
        for col, E in enumerate(basis):
            Q = self._cumulant_apply(Z, E)
            for row, F in enumerate(basis):
                Op[row, col] = float(np.sum(Q * F))
        lam, vec = np.linalg.eigh(Op)
        order = np.argsort(np.abs(lam))[::-1][:d]     # the d significant eigenmatrices
        Ms = [sum(vec[i, c] * basis[i] for i in range(m)) for c in order]

    The operator applied to each basis element, then projected onto every basis element: \(O_{rc} = \langle F_r, Q(E_c) \rangle_F\), an \(m \times m\) matrix with \(m = d(d+1)/2\). Because the basis is orthonormal, eigh on it gives both the eigenvalues and the eigenmatrices, and the last line turns a coordinate vector back into a matrix. Sorting by \(\lvert \lambda \rvert\) rather than by \(\lambda\) matters: kurtoses come with both signs, and a strongly sub-Gaussian source is as informative as a super-Gaussian one. Taking \(d\) of them is (18).

    8. The Jacobi angle #

                        u = np.array([(M[p, p] - M[q, q]) / 2.0 for M in Ms])
                    b = np.array([M[p, q] for M in Ms])

    then

                        theta = 0.25 * math.atan2(2.0 * float(u @ b), float(u @ u - b @ b))
                    c, s = math.cos(theta), math.sin(theta)
                    R = np.eye(d)
                    R[p, p] = R[q, q] = c
                    R[p, q], R[q, p] = -s, s
                    Ms = [R.T @ M @ R for M in Ms]
                    V = V @ R

    (19)–(21). u and b are the two vectors-of-scalars over the set of matrices: the half-difference of diagonals and the off-diagonal, both for the pair \((p,q)\). Everything after the angle is bookkeeping — build the Givens rotation, apply it to every matrix by congruence, and accumulate it in V so the total map is recoverable. V starts as the identity and ends as the diagonaliser; the returned V.T is the unmixing, because sources = Z @ V and the class composes rotation_ on the left.

    Note the difference between "apply" and "replace": Ms = [R.T @ M @ R ...] is what makes the sweep monotonically increase the diagonal mass, and the stopping test compares that mass before and after the whole sweep, not the angle.

    9. Kernel alignment, and the bandwidth that must not include the diagonal #

            Y = Z @ U.T
        n = Y.shape[0]
        H = np.eye(n) - 1.0 / n
        Ks = []
        for i in range(Y.shape[1]):
            D = (Y[:, i][:, None] - Y[:, i][None, :]) ** 2
            K = H @ np.exp(-0.5 * D / self.bandwidth_) @ H
            Ks.append(K / max(float(np.linalg.norm(K)), EPS))
        return float(sum(np.sum(Ks[i] * Ks[j])
                         for i in range(len(Ks)) for j in range(i + 1, len(Ks))))

    (22). H @ K @ H is the double centring, D is the squared-distance matrix, and the normalisation to unit Frobenius norm is what makes each pair's score comparable across bandwidths. np.eye(n) is an \(n \times n\) matrix and this is the reason the contrast is restricted to a subsample: at \(n = 1500\) one evaluation would be \(2\) kernel matrices and four \(1500^2\) multiplications, times dozens of grid points.

            D = ((sub[:, None, :] - sub[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(axis=2)
        self.bandwidth_ = max(float(np.median(D[np.triu_indices(len(sub), 1)])), 1e-6)

    One bandwidth, from the median pairwise distance — of the full vectors, so each pair contributes one number, and restricted to the strict upper triangle, so the \(n\) zeros on the diagonal do not drag the median down. Getting this wrong is not a tuning issue: a median of zero makes the kernel the identity and the contrast a number with no dependence signal in it at all.

    10. Composition, and the one refusal #

            self.unmixing_ = self.rotation_ @ self.whitening_
        return self

    The whole model in one line: whiten, then rotate. Every variant is a different rotation_, and every attribute downstream — components_, mixing_, transform — goes through this product, so a variant with a transposed convention cannot hide.

            if k < d and self.variant != "fastica_deflation":
            raise ValueError(

    Only deflation can extract fewer components than there are features, and the reason is structural rather than a missing feature: the other variants optimise a contrast over a full orthogonal matrix, and dropping to a \(k \times d\) rectangular one changes the constraint set from \(O(d)\) to a Stiefel manifold. The error says which variant to use instead, because the alternative — silently returning the first \(k\) rows of a \(d\)-component fit — would look like it worked.

    Complexity #

    \(n\) rows, \(d\) features, \(T\) iterations.

    Operation Time Memory Note
    _whiten \(O(nd^2 + d^3)\) \(O(d^2)\) one covariance, one eigendecomposition
    fastica_symmetric \(O(Tnd^2)\) \(O(d^2)\) two products per iteration
    fastica_deflation \(O(Tnd^2)\) \(O(d^2)\) \(d\) rows, each with its own iteration
    infomax \(O(Tnd^2)\) \(O(d^2)\) three starts by default
    jade \(O(nd^4 + d^6)\) \(O(d^4)\) the \(m \times m\) operator, \(m = d(d+1)/2\)
    kernel_ica \(O(Gn_s^2d)\) \(O(n_s^2)\) \(G\) contrast evaluations, \(n_s\) subsample
    transform \(O(mdk)\) \(O(mk)\) one product

    Three things follow. First, the gradient variants are linear in \(n\) and quadratic in \(d\), and the cost per iteration is dominated by producing \(U = ZW^\top\); the constant is small enough that 4000 rows and three components converge in a few milliseconds. Second, JADE is the outlier: the operator is \(d(d+1)/2\) squared, so it is the cheapest method at \(d = 3\) and hopeless at \(d = 100\) — it is the variant for small, well-conditioned problems where exactness is worth the price. Third, kernel ICA is quadratic in the subsample, not in \(n\), which is why it is affordable at all; the price is a contrast with sampling noise, and \(3.8°\) rather than \(1°\) of accuracy.

    The whole six-section demo runs in 2.0 s.

    Failure modes #

    1. Two Gaussians are not separable, and more data does not help. The demo measures a negentropy range of \(10^{-4}\) over the entire rotation circle, and six FastICA seeds stopping at \(40.5°\) and \(49.5°\) — both converged_ = True. This is the one failure mode that no amount of engineering fixes, because the objective is flat (8). The practical test is to look at the recovered kurtoses: if one is near zero, its component is not identified, and the reported direction is whatever the seed produced. A corollary that catches people: whitening a Gaussian mixture produces something exactly spherical, so a pipeline that whitens, runs ICA and reports the components will report five orthogonal directions for pure noise.

    2. A near-zero cumulant defeats a cumulant method. On the demo's two-source pair with kurtoses \(2.09\) and \(-0.08\), JADE lands \(58°\) from the truth. The operator's eigenvalues are \(2.09\) and \(-0.08\); the remaining \(d(d-1)/2\) are exactly zero by whiteness, so "the \(d\) largest in absolute value" picks a noise eigenvector and the joint diagonalisation does its best with it. The fix is not a better Jacobi sweep — there is nothing there to diagonalise. Use a contrast that is not a moment (kernel_ica gets \(3.8°\) here), or get more data so that \(-0.08\) separates from zero, or reduce \(d\) so that the zero subspace is smaller.

    3. A wrong score function is a wrong model, not a slow fit. Forced to assume every source is super-Gaussian, infomax converges cleanly, satisfies its own fixed-point condition, and lands \(48°\) away with the uniform source's kurtosis reported as \(0.1\) instead of \(-1.2\) — the model has fitted the source as a mixture of two super-Gaussian directions. Nothing in the output flags this. The tell is again the recovered kurtoses: a source that was strongly sub-Gaussian in the data and comes back near Gaussian has been absorbed.

    4. Extended infomax oscillates if the switch is not frozen. Re-estimating \(K\) every iteration gives a run whose relative step never falls below about \(10^{-2}\) — it cycles between two score functions instead of settling on one, so it neither converges nor satisfies the stationarity condition, and 20 000 iterations do not help. Frozen after the first estimate, nine of ten seeds reach the same stationary point and the tenth lands \(48°\) away, which is what the three-start selection in _fit_infomax is for. This is a batch-versus-online difference: the online form updates the matrix once per sample, so the switch is a slowly varying function of the data, while a full-batch iteration that re-evaluates it sees a discontinuity every step.

    5. Whitening alone is not an unmixing. It is \(67°\) from the truth on the demo's data, and it is the answer to a different question. It is also easy to mistake for ICA because it produces decorrelated components with unit variance and a plausible-looking mixing matrix. The diagnostic is the same one the demo opens with: correlate the squares of the components. If those are not near zero, the components are still dependent, whatever the covariance says.

    6. FastICA's answer is accurate to about a degree, not exactly. The demo's two implementations recover the true directions to \(1.20°\) and \(1.25°\) on 4000 rows. That is sampling error, not a convergence failure: the contrast is estimated from data and its maximum moves with the sample. Reporting six decimal places from a 4000-row fit is reporting the optimiser's tolerance, not the statistical accuracy. It also means that "deflation and symmetric agree to \(1.0°\)" is the strongest statement available — they agree to within the noise, which is what the demo asserts.

    7. Deflation's error accumulates, and one Gram–Schmidt pass is not enough. Row \(i\) is estimated in the orthogonal complement of rows \(1 \ldots i-1\), so their errors are inherited. One projection pass leaves a residual of the size of the projection error, which is the same order as the tolerance and enough to stop the iteration converging; two passes are standard and cost almost nothing. With \(k\) components the composite error grows roughly like \(\sqrt{k}\) and the last component is the worst — the reason to prefer the symmetric variant when all \(d\) are wanted.

    8. Kernel ICA is slow, and its contrast is noisy. 70 contrast evaluations on a 300-row subsample cost about 0.1 s here, and the search is over \(d(d-1)/2\) angles in sequence, so the cost grows like \(d^2\) evaluations with \(O(n_s^2 d)\) each. The contrast itself is a \(U\)-statistic with \(O(1/n_s)\) bias, so the subsample sets a floor on accuracy: \(3.8°\) on the demo's pair against FastICA's \(2°\). It is the variant to reach for when the moments are uninformative, not the variant to reach for by default.

    9. The scale of the recovered components is arbitrary. \(s\) and \(Ds\) for diagonal \(D\) give the same \(x\), so no method can recover the true scale, and the one this code returns is fixed by whitening: every component has unit variance on the training data. A coefficient with a physical meaning, such as a source amplitude in physical units, has to be calibrated against something external. The same applies to sign: components_ rows may come out flipped, which is why every comparison in the demo uses \(\lvert\cos\rvert\) rather than a dot product.

    10. It assumes as many sensors as sources, and no noise. With \(A\) square and whitening exact, (3) makes the problem orthogonal. With more sources than sensors the problem is underdetermined and the answer is a sparse decomposition rather than a rotation; with noise the whitening is biased and the bias propagates into every component. Neither is handled here, and both are common in the applications below — which is why the classical ICA pipeline is nearly always preceded by a dimension reduction (PCA to \(k\) components) and a noise-whitening step, at the cost of assuming the discarded directions are noise.

    11. It needs more rows than columns, and it says so. The check refuses \(n < 2d\). Whitening needs \(n \ge d\) to have a nonsingular covariance; the higher-order statistics need considerably more, because a fourth moment's standard error is \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\) and the contrast differences between candidate rotations are small. At \(n = 2d\) the fit runs and the answer is noise.

    Applications #

    • The cocktail party. The original problem: \(d\) microphones, \(d\) speakers, and a room that convolves as well as mixes. Instantaneous ICA is what this file implements; the convolutive version moves to the frequency domain and runs one ICA per bin, then has to solve the permutation ambiguity across bins — the same non-identifiability, in a form that costs real engineering.
    • EEG and MEG. Blind source separation of eye blinks, cardiac artefact and muscle from neural signal. The sources are super-Gaussian and few, the mixing is instantaneous because the skull conducts slowly, and ICA is the standard tool. Care is needed: the components are only as interpretable as the independence assumption, and a dipole fitted to an artefact component is a dipole fitted to a rotation.
    • fMRI. Spatial ICA for resting-state networks, where the "sources" are spatial maps and the samples are time points. The independence assumption is weaker here — networks are not independent in any physical sense — and the method works anyway, which is worth knowing but does not make the components identifiable.
    • Telecommunications. MIMO demultiplexing, where the sources are symbol streams and the mixing is the channel matrix. This is the cleanest application: the sources are genuinely independent by construction, at most one is Gaussian, and the sample size is large.
    • Audio and music. Separating instruments and vocals; also the honest counterexample, since musical sources are dependent by design — they are played together, in time.
    • Financial returns. Independent components of a return matrix are used as statistical risk factors, the same job as PCA factors but with a different criterion. The independence assumption is plainly false (factors comove in a crisis), and the honest reading is "a rotation chosen by a different rule", not "the true economic drivers".
    • Astronomy. Separating the cosmic microwave background from foregrounds; the components have very different marginal shapes, which is the regime ICA is built for.
    • A testbed for identifiability. Along with factor analysis's rotation problem, this is the cleanest example in statistics of a model whose likelihood is flat along a manifold of parameters — and here the manifold is continuous only in the Gaussian case. §8 and the demo's sixth section are the same phenomenon as factor analysis's rotation non-identifiability, with the difference that ICA's criterion resolves it and the Gaussian case does not.

    Where it is a trap: whitening, running ICA, and reading the components as the data's true structure when the data is nearly Gaussian; reporting component order or sign; fitting a physical model to a component that is one of several equivalent rotations; and treating a deflation fit's \(k\)-th component as being as reliable as its first.
