    Probabilistic PCA

    Intuition #

    PCA answers one question — which \(k\)-plane is closest to the data — and refuses to answer any other. There is no likelihood, so two different \(k\) cannot be compared; there is no noise parameter, so the distance from the plane has no units; and a row with one missing coordinate cannot be projected at all.

    Probabilistic PCA keeps the plane and adds a story that generated the data:

    \[x = W z + \mu + \varepsilon, \qquad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_k), \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I_d), \qquad (1)\]

    which marginalises to a single Gaussian with a low-rank-plus-diagonal covariance:

    \[x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, C), \qquad C = W W^\top + \sigma^2 I_d. \qquad (2)\]

    z is the position on the plane, W z puts it in the observed space, and ε is the part of the row the plane does not explain.

                            σ  ┌─ the fog: isotropic, thickness σ
                     ╭──╮   │
        · · ·   ·  · │··│ · │ ·        ·  scattered around a 2-plane
      ·    ·   ·   · ╰──╯   │
        ·   ·    ·  ·   ·   │  ·  ·
     ────────────────────────────────  the retained subspace (k = 2)

    The quiet fact that makes this worth doing: the maximum-likelihood W spans exactly the PCA subspace. Tipping and Bishop showed that

    \[W_{\mathrm{ML}} = V_k \left(\Lambda_k - \sigma^2 I\right)^{1/2} R \qquad (3)\]

    for any orthogonal \(R\), where \(V_k \Lambda_k V_k^\top\) is the top-\(k\) part of the sample covariance. So PPCA does not find better directions than PCA. It finds the same directions and additionally reports how far off them the data sits.

    That extra number buys three things.

    PCA PPCA
    rank selection a scree plot, read by eye a held-out likelihood
    distance to the plane unnormalised \(\sigma^2\), in the data's units
    a partly observed row cannot project it \(E[x_{\text{miss}} \mid x_{\text{obs}}]\)
    new data no generative story sample from the fitted model

    The picture that makes (2) concrete is the eigenvalue spectrum. PPCA's covariance has the top \(k\) sample eigenvalues and then \(d - k\) copies of \(\sigma^2\):

        eigenvalue
      │  ●                                      sample covariance
      │   ●
      │    ●  ●                                 ●
      │         ●  ●  ●  ●  ●  ●  ●             ●  ●  ●  ●  ●  ●
      │  ─────────────────────────── σ²  ───────────────────────
      └────────────────────────────────────────────────────▶ index
         ╰── retained ──╯   ╰────── flattened tail ──────╯

    So \(\sigma^2\) is the mean of the discarded eigenvalues, and PPCA is the statement "everything beyond \(k\) looks like isotropic noise". When that statement is false — the tail decays smoothly instead of flattening — the fit is still the best rank-\(k\) Gaussian, but \(\sigma^2\) is an average of things that are not equal, and the residual structure is real.

    One consequence deserves its own sentence, because it is what makes the posterior useful: the model's Gaussian fog means the reconstruction \(\mu + W E[z \mid x]\) is a denoiser, not a projection. Along the retained directions the noise is almost entirely subtracted; along the discarded ones it is kept. The demo measures the result: mean squared reconstruction error is \((d - k)\sigma^2\), not \(d\sigma^2\).

    Variants #

    Variant Fit Algorithm What it adds
    ppca_em observed likelihood EM the default, and the one that generalises
    ppca_closed same optimum eigendecomposition exact, one SVD, no iterations
    missing_data observed-block likelihood EM with per-row conditioning NaN-tolerant fitting
    mixture_ppca mixture likelihood EM on responsibilities several subspaces at once

    ppca_em is the workhorse. The E-step computes the posterior of z given the row, the M-step solves for W and \(\sigma^2\) in closed form given the posterior second moments. Both steps are exact, so the likelihood is monotone by construction, and the demo checks that over the whole curve. It converges in a few dozen iterations from the closed-form initialisation and is the variant that extends to missing data and to mixtures.

    ppca_closed is (3) directly: one eigendecomposition of the sample covariance, \(\sigma^2\) = mean of the discarded eigenvalues, and a square root. It is exact, deterministic, and needs no tolerances — which is why it is the oracle in the demo: EM's answer has to match it in \(C\) (not in \(W\), which is only identified up to a rotation) or one of the two is wrong.

    missing_data conditions on the observed coordinates of each row instead of the full row. Every row gets its own \(k \times k\) system, the M-step for W becomes one least-squares problem per feature over the rows where that feature is present, and \(\sigma^2\) normalises by observed cells rather than all cells. With nothing missing it reduces exactly to ppca_em — the demo asserts that — so the extra machinery costs nothing when it is not needed.

    mixture_ppca replaces "the data is near one \(k\)-plane" with "the data is a mixture of \(m\) of them", each with its own centre, loading and noise:

    \[p(x) = \sum_{c=1}^{m} \pi_c \, \mathcal{N}(x;\, \mu_c,\, W_c W_c^\top + \sigma_c^2 I). \qquad (4)\]

    Responsibilities are computed with the same density as the single model, so the E-step is one _logpdf call per component, and the M-step is the closed form on a weighted scatter matrix plus one EM refinement of W. It is the natural generalisation of PCA to a union of subspaces, and it is the entry point to mixtures of factor analysers.

    Derivation #

    The density without a \(d \times d\) inverse #

    For \(n\) rows and \(d\) features, forming \(C\) costs \(O(d^2)\) memory and inverting it \(O(d^3)\). With \(k \ll d\) both are avoidable by the matrix determinant lemma and Woodbury:

    \[\log \lvert C \rvert = (d - k) \log \sigma^2 + \log \lvert W^\top W + \sigma^2 I_k \rvert, \qquad (5)\]
    \[x^\top C^{-1} x = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\left( \lVert x \rVert^2 - (W^\top x)^\top (W^\top W + \sigma^2 I_k)^{-1} (W^\top x) \right). \qquad (6)\]

    Both right-hand sides involve only \(k \times k\) objects. This is not a micro-optimisation: it is what lets PPCA be applied at \(d = 10^4\) where \(C\) alone would be 800 MB, and it is the same identity that gives the posterior below.

    E-step: the posterior of the latent #

    With \(M = W^\top W + \sigma^2 I_k\),

    \[z \mid x \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(M^{-1} W^\top (x - \mu),\; \sigma^2 M^{-1}\right). \qquad (7)\]

    Every row of a complete matrix shares the same posterior covariance — it is independent of \(x\) — which is why EM here is so cheap: the covariance is computed once per iteration, and only the means are per-row.

    M-step: the second moment, not the covariance #

    Maximising the expected complete-data log-likelihood over \(W\) with the posterior held fixed gives a weighted least-squares problem whose normal equations are

    \[W = \left( \sum_i (x_i - \mu)\, E[z_i]^\top \right)\left( \sum_i E[z_i z_i^\top] \right)^{-1}, \qquad (8)\]

    and the second factor is the second moment, not the covariance:

    \[E[z_i z_i^\top] = E[z_i]E[z_i]^\top + \mathrm{Cov}(z_i). \qquad (9)\]

    Dropping the \(\mathrm{Cov}\) term is the classic mistake: it makes the fit systematically over-confident in directions the data constrains weakly, because it treats a posterior mean as if it were an observed value. The demo's fit would still look plausible; it just would not agree with the closed form.

    For the noise,

    \[\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{nd}\left( \lVert X_c - E[Z] W^\top \rVert_F^2 + n\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left( \sigma^2 M^{-1} W^\top W \right) \right). \qquad (10)\]

    The second term is the expected residual of the latent, and for a mixture that term shows up weighted by the responsibilities.

    The factor that must appear once. \(\mathrm{Cov}(z) = \sigma^2 M^{-1}\) already carries a \(\sigma^2\). Multiplying the trace term by \(\sigma^2\) a second time — which is easy to do while transcribing (10) — makes every M-step shrink \(\sigma^2\) a bit too fast. EM still converges — to \(\sigma^2 = 0.123\) against the closed form's \(0.158\), 62 nats worse in likelihood — and every loss curve is still monotone, so nothing looks wrong until a quantity that does not depend on the path checks it. That was a real bug in this file, and the closed form is what caught it.

    The closed form #

    Stationarity of the log likelihood with respect to \(W\) means \(W\)'s columns have to be eigenvectors of the sample covariance in the retained subspace and the residual has to be isotropic. Writing \(S = V \Lambda V^\top\) with \(\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_d\):

    \[W = V_k \left(\Lambda_k - \sigma^2 I\right)^{1/2} R, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{d - k}\sum_{j > k} \lambda_j, \qquad (11)\]

    and the resulting covariance is the sample spectrum with its tail replaced by its mean:

    \[C = V_k \Lambda_k V_k^\top + \sigma^2 \left(I - V_k V_k^\top\right). \qquad (12)\]

    Two limits are worth remembering. As \(\sigma^2 \to 0\), (11) becomes \(W = V_k \Lambda_k^{1/2}\), the PCA projection, and the posterior mean becomes the orthogonal projection — PPCA contains PCA. And when \(k = d\) there is no tail, so the formula divides by zero and \(\sigma^2\) hits the boundary at 0; that is why the code guards with max(..., 1e-12) rather than trusting the expression.

    Why the in-sample likelihood cannot choose \(k\) #

    Substituting (11) and (12) into the log likelihood gives the profile

    \[\ell_k = -\frac{n}{2}\left[ d\log 2\pi + \sum_{j \le k} \log \lambda_j + (d - k)\log \hat\sigma_k^2 + d \right], \qquad \hat\sigma_k^2 = \frac{1}{d-k}\sum_{j>k}\lambda_j, \qquad (13)\]

    which is non-decreasing in \(k\) for the trivial reason that a bigger model contains the smaller one. Setting \(k = d\) gives \(\ell = \infty\) at \(\hat\sigma^2 = 0\). So rank selection needs either held-out data or an explicit test; the classical route is Anderson's likelihood-ratio test for the sphericity of the tail, whose null distribution is a mixture of \(\chi^2\) because \(\sigma^2 \ge 0\) sits on a boundary.

    The demo uses held-out log likelihood and shows both sides of it. On 2000 training rows against 2000 held out, the curve peaks at the true \(k = 2\) (\(-14.316\), \(-8.627\), \(-8.631\), \(-8.632\) for \(k = 1 \ldots 4\)). On 400 training rows it peaks at \(k = 3\) by \(10^{-3}\) — with that little data the sample tail is not yet spherical enough for the correct model to win.

    Missing data changes the M-step, not just the E-step #

    For a row with observed set \(O_i\), the model says

    \[x_{O_i} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu_{O_i},\; W_{O_i} W_{O_i}^\top + \sigma^2 I_{|O_i|}\right), \qquad (14)\]

    with \(W_{O_i}\) the rows of \(W\) in \(O_i\). The E-step is (7) with \(W\) replaced by \(W_{O_i}\) and \(M_i = W_{O_i}^\top W_{O_i} + \sigma^2 I_k\), which is one \(k \times k\) system per row. The M-step for W is where the structure changes: each row \(j\) of \(W\) has its own normal equations, over the rows where feature \(j\) is present,

    \[W_j = \left[ \sum_{i \in O_j} (x_{ij} - \mu_j) E[z_i]^\top \right] \left[ \sum_{i \in O_j} E[z_i z_i^\top] \right]^{-1}, \qquad (15)\]

    because the inner product that produced the shared system no longer exists. And \(\sigma^2\) normalises by the number of observed cells: a missing cell contributes \(E[\epsilon^2] = \sigma^2\) to the objective, a term that does not depend on \(W\) and so does not move the optimum.

    Filling the NaNs with column means and running ordinary PPCA is not the same estimator, and the difference is not small. Mean imputation shrinks each column's variance by the missing fraction and biases every correlation towards zero; on this demo's data it recovers the hidden entries with RMSE 1.962 against 0.522 for the conditional mean, and the model that comes out of it is not the one that generated the data.

    A mixture: weighted versions of the same three equations #

    With responsibilities \(r_{ic} \propto \pi_c\,\mathcal{N}(x_i; \mu_c, C_c)\),

    \[W_c = \left[ \sum_i r_{ic} (x_i - \mu_c) E[z_i^{(c)}]^\top \right]\left[ \sum_i r_{ic} E[z_i^{(c)} z_i^{(c)\top}] \right]^{-1}, \qquad (16)\]

    and likewise \(\mu_c\) is the responsibility-weighted mean, \(\pi_c\) the mean responsibility, and \(\sigma_c^2\) gets (10) with every sum weighted by \(r_{ic}\). The only genuinely new ingredient is the initialisation, and it is where mixtures actually fail: two centres drawn uniformly at random can land in the same cluster, both components then fit the same half of the data, and EM has no symmetry-breaking mechanism to escape — it converges to "everything is 50/50" and stops. The demo's earlier uniform initialisation did exactly that, at 53% purity, on data that a quadratic discriminant separates perfectly.

    Code walkthrough #

    PPCA(n_components, variant=..., n_mixtures=..., max_iter=..., tol=..., seed=...) with fit, transform (\(E[z \mid x]\)), reconstruct (\(E[x \mid x_{\text{obs}}]\)), fit_transform, score_samples, sample, and predict_proba for the mixture. W_ is \((d, k)\) — latent to observed — and components_ is its transpose, the same orientation as pca.PCA.components_, so the two atoms can be read side by side. covariance_ is \(C\); for the mixture the per-component tuples are means_, Ws_, sigma2s_ and weights_, and the single-model attributes stay unset.

    1. The density, without ever touching a \(d \times d\) matrix #

        M = W.T @ W + sigma2 * np.eye(k)
    _, logdet_M = np.linalg.slogdet(M)
    logdet = (d - k) * math.log(sigma2) + logdet_M
    P = Xc @ W                                   # (n, k)
    quad = (np.sum(Xc * Xc, axis=1) - np.sum((P @ np.linalg.inv(M)) * P, axis=1)) / sigma2
    return -0.5 * (d * math.log(2.0 * math.pi) + logdet + quad)

    That is (5) and (6) with P = Xc @ W standing in for \(W^\top x\) for every row at once. slogdet rather than log(det(...)) because \(\lvert C \rvert\) for \(d = 50\) and \(\sigma^2 = 0.01\) is \(10^{-100}\): the determinant underflows to exactly zero in floating point and the likelihood becomes -inf for reasons that have nothing to do with the fit.

    Note what is not here: no \(d \times d\) matrix, no Cholesky factor, no inverse of \(C\). The whole model is expressed through \(k \times k\) objects, and that is the form in which PPCA scales.

    2. The EM loop #

                M = W.T @ W + sigma2 * np.eye(k)
            Minv = np.linalg.inv(M)
            Ez = Xc @ W @ Minv                          # (n, k)
            Covz = sigma2 * Minv                        # same for every row
            Ezz = Ez.T @ Ez + n * Covz                  # Σ_i E[z_i z_iᵀ]
            W_new = np.linalg.solve(Ezz, Ez.T @ Xc).T   # (d, k)
            resid = Xc - Ez @ W_new.T
            # Covz is already σ²M⁻¹, so the posterior spread enters once
            ssr = (float(np.sum(resid * resid))
                   + n * float(np.trace(Covz @ (W_new.T @ W_new))))
            sigma2_new = max(ssr / (n * d), 1e-12)

    Line by line this is (7), (9), (8) and (10). Three details carry the derivation. Ezz = Ez.T @ Ez + n * Covz is the sum of second moments — the n * is there because the covariance is shared across rows but the sum is not. np.linalg.solve with Ez.T @ Xc transposed afterwards solves for \(W^\top\) rather than \(W\), which avoids forming an inverse and keeps the \((k,k)\) system on the left. And the comment on ssr is load-bearing: Covz already equals \(\sigma^2 M^{-1}\), so the trace enters once. Writing n * sigma2 * trace(...) — the natural reading of "posterior spread times \(\sigma^2\)" — silently changes the fixed point, and the only symptom is that EM and the closed form disagree at the third digit.

    3. The closed form, which is also the oracle #

            lam, V = _top_eigs(Xc.T @ Xc / n, k)
        sigma2 = float(lam[k:].mean()) if d > k else 0.0
        self.W_ = V[:, :k] * np.sqrt(np.maximum(lam[:k] - sigma2, 0.0))
        self.sigma2_ = max(sigma2, 1e-12)

    Xc.T @ Xc / n is the biased covariance. Using \(n - 1\) is the standard sample-covariance convention and it is wrong here: the likelihood is a function of the second moment about the fitted mean, so dividing by \(n - 1\) makes the closed form disagree with EM. On the demo's 600 rows that is a factor of 0.998 in \(\sigma^2\) — small, systematic, and exactly the kind of discrepancy that makes someone go looking for a bug in EM.

    np.maximum(lam[:k] - sigma2, 0.0) guards the case where a retained eigenvalue is smaller than the noise level; that happens when \(k\) is too large for the data, and the unclipped value would put a nan in W via the square root.

    4. Conditioning on the observed block, batched #

            M = sigma2 * np.eye(k) + np.einsum("dj,id,dk->ijk", W, m, W)
        Minv = np.linalg.inv(M)                        # (n, k, k)
        P = Xf @ W                                     # Wᵀ x_i, on observed coords
        Ez = np.einsum("ijk,ik->ij", Minv, P)
        Cov = sigma2 * Minv

    With a per-row observed mask m, the system is \(M_i = W^\top \mathrm{diag}(m_i) W + \sigma^2 I\), and einsum("dj,id,dk->ijk", W, m, W) reads exactly that: sum over features \(d\), keep row \(i\), land on the \(j,k\) block. np.linalg.inv on an \((n,k,k)\) stack is a batched inverse, so the whole E-step is three array operations rather than \(n\) Python iterations.

    Xf is the centred data with the missing entries set to zero, which is what makes P = Xf @ W equal \(W_{O_i}^\top (x_{O_i} - \mu_{O_i})\) without any slicing: the missing coordinates contribute exactly \(0 \cdot W_j = 0\). The same M then gives the observed log density

            logdet = np.log(sigma2) * (nobs - k) + logdet_M
        quad = (np.sum(Xf * Xf, axis=1) - np.einsum("ij,ik,ijk->i", P, P, Minv)) / sigma2
        logp = -0.5 * (nobs * math.log(2.0 * math.pi) + logdet + quad)

    which is (5) and (6) with \(d\) replaced by the per-row observed count nobs. That one substitution is the whole of "condition on the observed block": a row with 3 of 8 coordinates observed is a 3-dimensional Gaussian with the corresponding \(3 \times 3\) marginal, and its density must not be diluted by the 5 coordinates nobody measured.

    The batching is not cosmetic. The straightforward per-row loop took 10.9 s for this demo's missing-data fit, 371 iterations of \(n = 600\) small inverses; the batched version takes 0.67 s for the same answer — 16 times faster, and the reason the whole demo runs in under a second.

    5. The missing-data M-step has one system per feature #

                for j in range(d):
                rows = mask[:, j]
                if not rows.any():
                    continue                     # never observed: W_j stays put
                Aj = second[rows].sum(axis=0)
                bj = ((X[rows, j] - mean[j])[:, None] * Ez[rows]).sum(axis=0)
                W_new[j] = np.linalg.solve(Aj, bj)
                resid = X[rows, j] - mean[j] - Ez[rows] @ W_new[j]
                ssr += float(resid @ resid) + float(np.sum(W_new[j] @ Cov[rows] @ W_new[j]))

    This is (15). second is the per-row \(E[z_i z_i^\top]\) from (9), and rows is the set of rows where feature \(j\) exists — the thing that cannot be shared, since two features generally have different missing patterns. Aj is the sum of second moments over those rows only; using all rows instead is the single easiest way to get a confidently wrong W row for a feature with many gaps.

    The last line accumulates both halves of the residual, exactly as in (10): the squared error on observed cells and the posterior spread \(W_j \mathrm{Cov}(z_i) W_j^\top\). if not rows.any(): continue leaves an entirely unobserved feature's loading at its initialisation rather than dividing by zero — honest, since there is no information about it in the data.

    Measured on 14% hidden entries: the fit recovers \(\sigma^2 = 0.1587\) against 0.1583 for the complete matrix, and the true loading subspace to 0.026 rad, in 371 iterations.

    6. The two conditionals, and the difference between them #

            Ez, _ = self._posterior(X, self.W_, self.sigma2_, self.mean_)
        return self.mean_ + Ez @ self.W_.T

    transform returns Ez — the position on the plane, shape \((m, k)\). reconstruct returns mean_ + Ez @ W_.T — the position and the plane, shape \((m, d)\), with the missing coordinates filled in by their conditional mean.

    That second line is the denoiser from the introduction. Because \(P = W(W^\top W + \sigma^2 I)^{-1}W^\top\) has eigenvalues \(\lambda_j/(\lambda_j + \sigma^2)\) near 1 on the retained directions and 0 elsewhere, the residual operator \(I - P\) kills the noise inside the subspace and passes the rest through unchanged. The demo verifies both halves: the mean squared error is exactly \(\mathrm{tr}((I-P)^2 S)\) — a plumbing identity that pins the arithmetic — and it equals \((d - k)\sigma^2 = 0.950\) to within a percent, which is the statistical statement.

    7. A mixture needs a symmetry-breaking start #

            centres = [X[rng.integers(n)]]
        for _ in range(m - 1):
            gap = np.min([((X - c) ** 2).sum(axis=1) for c in centres], axis=0)
            centres.append(X[rng.choice(n, p=gap / gap.sum())])

    k-means++ seeding, followed by ten Lloyd iterations before the first EM step. Each new centre is drawn with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest existing centre, so the second one lands far away by construction. Two uniform draws — the obvious initialisation — put both centres in the same cluster about half the time when the clusters are unbalanced, and when that happens the responsibilities come out near \(1/m\) for every row and nothing downstream can recover.

                    A = (Ez.T * resp[:, c]) @ Ez + Nc[c] * Cov
                Wc = np.linalg.solve(A, ((xc * resp[:, c][:, None]).T @ Ez).T).T
                resid = xc - Ez @ Wc.T
                ssr = (float(np.sum(resp[:, c] * np.sum(resid * resid, axis=1)))
                       + Nc[c] * float(np.trace(Cov @ (Wc.T @ Wc))))

    (16) with the responsibility as a weight. (Ez.T * resp[:, c]) @ Ez is \(E[Z]^\top \mathrm{diag}(r_c) E[Z]\), and Nc[c] * Cov is Cov scaled by the component's effective sample size — the mixture's stand-in for the n * Covz in the single-model M-step. The ssr line is (10) with the per-row squared error weighted by resp[:, c], which is what makes \(\sigma_c^2\) the noise within a component rather than across the mixture.

    8. Sampling, and the check it enables #

            Z = rng.standard_normal((n, self.n_components))
        return (self.mean_ + Z @ self.W_.T
                + math.sqrt(self.sigma2_) * rng.standard_normal((n, self.W_.shape[0])))

    (1) literally: draw the latent, push it through the plane, add the fog. Having this is what makes the model falsifiable in a way PCA is not — the demo draws 20000 rows and compares their sample covariance against \(C\). That comparison has to be made on the right scale: a covariance entry has Monte Carlo error \(\sqrt{(C_{ij}^2 + C_{ii}C_{jj})/n}\), which is 0.02 on the demo's 25.0 entry and 0.001 on its 0.16 ones. A single absolute tolerance either passes everything or fails on the largest entry for no reason; normalising by \(\sqrt{C_{ii}C_{jj}}\) puts every entry on the same footing, and 20000 draws land within 1.9%.

    9. The refusals that keep the API honest #

            if has_nan and self.variant != "missing_data":
            raise ValueError(
                f"{self.variant} needs a complete matrix; variant='missing_data' is the "
                f"one that conditions on the observed block, and it treats NaN as missing")

    A NaN reaching the closed form produces a nan covariance and a nan likelihood that compares False against everything, so the model silently "converges" on the first iteration and reports loglik_ = nan. Failing at fit with the name of the variant that handles it is the difference between a five-minute fix and an afternoon.

            if self.variant == "mixture_ppca":
            raise NotImplementedError("a mixture has one covariance per component")

    A mixture has no single \(\mu\), \(W\) or \(\sigma^2\), so covariance_, components_, reconstruct and sample raise for it rather than returning the largest component's parameters — which would be a plausible-looking number answering a question nobody asked. The per-component lists are there for anyone who wants them.

    Complexity #

    \(n\) rows, \(d\) features, \(k\) components, \(T\) iterations, \(m\) mixture components.

    Variant Time Per-iteration dominant term Memory
    ppca_em \(O(T(ndk + dk^2 + k^3))\) Xc @ W @ Minv \(O(nd + dk)\)
    ppca_closed \(O(nd\min(n,d) + dk^2)\) one eigendecomposition \(O(nd + d^2)\)
    missing_data \(O(T(ndk + nk^3 + dk^3))\) the batched \((n,k,k)\) inverse \(O(nd + nk^2)\)
    mixture_ppca \(O(Tm(ndk + dk^2 + k^3))\) \(m\) densities per E-step \(O(nd + mdk)\)
    _logpdf \(O(ndk + k^3)\) Xc @ W \(O(nk + k^2)\)

    Three notes. The closed form's one eigendecomposition beats \(T\) EM iterations on a complete matrix, which is why ppca_closed exists and why it is the fastest route to the same optimum; EM's advantage is that it survives missing data and mixtures, where no eigendecomposition is available. missing_data is more expensive than its \(O(ndk)\) suggests because the \((n,k,k)\) posterior covariance tensor is materialised — at \(k = 20\) and \(n = 10^5\) that is 320 MB, so it is the one variant where \(k\) has to stay small or the loop has to be chunked. And the cost of mixture_ppca is linear in \(m\) with no sharing, because each component has its own centre and its own eigenbasis.

    Failure modes #

    1. W is not identified; C is. For any orthogonal \(R\), \((WR)(WR)^\top = WW^\top\), so the loadings are only defined up to a rotation — infinite solutions with identical likelihood. The demo asserts the invariance to \(10^{-10}\) and checks the subspace against PCA's (\(2.1 \times 10^{-8}\) rad). Two consequences: never compare W entries across fits, seeds or packages, because they will differ; and if you need comparable loadings, align them (Procrustes) or canonicalise them (varimax or similar, which is factor_analysis's job).

    2. The in-sample likelihood cannot choose \(k\). It is non-decreasing by construction, and \(k = d\) sends it to infinity. The demo's numbers show the other trap: held-out selection works but needs data. On 400 training rows the peak was at \(k = 3\) by \(10^{-3}\) on data whose true rank is 2; on 2000 rows the correct model wins by \(0.007\). Reporting "the model chose \(k\)" from a small sample is reporting noise.

    3. \(\sigma^2\) collapses at the boundary. When \(k \ge d\) or \(n \le d\) the fit wants \(\sigma^2 = 0\), and then \(\log \sigma^2 \to -\infty\), logdet is -inf, and every comparison against another model is meaningless. The guard in the code (max(..., 1e-12)) keeps the arithmetic finite but does not make the model valid. Check k < min(n, d) and treat a \(\sigma^2\) at the floor as "this model is too big for this data", not as a very good fit.

    4. Mean imputation is a different estimator. Filling NaNs with column means shrinks variance by the missing fraction, biases every correlation towards zero, and destroys exactly the low-rank structure PPCA is looking for. The demo's numbers: RMSE 1.962 for imputation against 0.522 for the conditional mean. The same applies to "fit on complete rows only", which throws away all the partially observed ones and biases the sample towards its easy part.

    5. Missing-not-at-random is not covered. The E-step conditions on the observed values, which is valid when the missingness is independent of the unobserved value (MCAR, or MAR given the model). If a sensor drops out because the reading was extreme, the conditional mean is biased and no amount of EM fixes it — that is a model of the missingness process, which PPCA does not have. All-missing rows fall back to the prior (\(E[z] = 0\)), which is the right answer and also a silent one; count them before trusting the fit.

    6. Mixtures have local optima, label switching and \(m\) more parameters. The demo's uniform initialisation stalled at 53% purity on data that is 100% separable, and the fixed version reaches 100%. Component labels are arbitrary — the demo matches them before scoring, because "component 0" means nothing across runs. And \(\sigma_c^2 \to 0\) on a component with few points is the classic singularity: the likelihood diverges while the model becomes useless. A minimum noise floor per component is not a hack; it is the constraint that makes the likelihood bounded.

    7. The biased covariance is the right one. \(n - 1\) is the convention for estimating a covariance and the wrong divisor for this likelihood: it is exactly a factor of \(n/(n-1)\) in \(\sigma^2\), 0.17% at \(n = 600\), and it shows up as a small standing disagreement between EM and the closed form that is easy to misdiagnose as a bug in either.

    8. Isotropic noise is a strong assumption. PPCA says the noise is the same in every direction. Factor analysis says it is \(\Psi = \mathrm{diag}(\psi_1, \ldots, \psi_d)\), which is one parameter per feature; if the features have different units or different measurement quality, PPCA's single \(\sigma^2\) is an average that fits neither, and the loadings absorb the mismatch. The symptom is a residual covariance that is visibly non-spherical at \(k = d - 1\). PPCA is factor analysis with \(\Psi = \sigma^2 I\) — one noise parameter instead of \(d\) — so choosing it is a statement about the noise, not a simplification.

    Applications #

    • Missing data imputation. The conditional mean \(E[x_{\text{miss}} \mid x_{\text{obs}}]\) is the reason missing_data exists; it is the low-rank imputer behind a lot of "fill the gaps in the sensor matrix" tooling, and it degrades gracefully as the missing fraction grows.
    • Collaborative filtering. The user-item rating matrix is sparse and roughly low rank; PPCA is the probabilistic version of the truncated-SVD baseline, and its \(\sigma^2\) is the "how noisy are ratings" parameter that the plain SVD baseline has to set by hand.
    • Denoising and compression. Reconstruction is a denoiser whose error is exactly \((d - k)\sigma^2\) — image patches, spectra, sensor arrays. The rank \(k\) is a tunable knob with a likelihood attached, instead of a guess.
    • Anomaly detection. score_samples is a density, so a row far from the subspace and far along it both score low; the Mahalanobis-style decomposition into "distance off the plane" and "distance along it" is standard in process monitoring.
    • Model comparison as a building block. Because the model has a likelihood, it composes: mixtures of PPCAs, PPCA with a prior on \(W\), PPCA inside a larger graphical model all need the marginal density that PCA cannot provide.
    • Nonlinear limits. Replace \(W z\) with \(f(z)\) for a GP and the same marginal likelihood gives the Gaussian process latent variable model; PPCA is its linear special case, which is why the GP-LVM literature starts here.
    • Subspace identification. In control, the state-space model \(x_{t+1} = A x_t + w_t\), \(y_t = C x_t + v_t\) has a stationary covariance of exactly the form (2) plus a second factor; PPCA and its two-block cousin CCA are the static versions of the subspace methods used to identify such systems.

    Where PPCA is a trap: reading \(\sigma^2\) as measurement noise when it is really everything the plane does not explain, including the tail of a spectrum that was never noise. And treating the loadings as interpretable factors — they are identified only up to rotation, and the rotation that makes them interpretable is a separate problem (factor_analysis's varimax, or a sparse prior).
