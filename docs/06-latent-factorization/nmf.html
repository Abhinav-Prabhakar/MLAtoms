    Non-negative matrix factorisation

    Intuition #

    Two matrices multiply to a data matrix. The only constraint is that all three are non-negative:

    \[X \approx WH, \qquad X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}_{\ge 0},\quad W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}_{\ge 0},\quad H \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times d}_{\ge 0}. \qquad (1)\]

    That looks like a small tweak on the SVD, and it changes the answer completely. The SVD minimises the same squared error without the constraint, and its solution is the truncated SVD — signed, orthogonal, and cancelling. Allow subtraction and the first component can be a contrast, feature_a − feature_b, which is a perfectly good direction for explaining variance and a meaningless one for describing what the data is made of.

    Remove the option to subtract and each row of \(X\) becomes a sum of the rows of \(H\), weighted by the corresponding row of \(W\):

    \[x_i \approx \sum_{c=1}^{k} W_{ic}\, h_c, \qquad W_{ic} \ge 0. \qquad (2)\]

    Nothing can cancel, so every part pays its own way. This is the whole reason the method is used: on a face dataset the rows of \(H\) come out as eyes, a nose, a mouth, and each face is a weighted sum of the parts at their positions; on a document-term matrix they come out as topics, and each document is a mixture.

            X          ≈        W         H
   ┌─────────┐          ┌───┐   ┌───────────────┐
   │ 0 1 1 0 │  = 0.9 ·  │ ▪ │ ⊗ │ ████          │  ∧ part 1
   │ 1 0 0 1 │  + 0.4 ·  │ ▪ │   │         ████  │  ∧ part 2
   │ 0 1 0 1 │  + 0.7 ·  │ ▪ │   │      ████     │  ∧ part 3
   └─────────┘          └───┘   └───────────────┘
      data               codes        parts

    The same picture explains the failure mode: if a part never fires, no multiplicative update can start it firing (zero times anything is zero). Parts are switched on, never grown from nothing, which is why initialisation is a real part of the algorithm and not a formality.

    The objective also has to be chosen, and the choice is about variance, not taste. For counts with variance equal to the mean, squared error is the wrong geometry; the right one is the generalised Kullback–Leibler divergence:

    \[D_{KL}(X \,\|\, WH) = \sum_{ij} \left( X_{ij}\log\frac{X_{ij}}{(WH)_{ij}} - X_{ij} + (WH)_{ij} \right). \qquad (3)\]

    Both objectives are non-convex in \((W,H)\) jointly, both have simple monotone updates, and both have the same two ambiguities — scale and permutation — which is what makes the output interpretable and the parameters unidentifiable at the same time.

    Variants #

    Variant Objective Update Exact? When
    frobenius_mu \(|X-WH|_F^2\) multiplicative monotone, sublinear the default
    kl_mu \(D_{KL}(X|WH)\) multiplicative monotone, sublinear counts, rates, intensities
    als \(|X-WH|_F^2\) exact solve per factor alternating, no monotonicity proof small \(k\), fast per sweep
    hals \(|X-WH|_F^2\) coordinate descent monotone, fast the practical default
    sparse_nmf Frobenius + L0 screening threshold the parts not an objective, a constraint interpretable supports
    online_nmf Frobenius, minibatches decayed sufficient statistics stochastic \(n\) too large for memory
    semi_nmf \(|X-WH|_F^2\) Ding's square-root rule monotone in \(H\) signed \(X\), non-negative basis

    frobenius_mu is Lee and Seung's original pair of updates. Each factor is updated by multiplying it by the ratio of the negative to the positive part of the gradient, which is non-negative by construction and therefore needs no projection step. The demo checks that the loss falls at every sweep and that it lands on the same value as the other two routes.

    kl_mu is the same idea for the divergence (3). The update is not the Frobenius one with a different loss plugged in — the denominator is a sum of \(W\) rather than a product with \(H\), which is what makes it the right generalisation, and it reduces to the Frobenius update only in the small-residual limit.

    als alternates exact least-squares solves: with \(H\) fixed, the optimal \(W\) is \(XH^\top(HH^\top)^{-1}\), and the non-negativity is restored by clipping. The clip is not the constrained optimum, so there is no monotonicity guarantee; in practice it converges faster per sweep than MU and needs the same regularisation.

    hals is coordinate descent taken literally: hold all of \(W\) and \(H\) fixed except one column of \(W\) or one row of \(H\), and solve that exactly. Because the objective is quadratic, each such solve is one division, and because the subproblem is one-dimensional with a positivity constraint, the solution is the unconstrained one clipped at zero. It is monotone, has no step size, and — the reason it is used — it converges in tens of sweeps where MU needs hundreds.

    sparse_nmf switches off loadings below a fraction of their part's maximum. This is a projection, not a penalty, and the distinction matters: the L1 route on \(H\) alone is scale-unbounded and drifts to a degenerate solution (the demo measures it — \(\|H\|\) collapses by six orders of magnitude while the fit improves, because \(H \to 0\), \(W \to \infty\) with \(WH\) fixed is free).

    online_nmf replaces the dataset with sufficient statistics. Keep \(A = \sum_i w_i w_i^\top\) and \(B = \sum_i x_i w_i^\top\) over minibatches with exponential forgetting, and update \(H\) from those. Memory is \(O(kd)\) instead of \(O(nd)\), and the demo's 16-row-per-step fit is within 1% of the batch answer on 200 rows.

    semi_nmf drops the constraint on \(W\) and keeps it on \(H\): signed weights over non-negative parts. That is a different model class — a conical combination with negative coefficients — and it fits data that non-negative NMF cannot represent at all, such as a similarity matrix with negative entries.

    Derivation #

    Multiplicative updates come from an auxiliary function #

    Take the Frobenius objective with \(W\) fixed, and write \(h\) for one row of \(H\):

    \[F(h) = \|X - WH\|_F^2. \qquad (4)\]

    Its gradient is \(\nabla F = 2\,W^\top(WH - X)\), which splits into a non-negative and a non-positive part: \(W^\top WH \ge 0\) and \(W^\top X \ge 0\). The descent direction is \(-\nabla F\), and a gradient step would need a step size to stay non-negative. Lee and Seung's observation is that the ratio of the two parts is a valid step of exactly the right size:

    \[H \leftarrow H \odot \frac{W^\top X}{W^\top W H}, \qquad (5)\]

    which is non-negative for any \(W \ge 0\), \(X \ge 0\), and \(H > 0\). The proof of monotonicity is an auxiliary-function argument: \(F\) is majorised by \(G(h, h^t)\) built from \(W^\top W H\) at the current point, \(G(h^t,h^t) = F(h^t)\), and minimising \(G\) in closed form gives exactly (5). Two consequences follow.

    • Every fixed point of (5) satisfies the KKT conditions of the constrained problem, because at a fixed point \(W^\top X = W^\top W H\), which is the stationarity condition with a non-negative multiplier.
    • The convergence rate is sublinear, and slow. This is not a defect of the implementation; MU takes hundreds of sweeps because it is a majorise–minimise scheme with a very loose majoriser.

    The \(W\) update is (5) transposed, using \(X H^\top\) and \(W H H^\top\).

    The KL update is the same manoeuvre with a different split #

    For (3) with \(W\) fixed, the gradient is \(\nabla = \mathbf{1} - W^\top (X / WH)\) where the division is elementwise. The non-negative part is \(W^\top(X/WH)\) and the non-positive part is the constant row vector \(\mathbf{1}\) times \(W\) column sums, giving

    \[H \leftarrow H \odot \frac{W^\top (X / WH)}{W^\top \mathbf{1}}, \qquad (6)\]

    and the \(W\) update is its transpose. The structure is identical — a ratio, not a difference — which is why both fits are monotone and both are slow.

    The choice between them is a statement about the noise. If \(X_{ij}\) is a count, its variance is its mean, so the squared error over-weights the large entries; the KL divergence is the Bregman divergence whose Hessian is \(1/(WH)\), which is the variance of a Poisson. The demo makes the difference visible: on Poisson counts, factors fitted by KL have KL divergence 7911 against 8030 for factors fitted by Frobenius — a 1.5% difference that is consistent across seeds and would be larger on sparser counts.

    Coordinate descent: one row at a time #

    HALS applies the obvious idea. Write the objective as a sum over parts:

    \[\|X - WH\|_F^2 = \left\| R_c - w_c h_c \right\|_F^2 + \text{const}, \qquad R_c = X - \sum_{j \ne c} w_j h_j. \qquad (7)\]

    Minimising over \(h_c\) with \(w_c\) fixed is a one-dimensional least-squares problem in each of the \(d\) entries, because \(w_c h_c\) is rank one:

    \[h_c \leftarrow \max\left(0,\ h_c + \frac{w_c^\top R_c}{\|w_c\|^2}\right), \qquad w_c \leftarrow \max\left(0,\ w_c + \frac{R_c h_c^\top}{\|h_c\|^2}\right). \qquad (8)\]

    Both are exact, both are cheap (\(O(nd)\) per part), and the update to \(R_c\) after each is a rank-one correction, so a full sweep is \(O(ndk)\) with no matrix inversion. The clipping is exact rather than a projection: the subproblem is one-dimensional and the unconstrained optimum is the optimum when positive.

    The demo's numbers: after 50 sweeps HALS is at 0.0743 relative error where MU is at 0.0866, and at 400 sweeps all three sit between 0.0741 and 0.0745 — the noise floor of that construction, since the data was built with 1% noise.

    NNDSVD: start where the SVD wants to be #

    Random non-negative initialisation works, but it seeds the zero-locking problem and it wastes sweeps. NNDSVD takes the first singular triple and, for each subsequent one, keeps whichever of the positive or negative parts carries more energy:

    \[x = u_j,\quad y = v_j, \qquad \sigma^+ = \|x^+\|\,\|y^+\|, \quad \sigma^- = \|x^-\|\,\|y^-\|, \qquad (9)\]

    then sets \(w_j = \sqrt{s_j \sigma}\, \hat{x}\), \(h_j = \sqrt{s_j \sigma}\, \hat{y}\) for the winning sign. Because each \(s_j\) multiplies the retained fraction, the result reproduces the SVD's energy ordering and is non-negative everywhere.

    Two details are load-bearing and both are in the code. Entries that come out as exactly zero must be seeded — under (5) a zero is absorbing — but the seed has to be small relative to that component, not borrowed from component 1, or a weak component gets inflated by a factor of four and NNDSVD ends up worse than noise. With the proportional seed the init costs 1.31 in Frobenius norm on the demo's data and reaches 0.68 after ten sweeps, against 5.03 and 1.02 for the borrowed seed.

    Online: the objective you can actually compute #

    With \(n\) too large to hold, minimise the expected loss over the empirical distribution instead. Mairal's scheme keeps

    \[A_t = (1-\rho)A_{t-1} + \rho\, \tilde{W}_t^\top \tilde{W}_t, \qquad B_t = (1-\rho)B_{t-1} + \rho\, X_t^\top \tilde{W}_t, \qquad (10)\]

    where \(\tilde{W}_t\) are the codes of the current minibatch (with \(H\) fixed, so it is a linear least-squares problem per batch). The basis is then updated with the same multiplicative rule, using the statistics in place of the full matrix:

    \[H \leftarrow H \odot \frac{B_t^\top}{A_t H}. \qquad (11)\]

    Note the orientation: \(A\) is \(k \times k\) and \(B\) is \(d \times k\), so both are independent of \(n\). Forgetting (\(\rho < 1\)) is what makes the scheme able to track a drifting basis, and it is also the only hyper-parameter that matters: \(\rho = 1\) is plain stochastic descent on the current batch, \(\rho \to 0\) freezes the basis.

    Semi-NMF: splitting the sign #

    Drop the constraint on \(W\) and the objective is still quadratic; the trick is that every matrix splits into a non-negative and a non-positive part, \(A = A^+ - A^-\) with \(A^\pm \ge 0\), so the same ratio construction applies with a square root because the update is now multiplicative in both directions:

    \[H \leftarrow H \odot \sqrt{\frac{(W^\top X)^+ + (W^\top W)^- H}{(W^\top X)^- + (W^\top W)^+ H}}. \qquad (12)\]

    Why a square root? For a signed \(W\) the cross term \(W^\top W H\) can point either way, so the gradient split is no longer into two non-negative matrices and the linear ratio can go negative. Ding, Li and Jordan's derivation uses a majoriser that is a geometric mean of the two terms, and the square root is what makes it a valid majoriser. The \(W\) update is an exact least-squares solve, because with \(H\) fixed there is no constraint to project onto.

    Code walkthrough #

    NMF(n_components, variant=..., seed=..., lam=..., init=..., n_iter=..., batch_size=..., forget=...) is the surface: fit, transform (codes for new rows against the fixed basis), inverse_transform, fit_transform, and loss_curve_ for the diagnostic. W_ is \((n,k)\) and components_ is \((k,d)\) — the same convention as sklearn.decomposition.NMF, chosen so that transform(X) and inverse_transform(codes) compose to the identity on the fitted subspace.

    1. The two Frobenius updates #

        def _update_h(self, X, W, H) -> np.ndarray:
        return np.maximum(H * (W.T @ X) / np.maximum(W.T @ W @ H, EPS), EPS)

    def _update_w(self, X, W, H) -> np.ndarray:
        num = X @ H.T
        den = W @ H @ H.T
        return np.maximum(W * num / np.maximum(den, EPS), EPS)

    That is (5) and its transpose, and the np.maximum(..., EPS) does double duty: it is the guard against a zero denominator, and it is what stops an entry that has been driven to zero from becoming nan on the next pass. EPS = 1e-12 is deliberately not a "small number" in the numerical-analysis sense — the entries here are \(O(10^{-1})\), so it behaves as an exact zero and the demo's sparsity count (< 1e-10) treats it as one.

    2. The KL updates, where the denominator is a sum #

        def _update_h_kl(self, X, W, H) -> np.ndarray:
        ratio = X / np.maximum(W @ H, EPS)
        return np.maximum(H * (W.T @ ratio) / np.maximum(W.sum(axis=0)[:, None], EPS), EPS)

    X / WH is the elementwise ratio of data to model; W.T @ ratio is its non-negative gradient part; and W.sum(axis=0) is \(W^\top\mathbf{1}\) — the sum down the columns of \(W\), broadcast across features. The tell that this is the KL update and not the Frobenius one is that the denominator does not involve \(H\).

    The np.maximum(W @ H, EPS) is not cosmetic. A count matrix has exact zeros, and X / 0 is inf, one inf spoils the whole product, and inf * 0 is nan; the clip keeps \(X_{ij}/(WH)_{ij}\) at a large finite number instead, which the subsequent multiplication by \(H\) recovers.

    3. Exact solves, and the projection that is not an optimum #

        def _solve_w(self, X, H, nonneg: bool = True) -> np.ndarray:
        G = H @ H.T + 1e-10 * np.eye(H.shape[0])
        W = np.linalg.solve(G, H @ X.T).T
        return np.maximum(W, EPS) if nonneg else W

    This is the \(W\) half of ALS: with \(H\) fixed, minimise over \(W\) and get \(XH^\top(HH^\top)^{-1}\). Three details. The solve is written transposed (solve(G, H @ X.T).T) because G is symmetric and it avoids forming an inverse. The 1e-10 * np.eye ridge is what keeps a rank-deficient \(H\) — a part that has died — from making the system singular. And np.maximum(W, EPS) is a projection, not the constrained optimum: the true non-negative least-squares solution needs an active-set method, and clipping is what makes als the one Frobenius variant with no monotonicity guarantee.

    The nonneg=False path is semi_nmf, where \(W\) may be signed, so the unclipped solution is the right answer and the ridge is the only regularisation.

    The loss that is reported in loss_curve_ is a separate function, because the KL variant has to be measured in its own divergence:

        def _loss(self, X) -> float:
        if self.variant == "kl_mu":
            R = X / np.maximum(self.W_ @ self.components_, EPS)
            return float(np.sum(X * np.log(np.maximum(R, EPS)) - X + self.W_ @ self.components_))
        return float(np.linalg.norm(X - self.W_ @ self.components_) ** 2)

    The np.maximum(..., EPS) inside the log matters for the same reason it did in the update: a zero count against a zero model entry would otherwise produce log(0) and a nan that silently poisons the monotonicity check the demo runs over the whole curve.

    4. HALS, and the sign that has to be right #

            for c in range(W.shape[1]):
            denom = float(H[c] @ H[c])
            if denom < EPS:                          # a dead component stays dead
                continue
            w_old = W[:, c].copy()
            W[:, c] = np.maximum(W[:, c] + (R @ H[c]) / denom, EPS)
            # R is X − WH, so raising column c *subtracts* its contribution:
            # R += outer(w_old − w_new, H[c]). Getting this sign wrong makes the
            # textbook HALS look like a divergent algorithm.
            R += np.outer(w_old - W[:, c], H[c])

    R is the residual \(X - WH\), maintained incrementally so that a sweep costs \(O(ndk)\) rather than \(k\) full recomputations. The subtlety is the direction: raising column \(c\) of \(W\) reduces the residual, so the correction to \(R\) has the sign of \(w_{old} - w_{new}\). Written the other way round — which is how the identity is usually printed, with the residual defined as \(WH - X\) — HALS looks like it oscillates between two losses and a component dies on the first sweep. That is exactly what happened here before the sign was fixed, and it produced a final loss eleven times worse than MU with every individual step looking plausible.

    The denom < EPS guard is for a component whose row of \(H\) is entirely zero: the division would be by \(10^{-12}\) and the column would explode. Skipping it keeps the component dead, which is the honest behaviour — a part with no loading anywhere cannot be updated to have one, under either update rule.

    5. Initialisation, and the seed that must not be borrowed #

            if mp >= mn:
            u, v, sigma = xp / max(np.linalg.norm(xp), EPS), yp / max(np.linalg.norm(yp), EPS), mp
        else:
            u, v, sigma = xn / max(np.linalg.norm(xn), EPS), yn / max(np.linalg.norm(yn), EPS), mn
        scale = math.sqrt(max(s[j] * sigma, 0.0))
        W[:, j], H[j, :] = scale * u, scale * v
        # NNDSVDa: a zero entry locks for ever under a multiplicative update,
        # so seed it. The seed has to be small compared with *this* component —
        # borrowing component 1's mean inflates a weak component by 4x here,
        # which is how NNDSVD turns into a worse starting point than noise
        W[W[:, j] == 0.0, j] = 1e-2 * W[:, j].max()
        H[j, H[j, :] == 0.0] = 1e-2 * H[j].max()

    This is (9). mp and mn are the energies of the two sign choices, and picking the larger is what keeps the component's contribution as close to \(s_j\) as the non-negativity allows. The seed line is the difference between a useful initialisation and a harmful one: with W[:, 0].mean() — the published NNDSVDa recipe, which assumes components of comparable scale — a weak component's zeros were filled with values four times its own loading, and the init loss was 5.03 against a data norm of 4.25. Starting from that, ten sweeps got to 1.02; with the proportional seed they get to 0.68.

    The demo does not rely on the initialisation for its conclusions: every "reaches the same place" assertion holds with init="random" too, and the agreement between MU, ALS and HALS is a statement about the objective, not about the start.

    6. Screening, which is a projection #

            if v in ("frobenius_mu", "sparse_nmf"):
            H = self._update_h(X, W, H)
            W = self._update_w(X, W, H)
            if v == "sparse_nmf":
                # a loading below lam × its part's largest is supported by
                # another part; switch it off. Because a zero is absorbing
                # under a multiplicative update, the support only shrinks.
                cut = self.lam * H.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
                H[cut > H] = 0.0

    keepdims=True is what makes cut a column vector, so each part is thresholded against its own maximum instead of against the global one — otherwise a part with small overall magnitude is deleted wholesale the moment another part dominates. The threshold is applied after the MU step and before the next one, so the update that follows sees a sparse \(H\) and reallocates mass onto the surviving entries. The demo's fit is 69% zeros with support 19/19/18 against a true 20 per part, at 1.16× the dense reconstruction error; the cost is a real one and is reported rather than hidden.

    7. Online: two statistics and a decay #

                rho = self.forget
            A = (1.0 - rho) * A + rho * (Wb.T @ Wb)
            B = (1.0 - rho) * B + rho * (Xb.T @ Wb)
            H = np.maximum(H * (B.T / np.maximum(A @ H, EPS)), EPS)

    Three lines that are (10) and (11). A accumulates the Gram matrix of the minibatch codes and B accumulates their cross-product with the data, so B.T / (A @ H) is the ratio of the gradient parts for the whole dataset, not just the batch. The codes Wb are themselves fitted against the current \(H\) by 30 MU steps on the batch alone, which is the linear subproblem.

    forget is the only knob that matters. The demo uses 0.3 with 16 rows per step and lands 0.6% below the batch answer — below, not equal, because a decayed average over minibatches weights recent rows more and behaves slightly like a bootstrapped ensemble.

    8. Semi-NMF, and why the square root #

        def _update_h_semi(self, X, W, H) -> np.ndarray:
        WtX = W.T @ X
        WtW = W.T @ W
        # positive and negative parts: A = A⁺ − A⁻ with A⁺, A⁻ ≥ 0
        num = 0.5 * (np.abs(WtX) + WtX) + 0.5 * (np.abs(WtW) - WtW) @ H
        den = 0.5 * (np.abs(WtX) - WtX) + 0.5 * (np.abs(WtW) + WtW) @ H
        return np.maximum(H * np.sqrt(np.maximum(num, EPS) / np.maximum(den, EPS)), EPS)

    0.5*(|A| + A) is \(A^+\) and 0.5*(|A| - A) is \(A^-\); writing them this way keeps the code to two lines instead of four temporaries. Note that the sign split is taken on \(W^\top W\) as well as on \(W^\top X\) — that is the whole point of the variant, since a signed \(W\) makes the Gram matrix indefinite and the cross term can subtract. The outer np.sqrt is (12).

    Because \(W\) is solved exactly rather than updated multiplicatively, this variant's sweep is _solve_w followed by one pass of (12). The demo's fit on data with signed weights reaches 0.016 relative error, which is the noise level; the two ways of forcing ordinary NMF onto the same data — clipping the negatives, or shifting everything up — cost 0.72 and 0.25.

    9. The two refusals that keep the API honest #

            if self.variant != "semi_nmf" and X.min() < 0.0:
            raise ValueError(f"{self.variant} needs a non-negative X; use semi_nmf for "
                             f"signed data, or shift the data and record the offset")

    A negative entry in \(X\) makes the objective unbounded below for the non-negative variants: with \(W\) free to grow, a negative \(x_{ij}\) can be explained by driving one factor negative and the other positive, which the constraint forbids — so the optimiser is being asked for something that does not exist. The error names the alternative, because the failure at run time is a nan in the loss curve three hundred sweeps later.

        def transform(self, X) -> np.ndarray:
        """Codes for new rows against the learned basis: `(m, k)`."""
        if self.W_ is None:
            raise RuntimeError("call fit before transform")
        X = self._check(X)
        if self.variant == "semi_nmf":
            return self._solve_w(X, self.components_, nonneg=False)
        W = np.full((X.shape[0], self.n_components), 1.0 / self.n_components)
        for _ in range(200):
            W = self._update_w(X, W, self.components_)
        return W

    transform refits only the codes, holding components_ fixed: that is the difference between projecting new rows onto a learned basis and refitting the model, and it is the only defensible way to score held-out data. The initial 1/k is a constant start rather than random, so transform is deterministic given the fit — for semi_nmf there is nothing to iterate, since the codes are an unconstrained least-squares solve.

    Complexity #

    \(n\) rows, \(d\) columns, \(k\) components, \(T\) sweeps, \(b\) rows per minibatch.

    Variant Time Memory Dominant term
    frobenius_mu \(O(Tndk)\) \(O(nd + k(n+d))\) two matrix products per sweep
    kl_mu \(O(Tndk)\) \(O(nd)\) the ratio \(X/WH\), recomputed
    als \(O(T(nk^2 + k^3 + dk^2))\) \(O(nd)\) the \(k \times k\) solve
    hals \(O(Tndk)\) \(O(nd)\) rank-one residual updates
    sparse_nmf \(O(Tndk)\) \(O(nd)\) MU plus a threshold
    online_nmf \(O(Tbk^2 + Tbk d)\) \(O(kd + bk)\) batch codes and \(B\)
    semi_nmf \(O(T(nk^2 + k^3))\) \(O(nd)\) the exact \(W\) solve
    _nndsvd \(O(nd\min(n,d))\) \(O(nd)\) one SVD

    Two notes. als looks cheap per sweep and is not: \(HH^\top\) is \(k \times k\) but forming \(XH^\top\) is \(O(ndk)\), the same as MU, and the \(k \times k\) inverse is in the noise only because \(k\) is small. And the \(O(nd)\) memory of every batch variant is the point of online_nmf: at \(n = 10^7\) and \(d = 10^4\) the matrix is 800 GB, while \(A\) and \(B\) are \(10^8\) floats — 0.8 GB — and that is the whole algorithm.

    Failure modes #

    1. Zero-locking, and the part that never fires. Under (5) an entry that is zero stays zero for ever. So a part initialised at zero never appears, and a part driven to zero by a bad sweep is dead. The symptom is a components_ row that is exactly zero while the reconstruction error is far above the noise floor. The diagnostic is np.sum(components_ == 0, axis=1); the fixes are a better initialisation (NNDSVD), a random restart, or re-seeding the dead row.

    2. Scale ambiguity: the factorisation is not unique. For any positive diagonal \(D\), \((WD)(D^{-1}H) = WH\), so a part can be scaled up and its codes down with no change to the fit. Nothing is wrong with this — the demo asserts it to \(10^{-12}\) — but it means loadings are not comparable across components without normalising, and "part 2 has 40% of the mass" is a statement about the parametrisation unless the parts are normalised first. Normalise (rows of \(H\) to unit norm, columns of \(W\) scaled up) before reading anything off.

    3. Permutation, and the ranking that is not there. Components come out in whatever order the initialisation and the numerical path produce; the demo's three parts come out in reverse. There is no canonical order, so any comparison across fits (a bootstrap, a cross-validation, two seeds) needs a matching step — Hungarian, or cosine similarity against a reference — or the "component 1" of the two fits will be different objects.

    4. Rank selection. \(k\) is a hyper-parameter and the objective is non-increasing in it, so more components always fit better. There is no information criterion that is not ad hoc here; the usable diagnostics are a held-out reconstruction error, the stability of the components across bootstraps, or — for the sparse variant — the number of parts you can name. Setting \(k\) to the true rank and reporting an exact fit is a test of the code, not of the model.

    5. The L1 penalty on one factor is not a well-posed objective. The demo measures it: 200 sweeps take \(\|H\|\) from 2.41 to \(7\times 10^{-6}\) and \(\|W\|\) from 2.5 to \(10^{6}\), with the fit improving from 1.02 to 0.36. The reason is that \(\|X - WH\|^2 + \lambda\|H\|_1\) has no minimiser when \(W\) is free — the infimum is reached by \(H \to 0\), \(W \to \infty\) at fixed product. Penalise both factors, normalise one of them, or use the screening projection; do not tune \(\lambda\) against a metric, because the metric will be satisfied.

    6. KL on data that is not counts. The divergence (3) requires \(X\) and \(WH\) to be positive and has its scale set by the data. Applying it to standardised features (which have negative entries) is a type error, and applying it to counts with a very different magnitude per column silently weights the large columns entirely. If the data is continuous and positive — intensities, rates — KL is still defensible; if it is mean-centred, it is not.

    7. Online forgetting that forgets the wrong thing. \(\rho\) weights recent minibatches. Too small and the basis never moves (the statistics are dominated by the initial transient); too large and it tracks noise. The diagnostic is the batch loss on a held-out block over time: it should fall and then stay flat, not fall and rise. The demo's 0.3 with 16-row batches is a choice that works at \(n = 200\); at \(n = 10^7\) the same \(\rho\) would be far too fast.

    8. Semi-NMF's sign convention is easy to get backwards. The constraint is on the basis, not the codes: \(W\) signed, \(H \ge 0\). Data whose true model is "signed basis, positive codes" is not semi-NMF data, and semi-NMF will fit it badly no matter how long it runs — the demo's earlier version did exactly that and converged to 0.20 relative error on data with an exact rank-3 representation.

    Applications #

    • Topic models and document-term matrices. The rows of \(H\) are topics — non-negative distributions over words — and the rows of \(W\) are the mixtures. pLSI and LDA add a probabilistic model on top; NMF gets most of the interpretability with none of the inference.
    • Hyperspectral unmixing. Each pixel is a mixture of endmember spectra, and the mixing proportions are non-negative by physics. NMF with a sum-to-one constraint on \(W\) recovers the endmembers and their abundances, which is the canonical remote-sensing use.
    • Audio source separation. A magnitude spectrogram is non-negative, and its rows of \(H\) are spectral templates of notes or instruments; \(W\) is their activation over time. kl_mu is the right divergence here (the spectrogram is a magnitude, the KL is the Itakura–Saito-adjacent loss), and sparse_nmf is what makes the templates look like single notes.
    • Face parts and image patches. Lee and Seung's original demonstration: the parts come out as noses, eyes and mouths, where PCA's components come out as whole-face contrasts. The reason is (2) — a part can only add.
    • Community detection on networks. The adjacency matrix, or its symmetrised non-negative version, factorises into overlapping communities, with \(W\) giving membership and \(H\) giving the block structure. Overlap is the point: NMF assigns a node to several communities with weights, unlike a partition.
    • Recommender systems with implicit feedback. A user-item interaction matrix of counts is non-negative and sparse; the factorisation is the classical baseline that matrix factorisation with explicit ratings replaced.
    • Semi-NMF on similarity matrices. Cosine or correlation similarity has negative entries, and its clusters are non-negative parts; semi-NMF is the variant that fits the signed matrix directly rather than clipping it.

    Where NMF is a trap: reading the parts as a unique decomposition. Scale and permutation are exact symmetries, and even after fixing them the decomposition is not identified — different initialisations find different local optima with the same loss and different parts. If the parts have to be comparable across fits, the non-negativity is not enough on its own; you need separability assumptions (each part has a feature only it uses) or a stability check across restarts.
