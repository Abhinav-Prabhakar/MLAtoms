    06 · Latent structure & factorisation/tier 2/5 variants/source

    Canonical correlation

    Intuition #

    Two data sets, the same \(n\) objects. Maybe the same patients with gene expression in one matrix and methylation in another; the same images with pixels and with captions; the same time points with two different sensor arrays. You want the one direction in each view along which the two views agree most.

            X (n × p)                  Y (n × q)
        ┌────────┐                 ┌────────┐
   rows │ · · ·  │                 │ · · ·  │     the same n objects
        │ · · ·  │                 │ · · ·  │     different measurements
        └───┬────┘                 └───┬────┘
            │ w_x                      │ w_y
            ▼                          ▼
        u = X w_x  ──── corr ────  v = Y w_y
                  ρ = max

    That is the whole problem. It is a maximisation over \(p + q\) numbers of a single cosine, and unlike almost everything else in this chapter it has a closed form: a generalised eigenvalue problem, solved by an SVD after whitening each view.

    The reason it deserves an atom of its own is what the answer means. CCA does not return coordinates; it returns directions in the span of each view. Apply any invertible linear map to \(X\) — rotate it, scale its columns, mix them — or any shift, and the canonical correlations are unchanged, because the map does not change the span and the answer was never about the coordinates. The demo asserts this to \(2.8 \times 10^{-14}\): the fitted correlation moves in the fourteenth digit when each view is multiplied by a random invertible matrix and shifted.

    That invariance is the whole difference between CCA and regression:

    asks invariant to
    regression \(y \sim X\) which of \(X\)'s columns predict \(y\) nothing — the columns are the model
    PCA which directions carry variance in one view rotation, not scaling
    CCA which directions two views share any invertible linear map

    The other side of the same coin: the weights are only defined up to that map. A coefficient of \(0.4\) in weights_[0] means nothing on its own, and a sparse CCA's support is a statement about the coordinates you happened to supply. That is fine when the coordinates are genes with names and wrong when they are the first 30 principal components of something.

    The four remaining variants are all responses to one failure, and the failure is worth stating precisely because it is not a numerical accident. The objective maximises over \(p + q\) parameters subject to two constraints. Once \(p + q \ge n\) there are enough degrees of freedom for the two centred views to intersect, and then a correlation of exactly \(1\) is attainable between views that are, in fact, independent. The demo measures it: two \(40 \times 20\) matrices of pure noise give \(\hat\rho = 1.000000\). Ridge it, sparsify it, kernelise it, or add views — those are the four standard answers, and they fail in four different ways.

    Variants #

    Variant What it does Cost Where it wins
    cca whiten both views, SVD the cross-covariance \(O(n(p^2+q^2))\) the answer, when \(n\) is large
    regularized_cca add \(\lambda I\) to each within-view covariance same \(p\) comparable to \(n\); held-out correlation
    kernel_cca CCA in a feature space given by a Gram matrix \(O(n^3)\) relations that are not linear
    sparse_cca L1 penalty on the weights, alternating exact lasso steps \(O(p^2)\) per inner sweep when a direction must be a short list
    multi_view one shared variate for \(D \ge 2\) views (SUMCOR) \(O(D n p^2)\) more than two views

    cca is Hotelling's 1936 problem, and "canonical" means the same thing it means for a basis: the components come out ordered and mutually orthogonal under each view's own metric. The demo's planted problem has 20 columns per view, a 5-column true direction in each, and a population correlation of \(0.90\); the fit returns \(0.9122\) with cosine \(0.964\) and \(0.949\) to the two planted directions. Getting the direction right to three digits out of five non-zero coefficients is what 400 samples buy at that noise level, and it is the number to compare every other variant against.

    regularized_cca is the ridge, and it is the variant most often used without being named. On 40 training rows of a planted \(0.60\) signal it reads \(1.00\) training and \(0.02\) held-out; at \(\lambda = 0.3\), \(0.91\) and \(0.37\); at \(\lambda = 3\), \(0.80\) and \(0.47\). Both curves are monotone, which is the entire trade in one line: the ridge gives up the quantity it maximises in exchange for one it does not. The held-out number still falls short of the true \(0.60\) — the ridge stops 40 rows from lying, it does not give them information.

    kernel_cca replaces \(X\) and \(Y\) with Gram matrices. On the same planted data with a linear kernel it reproduces cca to \(2 \times 10^{-9}\) in correlation and \(1.00000\) cosine between the variates, which is the dual derivation checked against the primal one. On a circle and its double angle — \(x = (\cos t, \sin t)\), \(y = (\cos 2t, \sin 2t)\), a deterministic quadratic relation — linear CCA finds \(0.11\) on 100 training points and \(0.00\) held out, while the RBF kernel finds \(0.90\) and \(0.79\). The kernel adds no information; it changes the space in which a linear direction is a nonlinear function.

    sparse_cca puts an L1 penalty on the weights and solves each half-step exactly. At \(\lambda = 0\) the fit is bit-for-bit cca (\(1.000000\) cosine, same correlation to four decimals), which is the correct sanity check and one that most implementations fail. At \(\lambda = 0.02\) on 800 rows it keeps 10 of 30 coordinates per view and 4 of the 5 that are real, at \(0.9069\) against the dense \(0.9124\); at \(\lambda = 0.05\) it keeps 4 and hits 4, at \(0.9035\); at \(\lambda = 0.2\) every coordinate is thresholded away and the solver raises rather than returning a zero vector.

    multi_view generalises to \(D\) views by asking every view's variate to agree with one shared \(z\) rather than pairwise. With two views it reduces to cca exactly (\(1.3 \times 10^{-13}\) apart). With three — one shared latent, noise \(0.5\), \(0.6\) and \(2.5\) — the joint correlation is \(0.40\) and the individual correlations with the latent are \(0.83\), \(0.83\) and \(0.21\). That \(0.40\) is not an average over the views, it is the mean of the three pairwise numbers (\(0.71\), \(0.26\), \(0.22\)), which is what the shared-variate objective maximises: one bad view costs a third of the score without destroying the good pair.

    Derivation #

    1. The objective is a correlation, and correlations are scale-free #

    Write \(\Sigma_{xx}\), \(\Sigma_{yy}\) for the within-view covariances and \(\Sigma_{xy}\) for the cross-covariance, all computed on centred data. For directions \(w_x \in \mathbb{R}^p\) and \(w_y \in \mathbb{R}^q\) the correlation of the variates is

    \[\rho(w_x, w_y) = \frac{w_x^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{xy} w_y}{\sqrt{w_x^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{xx} w_x}\;\sqrt{w_y^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{yy} w_y}}. \qquad (1)\]

    It is invariant to rescaling either \(w\) by a positive constant, so the maximum is a direction and not a point. Fixing the scale of each:

    \[\max_{w_x,\,w_y}\; w_x^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{xy} w_y \quad \text{subject to} \quad w_x^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{xx} w_x = 1,\;\; w_y^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{yy} w_y = 1. \qquad (2)\]

    The constraints are not cosmetic. They are \(\Sigma\)-orthonormality, and they are what makes the successive solutions orthogonal in the right metric, exactly as the eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix are orthogonal in the Euclidean one.

    2. The generalised eigenproblem #

    The Lagrangian of (2) is \(w_x^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{xy}w_y - \tfrac{\rho}{2}(w_x^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{xx}w_x - 1) - \tfrac{\rho}{2}(w_y^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{yy}w_y - 1)\). Stationarity gives two equations that must hold simultaneously,

    \[\Sigma_{xy} w_y = \rho\,\Sigma_{xx} w_x, \qquad \Sigma_{yx} w_x = \rho\,\Sigma_{yy} w_y. \qquad (3)\]

    Eliminating \(w_y\) from the second and substituting into the first,

    \[\Sigma_{xy}\Sigma_{yy}^{-1}\Sigma_{yx}\, w_x = \rho^2\,\Sigma_{xx}\, w_x, \qquad (4)\]

    a generalised symmetric eigenproblem with \(\min(p,q)\) solutions. The eigenvalues are the squared canonical correlations, ordered; the eigenvectors are the canonical directions. It is a generalised problem, not an ordinary one, because the metric on each side is \(\Sigma_{xx}\), and it is symmetric because \(\Sigma_{xy}\Sigma_{yy}^{-1}\Sigma_{yx}\) is a product of a matrix with its own transpose in that metric.

    Solving (4) directly is a bad idea: \(\Sigma_{yy}^{-1}\) destroys symmetry and conditioning, and the standard dense eigensolver is \(O(p^3)\) with no structure used. There is a two-line route that does better and, more importantly, makes the geometry obvious.

    3. Whiten, then SVD #

    Let \(\Sigma_{xx} = L_xL_x^{\mathsf T}\) be a Cholesky factor and define the whitened views

    \[X_w = X_c L_x^{-\mathsf T}, \qquad Y_w = Y_c L_y^{-\mathsf T}, \qquad (5)\]

    so that \(X_w^{\mathsf T}X_w/(n-1) = I\) and likewise for \(Y_w\): every column now has unit variance and they are uncorrelated. Substituting \(w_x = L_x^{-\mathsf T}u\) and \(w_y = L_y^{-\mathsf T}v\) into (1) turns the constraints into \(\lVert u\rVert = \lVert v\rVert = 1\) and the objective into

    \[\rho = \frac{u^{\mathsf T} C\, v}{\lVert u\rVert\,\lVert v\rVert}, \qquad C = \frac{1}{n-1} X_w^{\mathsf T} Y_w = L_x^{-1}\Sigma_{xy}L_y^{-\mathsf T}. \qquad (6)\]

    Maximising a bilinear form over the unit sphere is the definition of the leading singular pair, so the canonical correlations are the singular values of \(C\), the canonical directions are \(w_x = L_x^{-\mathsf T}u\), and one SVD returns all \(\min(p,q)\) components at once. No deflation, no iteration, no ordering logic.

    This is the route the code takes, and it is the one Golub and Zha recommend: the Cholesky is \(O(p^3/3)\), the SVD of a \(p\times q\) matrix is \(O(pq\min(p,q))\), and no covariance is ever inverted. The price is that \(\Sigma_{xx}\) must be positive definite, which it is not when \(p \ge n\) — that is §7, and it is why _whiten refuses rather than letting cholesky fail on its own.

    4. Why whitening makes the invariance visible #

    Two things fall out of (6) that are hard to see in (4).

    The correlations are properties of the whitened views. \(X_w\) and \(Y_w\) are canonical representatives of their spans: any two bases of the same subspace differ by an invertible map, and whitening is the map that fixes the representative. So the answer depends on the row space of \(X\) and nothing else. Replacing \(X \mapsto XA + \mathbf{1}b^{\mathsf T}\) gives \(X_w \mapsto X_w U\) for some orthogonal \(U\) — a rotation of the canonical coordinates — whose singular values are unchanged. The demo's \(2.8\times10^{-14}\) is that statement executed.

    A ridge is not invariant, and cannot be. Adding \(\lambda I\) to \(\Sigma_{xx}\) picks a coordinate system: the ridge penalises directions according to their variance in the units you supplied. It does not commute with a linear map, so the invariance survives only to the size of the ridge. The demo's gap is \(3\times10^{-10}\) at \(\lambda = 10^{-6}\) and \(3\times10^{-6}\) at \(\lambda = 10^{-4}\) — small, monotone in \(\lambda\), and the honest way to report it. Any claim that regularised CCA is "invariant" is a claim about a problem that has stopped being CCA.

    5. Ridge: where to put it and what it does #

    _whiten adds \(\lambda\) to the covariance before the Cholesky, so (4) becomes

    \[\Sigma_{xy}\left(\Sigma_{yy} + \lambda I\right)^{-1}\Sigma_{yx}\, w_x = \rho^2\left(\Sigma_{xx} + \lambda I\right)w_x. \qquad (7)\]

    The important part is what this is not. It is not "compute CCA, then shrink the correlations"; the directions move too. It is not a Bayesian prior with a posterior; it is a numerical statement that directions of small within-view variance are not trustworthy, which is the same trade as ridge regression and has the same justification. For cca the ridge is a crutch — \(10^{-6}\) is enough to keep cholesky happy on nearly singular data, and small enough that the invariance gap is \(10^{-9}\). For regularized_cca it is the model, and the demo's sweep is the evidence: the number the fit reports and the number it generalises to are different numbers, and only the second one is worth optimising.

    6. Kernel CCA: the same problem in a feature space #

    Map each row to \(\varphi(x_i)\) in a feature space \(\mathcal{H}\) and ask (2) there. The solution is a linear combination of the mapped training points — the usual representer argument — so \(w_x = \Phi^{\mathsf T}\alpha\). Substituting, with \(K_x = \Phi\Phi^{\mathsf T}\) the \(n\times n\) Gram matrix,

    \[w_x^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{xx}w_x \propto \alpha^{\mathsf T}K_x^2\alpha, \qquad w_x^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{xy}w_y \propto \alpha^{\mathsf T}K_xK_y\beta, \qquad (8)\]

    and the regularised problem — regularised in the only place the kernel formulation can be — is

    \[\max_{\alpha,\beta}\; \alpha^{\mathsf T}K_xK_y\beta \quad \text{subject to}\quad \alpha^{\mathsf T}(K_x^2 + \kappa K_x)\alpha = 1,\;\; \beta^{\mathsf T}(K_y^2 + \kappa K_y)\beta = 1. \qquad (9)\]

    Now substitute \(\alpha = S_x^{-1}u\) with \(S_x = (K_x^2 + \kappa K_x)^{1/2}\). The constraint becomes \(\lVert u\rVert = 1\) and the objective becomes \(u^{\mathsf T}F_xF_yv\) with

    \[F_x = S_x^{-1}K_x = K_x^{1/2}\left(K_x + \kappa I\right)^{-1/2}, \qquad (10)\]

    so the canonical correlations are the singular values of \(F_xF_y\), the training variates are \(F_xu\) and \(F_yv\), and the dual coefficients are \(S_x^{-1}u\). The code computes exactly (10) and stores \(\alpha\), which is why transform cannot use \(x\,w\) — see the walkthrough.

    The direction of the shrink in (10) is the part that goes wrong. \(F_x\) has eigenvalues \(\sqrt{w/(w+\kappa)}\): eigenvalues of \(K\) that are large survive, small ones are driven to zero. The naive reading of "ridge" — add \(\kappa\) to the eigenvalues of \(K\) before taking a square root — leaves \(n - \mathrm{rank}(K)\) directions of size \(\sqrt{\kappa}\), each whitened to unit variance by the next step, each carrying pure noise. That version trains to a correlation of \(1\) and generalises to nothing; the difference between it and (10) is a factor of \(w/(w+\kappa)\) versus \(w + \kappa\) applied to the same eigendecomposition, and it is invisible in the fit's own numbers.

    7. Why \(p + q \ge n\) is fatal, not merely bad #

    The centred \(X\) defines a linear map \(\mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}^n\) whose image is a subspace of dimension \(\min(p, n-1)\). Similarly for \(Y\). If the two images intersect in a non-zero vector, then some \(w_x, w_y\) satisfy \(X w_x = Y w_y\) and \(\rho = 1\) exactly. Two subspaces of dimensions \(p'\) and \(q'\) inside an \((n-1)\)-dimensional space must intersect once \(p' + q' > n - 1\), i.e. once \(p + q \ge n\) (for \(p, q \le n-1\)). So the maximum is attained by an algebraic identity, not by any shared structure.

    This is the same phenomenon as \(R^2 = 1\) from a regression with more features than rows, and the demo makes it concrete: \(40 \times 20\) of noise plus \(40 \times 20\) of noise gives \(1.000000\), and \(40 \times 10\) gives \(0.845\) — not a smaller effect but the same effect partially diluted. The pattern is entirely the ratio: at \(n = 60\) with 30 columns each it is \(1.000000\) again, and with 20 each \(0.908\). With \(\mathrm{reg} > 0\) the problem is no longer singular, and the ridge at \(10^{-8}\) is enough to reproduce the same \(1.000000\), which is worth knowing: a tiny ridge does not protect you here, because the ridge changes the answer by less than the sampling error it is supposed to fix.

    8. Sparse CCA: an exact lasso half-step #

    Write the penalised problem for one component as a two-block maximisation with \(\ell_1\) budgets, and hold \(v\) fixed. The problem in \(u\) is

    \[\min_{u}\; \tfrac{1}{2}u^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{xx}u - u^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{xy}v + \lambda_u\lVert u\rVert_1 \qquad (11)\]

    followed by a rescaling to \(u^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{xx}u = 1\). At \(\lambda_u = 0\) the minimiser is \(u = \Sigma_{xx}^{-1}\Sigma_{xy}v\), which is precisely the stationarity equation (3) with \(v\) fixed — that is why \(\lambda = 0\) reproduces cca and why the alternating scheme is CCA's power iteration rather than a different algorithm.

    (11) is a lasso with a \(p \times p\) Gram matrix, so cyclic coordinate descent is exact and needs no step size. Coordinate \(j\), with \(S = \Sigma_{xx}\):

    \[u_j \leftarrow \frac{1}{S_{jj}}\,\mathcal{S}\!\left(c_j - \sum_{k\ne j} S_{jk}u_k,\;\lambda_u\right), \qquad c = \Sigma_{xy}v, \qquad (12)\]

    where \(\mathcal{S}(z,\lambda) = \mathrm{sign}(z)\max(|z| - \lambda, 0)\). The optimality conditions are worth stating because they are what "sparse" means here:

    \[u_j \ne 0 \;\Rightarrow\; (Su)_j - c_j = -\lambda_u\,\mathrm{sign}(u_j), \qquad u_j = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; |(Su)_j - c_j| \le \lambda_u. \qquad (13)\]

    A coordinate is zero exactly when its partial residual is smaller than the penalty. Two consequences the demo measures: the support is a property of the standardised coordinates (the code divides each column by its standard deviation before the solve, so \(\lambda\) means the same thing across features of different units), and \(\lambda\) has to be read against the scale of \(c\), which grows with \(n\) — \(0.02\) is an interesting penalty at \(n = 800\) and \(0.2\) annihilates the solution, while at \(n = 400\) the interesting range is \(0.01\)–\(0.05\). There is no scale-free \(\lambda\).

    The deflation step is what makes the second component meaningful: after convergence the code subtracts \(\hat\rho\,\hat u\hat v^{\mathsf T}\) from the cross-covariance, which removes the found pair from the problem and leaves the next one. This is greedy — the components are not jointly optimal, and the support of component 2 depends on what component 1 found — which is true of every sparse multi-component method in use.

    9. More than two views: SUMCOR, and why it collapses to CCA at \(D = 2\) #

    For \(D\) views there is no single "the" canonical correlation, because there is no single pair. Kettenring's 1971 survey lists five criteria — SUMCOR, MAXVAR, SSQCOR, MINVAR and GENVAR — which differ in what they do with the \(D\) block correlations; all five agree at \(D = 2\) and disagree above it. This file implements SUMCOR: one shared variate \(z\), and the sum of the correlations between \(z\) and each view's variate maximised,

    \[\max_{z,\,w_1,\dots,w_D}\; \sum_{i=1}^{D} \mathrm{corr}(z,\, X_iw_i) \quad\text{subject to}\quad w_i^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{ii}w_i = 1. \qquad (14)\]

    Given \(z\), each \(w_i\) is a regression in the \(\Sigma_{ii}\) metric, \(w_i \propto \Sigma_{ii}^{-1}X_i^{\mathsf T}z\) rescaled to the constraint; given the \(w_i\), the best \(z\) is the mean of the unit-variance variates. Both steps are exact, so the loop is coordinate ascent on (14), and at \(D=2\) its fixed point is (3) — hence the \(10^{-13}\) agreement with cca.

    The reported correlations_ is the mean pairwise correlation of the fitted variates, which is not (14)'s value but is a function of it. With \(\lVert z\rVert = 1\) the objective is \(\sum_i z^{\mathsf T}s_i/\lVert s_i\rVert\), maximised by \(z \propto \sum_i s_i/\lVert s_i\rVert\), so

    \[\sum_i \mathrm{corr}(z, s_i) = \Bigl\lVert \sum_i s_i/\lVert s_i\rVert \Bigr\rVert = \sqrt{D + D(D-1)\,J},\]

    where \(J\) is the mean pairwise correlation the fit reports. The demo's three-view fit satisfies the identity to six decimals, \(2.318165\) on both sides: the number being maximised and the number being reported determine each other, so nothing is lost by reporting the mean.

    The deflation removes \(z\) from every view at once, \(C_i \leftarrow C_i - z(z^{\mathsf T}C_i)\), which is what keeps component \(c+1\) orthogonal to component \(c\) in all views simultaneously.

    (14) also has a one-step solution: concatenate the views, let \(R\) be the covariance and \(D\) its block diagonal, and take the leading eigenvector of \(D^{-1}R\). The demo computes it and checks the loop against it, block by block: cosine \(0.994\), \(0.995\) and \(1.000\). The directions are what match — the eigenvector normalises the blocks jointly while the loop gives each unit variance — and the disagreement in the third digit is that difference in scaling, not an optimisation gap.

    SUMCOR's weakness is the one the demo measures: the objective is an average over views, so a view that shares nothing drags the joint number down without being ignored (\(0.40\) against \(0.71\) for the good pair). MAXVAR or SSQCOR would weight it differently, and none is "correct" — the choice is a modelling statement about whether a bad view is noise to be averaged out or a view to be discarded.

    Code walkthrough #

    CCA(n_components=1, *, variant="cca", reg=1e-6, lam_u=0.0, lam_v=0.0, kernel="linear", gamma=None, n_iter=300, tol=1e-9, seed=0), with fit, transform, fit_transform, score, and the attributes weights_ (a list of (p_i, k) blocks — (n, k) for a kernel fit, where they are dual coefficients), correlations_ \((k,)\), mean_, n_iter_.

    fit(X, Y) for two views, fit([X1, X2, ...]) for multi_view. weights_ is a list even for two views because a kernel fit stores dual coefficients rather than primal weights, and because multi_view has one block per view; making the shape uniform removes a special case from every caller.

    1. Whitening, and the one refusal that matters #

        n, p = Xc.shape
    cov = Xc.T @ Xc / max(n - 1, 1)
    if reg <= 0.0 and p > n - 1:
        raise ValueError(
            f"a view with {p} columns and {n} rows has a singular covariance; "
            f"unregularised CCA needs n > p — use regularized_cca with reg > 0"
        )
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(cov + reg * np.eye(p))
    Xw = np.linalg.solve(L, Xc.T).T                    # n × p, XwᵀXw ≈ I
    return L, Xw

    (5). np.linalg.solve(L, Xc.T).T computes \(X_cL^{-\mathsf T}\) without forming an inverse — the solve is triangular and costs \(O(np^2)\), the same as the covariance it follows. The alternative, Xc @ inv(L).T, is the same arithmetic with an explicit inverse and worse conditioning; it is the first thing to fix in a CCA implementation that "works on small data".

    The refusal is deliberate and is not a try/except around cholesky. cholesky fails only when the matrix is numerically indefinite, which for cov + 0·I on wide data is not guaranteed — a \(40\times60\) covariance has rank 39 in exact arithmetic but rounding can leave a tiny positive eigenvalue, and then the code proceeds with a whitening that amplifies rounding into the answer. The explicit test states the condition the mathematics requires, and the message names the remedy, which is the only useful thing an exception can do.

    2. The closed form, in six lines #

                Lx, Xw = _whiten(m, self.reg)
            Ly, Yw = _whiten(n, self.reg)
            P, s, Qt = np.linalg.svd(Xw.T @ Yw / max(X.shape[0] - 1, 1))
            k = self.n_components
            wx = np.linalg.solve(Lx.T, P[:, :k])
            wy = np.linalg.solve(Ly.T, Qt.T[:, :k])
            rho = s[:k]

    (6), with every piece in the order the derivation gives it. Xw.T @ Yw is \(C\) up to the \(n-1\); svd returns \(P\), \(s\), \(Q^{\mathsf T}\) with \(s\) sorted descending, so [:k] is "the first \(k\) canonical components" with no further sorting. The weights are \(w = L^{-\mathsf T}u\), i.e. solve(Lx.T, u): Lx.T is upper triangular, so this is a back-substitution and not a matrix inversion.

    Qt.T[:, :k] deserves a second look: np.linalg.svd returns \(Q^{\mathsf T}\), so the right singular vectors are the columns of Qt.T, which is why the slice is Qt.T[:, :k] and not Qt[:k]. Writing Qt[:k] transposes the answer and gives a fit that is wrong in a way that still runs — one of the two classic CCA bugs, the other being \(L^{-1}\) instead of \(L^{-\mathsf T}\).

    3. Measuring the correlation rather than trusting the SVD #

            if U.shape[0] < 3:
            return np.asarray(fallback, dtype=float)
        return np.array([abs(float(np.corrcoef(U[:, j], V[:, j])[0, 1]))
                         for j in range(U.shape[1])])

    The singular values of \(C\) are the canonical correlations only when (5)'s whitening is exact, and with a ridge it is not. The discrepancy is the size of the ridge, so trusting s makes the primal path report a correlation that is \(8.7\times10^{-7}\) away from the variates' own at \(\mathrm{reg}=10^{-6}\), and \(8.7\times10^{-5}\) at \(10^{-4}\) — and it is enough to make two variants that should agree to machine precision look like different estimators. Measuring the correlation of the fitted variates costs two matrix products and makes every variant comparable with every other by construction. This is also why correlations_ is always in-sample: it is the thing that was maximised, and score is the only honest number in the file.

    The U.shape[0] < 3 branch exists because transform can be called on a single row, where corrcoef is undefined; it falls back to the singular value, which is the right limit.

    4. The lasso half-step #

        u = np.zeros(S.shape[0])
    d = np.diag(S)
    for _ in range(500):
        u_old = u.copy()
        for j in range(S.shape[0]):
            partial = c[j] - (S[j] @ u - d[j] * u[j])
            u[j] = _soft(partial, lam) / d[j]
        if float(np.linalg.norm(u - u_old)) < 1e-12 * (1.0 + float(np.linalg.norm(u_old))):
            break
    if np.all(u == 0.0):
        raise ValueError("every coordinate was thresholded away — λ is too large")
    return u

    (12), and the reason sparse CCA is implementable without a convex solver. S[j] @ u is the full matrix-vector product recomputed for every coordinate, which is \(O(p)\) per coordinate and \(O(p^2)\) per sweep — the naive form, and the right one at the sizes this file handles. The standard optimisation is to maintain \(Su\) incrementally: subtract the old contribution and add the new, \(O(1)\) per coordinate. That is a factor of \(p\) and it is what a production implementation does; it is also six more lines and one more place to introduce a bug that converges to the wrong answer, which is why it is not here.

    partial is the partial residual \(c_j - \sum_{k\ne j}S_{jk}u_k\), written as c[j] - (S[j] @ u - d[j] * u[j]) because the diagonal term is already included in S[j] @ u. d = np.diag(S) is hoisted out of the loop and is safe: S has a ridge on its diagonal, so \(S_{jj} > 0\) always and the division cannot blow up.

    The convergence test is relative to 1 + ‖u_old‖ rather than absolute, because the scale of \(u\) is problem-dependent and S⁻¹c can be large. And the all-zero exit is a feature: at large \(\lambda\) the true minimiser is exactly \(0\), and returning it would make _unit_in divide by zero and produce a NaN correlation that propagates silently. Raising names the cause.

    5. The alternating loop, and the constraint everyone forgets #

                for _ in range(self.n_iter):
                u = _unit_in(Sxx, _lasso(Sxx, Sxy @ v, self.lam_u))
                v_new = _unit_in(Syy, _lasso(Syy, Sxy.T @ u, self.lam_v))
                self.n_iter_ += 1
                if float(np.linalg.norm(v_new - v)) < self.tol:
                    v = v_new
                    break
                v = v_new
            u = _unit_in(Sxx, _lasso(Sxx, Sxy @ v, self.lam_u))

    (11) twice per iteration. Each half-step solves its lasso and then rescales so that \(u^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{xx}u = 1\):

        return u / math.sqrt(max(float(u @ S @ u), EPS))

    Without it both vectors shrink geometrically. On the demo's data the norms fall from \(0.21\) to \(0.037\) in six steps at \(\lambda = 0\), and at \(\lambda = 0.02\) they reach zero and the lasso raises. The correlation is scale-free, so the direction survives for a while — what breaks first is the deflation, \(\Sigma_{xy} - \rho\, uv^{\mathsf T}\), which is scaled by the same disappearing factor: component 2 comes out as a copy of component 1, and the fit reports two components with the same correlation and no warning.

    The final u after the loop is not redundant. The loop breaks when v stops moving, at which point u was computed from the previous v; recomputing it from the final one makes the pair consistent — at tol when the loop converged, and at the last iterate when it hit n_iter. It also means the returned pair always satisfies both stationarity equations to the same tolerance, which is what makes the reported support a property of a fixed point rather than of a half-step.

    6. Kernel CCA, and the two matrices the derivation produces #

        K = 0.5 * (K + K.T)
    w, U = np.linalg.eigh(K)
    w = np.maximum(w, 0.0)
    F = (U * np.sqrt(w / (w + reg))) @ U.T
    S = (U * np.sqrt(w * (w + reg))) @ U.T
    return F, S

    (10), computed as a symmetric eigendecomposition rather than as a matrix square root. 0.5 * (K + K.T) symmetrises: a Gram matrix of an RBF kernel is positive definite in exact arithmetic and routinely has small negative eigenvalues in floating point, and sqrt of a negative eigenvalue is nan three functions later. np.maximum(w, 0.0) clips them.

    Both \(F\) and \(S\) are returned because the two are used in different places and both are needed: \(F\) for the correlation and the training variate, \(S\) for the dual coefficients. Computing \(F\) as (U * np.sqrt(w / (w + reg))) @ U.T rather than as sqrtm is exact and cheap — the eigendecomposition is already in hand — and it is the only place the shrink direction is expressed. Reverse the ratio to (w + reg) / w and the kernel becomes a noise amplifier; use w + reg under the root and you get the n − rank noise dimensions of the derivation's §6.

    7. The kernel path #

            Fx, Sx = _kcca_factors(_kernel(self._train_[0], self._train_[0],
                                       self.kernel, self._gammas[0]), self.reg)
        Fy, Sy = _kcca_factors(_kernel(self._train_[1], self._train_[1],
                                       self.kernel, self._gammas[1]), self.reg)
        P, s, Qt = np.linalg.svd(Fx @ Fy)
        k = self.n_components
        u, v = P[:, :k], Qt.T[:, :k]
        self.weights_ = [np.linalg.solve(Sx, u), np.linalg.solve(Sy, v)]
        self.correlations_ = self._corr_of(Fx @ u, Fy @ v, s[:k])

    This is the primal §2 code with \(X_w\) replaced by \(F\) and \(L\) replaced by \(S\), which is the point of (10): once the constraint is in \(F\), no whitening is needed. np.linalg.solve(Sx, u) is \(S_x^{-1}u\), the dual coefficients — note solve(Sx, u) and not solve(Sx.T, u), because \(S\) is symmetric and the transpose distinction that matters in the primal path has collapsed.

    The variates passed to _corr_of are \(F_xu\) and \(F_yv\), not \(K_x\alpha\) and \(K_y\beta\). They are equal in exact arithmetic (\(K\alpha = KS^{-1}u = Fu\)) and differ in floating point; using \(F\) is cheaper and keeps the correlation a property of the same objects the SVD saw.

    8. Inference through the dual #

            Xc = X - self.mean_[view]
        if self._kernel:
            # in kernel space the weights are dual coefficients, so the variate is
            # `k(x, X_train) α` rather than `x w`
            K = _kernel(Xc, self._train_[view], self.kernel, self._gammas[view])
            return K @ self.weights_[view]
        return Xc @ self.weights_[view]

    The one place a kernel CCA differs from a linear one at prediction time. w lives in the \(n\)-dimensional feature space, so X @ w is a shape error; the projection of a new point is \(\varphi(x)^{\mathsf T}w = k(x, X_{\text{train}})\alpha\), an \(n\)-vector times the stored coefficients. Getting this wrong — storing \(F^{-1}u\) instead of \(S^{-1}u\) — gives a training correlation that is correct and a held-out correlation that is garbage, which is exactly the failure the demo's harmonics section would show as "kernel CCA overfits" rather than "kernel CCA is implemented wrong".

    Centring is the other trap: Xc subtracts the training mean, and the Gram matrix is built on centred rows, which is not the same as centring the Gram matrix. For a linear kernel the two agree up to a rank-one correction; for an RBF kernel they do not agree at all, and the code's choice (centre the features, then kernelise) is the one consistent with the primal derivation.

    9. Many views, one variate #

            for c in range(self.n_components):
            z = _unit(rng.standard_normal(n))
            for it in range(self.n_iter):
                scores = []
                for i, C in enumerate(centred):
                    w = _canonical_weight(C, covs[i], z)
                    scores.append(_unit(C @ w))
                z_new = _unit(np.mean(scores, axis=0))
                self.n_iter_ = it + 1
                if float(np.linalg.norm(z_new - z)) < self.tol:
                    z = z_new
                    break
                z = z_new

    (14), alternating. Given \(z\), each view's best direction is a \(\Sigma\)-metric regression solved in closed form:

        w = np.linalg.solve(cov, C.T @ z)
    return w / math.sqrt(max(float(w @ cov @ w), EPS))

    Given the directions, the best shared variate is their mean after unit-scaling — _unit(C @ w) per view and np.mean across views, then _unit again. The per-view _unit is redundant at the fixed point, where the constraint already gives every variate unit variance; it matters in the first few iterations, when the weights are still far from satisfying it and one view would otherwise dominate the average by scale alone.

    The demo checks the loop against the one-step solution of (14), which it computes in ten lines:

        X = np.hstack([_centre(V) for V in views])
    R = X.T @ X / (X.shape[0] - 1)
    D = np.zeros_like(R)
    start = 0
    for V in views:
        k = V.shape[1]
        D[start:start + k, start:start + k] = R[start:start + k, start:start + k]
        start += k
    vals, vecs = np.linalg.eig(np.linalg.solve(D, R))
    lead = vecs[:, int(np.argmax(vals.real))].real

    np.linalg.eig and not eigh: solve(D, R) is not symmetric, and eigh reads one triangle of whatever it is given and returns a plausible answer — it gave cosine \(0.94\) against the loop where the correct call gives \(0.994\). That is the kind of bug that survives review, because the numbers it produces are close.

    The initialisation is a random unit vector from seed, which is the only stochastic ingredient in the whole file. It matters only for sign and for which component comes out first when two are nearly tied; with the deflation in place the components are determined up to sign, and _cos and score both take absolute values for that reason.

    10. What gets stored, and what "correlation" is measured on #

            U, V = self.transform(X, 0), self.transform(Y, 1)
        return np.array([abs(float(np.corrcoef(U[:, j], V[:, j])[0, 1]))
                         for j in range(self.correlations_.shape[0])])

    score is the held-out twin of correlations_, and the reason every number in this file's demo is reported as a pair. transform centres with the training mean and applies the training weights, so score(X, Y) on new data measures the variates the fit would actually produce — which is the only quantity a generalisation claim can be about. The abs is because canonical directions are defined up to sign; a caller who needs the sign should look at the weights, not at the correlation.

    11. The planted problem #

        sigma = 0.0 if rho <= 0.0 else math.sqrt((1.0 - rho) / rho)
    X = np.outer(z, wx) + sigma * rng.standard_normal((n, p))
    Y = np.outer(z, wy) + sigma * rng.standard_normal((n, q))
    return X, Y, wx, wy

    The demo's generator, and worth reading as the model CCA assumes: one latent \(z\), a sparse loading in each view, independent noise. The noise scale solves \(\rho = 1/(1 + \sigma^2)\) for \(\sigma\), so the population canonical correlation is \(\rho\) by construction and a fit can be scored against a number that was not itself estimated. nnz=5 of 20 columns is what makes sparse CCA's support recovery a meaningful measurement rather than a tautology.

    Complexity #

    \(n\) rows, \(p\) and \(q\) columns, \(D\) views, \(k\) components, \(T\) outer iterations.

    Operation Time Memory Note
    _centre + covariances \(O(n(p^2+q^2))\) \(O(n(p+q))\) dominates when \(n \gg p\)
    _whiten \(O(np^2 + p^3)\) \(O(np)\) Cholesky plus a triangular solve
    svd(Xwᵀ Yw) \(O(pq\min(p,q))\) \(O(pq)\) all \(k\) components at once
    _fit_pair total \(O(n(p^2+q^2) + p^3 + q^3)\) \(O(n(p+q))\) \(1\) ms at \(n=800\), \(p=q=30\)
    _lasso \(O(p^2)\) per sweep \(O(p^2)\) naive; \(O(p)\) per coordinate update
    _sparse_pair \(O(T(p^2+q^2))\) \(O(p^2+q^2)\) \(T \approx 10\)–\(20\) here
    _kernel (RBF) \(O(n^2(p+q))\) \(O(n^2)\) the Gram matrices
    eigh of the Grams \(O(n^3)\) \(O(n^2)\) why kernel CCA is \(0.30\) s at \(n=800\)
    _fit_multi \(O\big(T\sum_i (np_i + p_i^3)\big)\) \(O(nD)\) 71 iterations on the demo's 3 views
    transform (kernel) \(O(n_{\text{new}}\,n)\) \(O(n_{\text{new}}n)\) one kernel row per point
    score \(O(n(p+q))\) \(O(n)\) two matvecs per component

    The row that matters is the first against the second: the covariance products are \(O(n(p^2+q^2))\) and the factorisation is \(O(p^3)\), so CCA is linear in the number of rows once the columns are fixed. That is why the closed form scales to \(n = 10^6\) with \(p = q = 50\) and why the ridge sweep in the demo is cheap enough to run three times: \(n\) enters only through a matrix product.

    Kernel CCA is the opposite: \(O(n^3)\) time and \(O(n^2)\) memory in the number of rows, which is the entire capacity story. The demo's 100-training-point fit takes milliseconds; the \(n = 800\) timing above is \(0.30\) s; at \(n = 10^4\) the Gram matrix alone is 800 MB and the eigendecomposition is hours. Every practical kernel CCA uses a low-rank approximation of \(K\) (Nyström, random features, or a truncated eigendecomposition) and pays for it in exactly the accuracy the demo's harmonics experiment measures.

    _sparse_pair is the one place the naive implementation is asymptotically worse than it needs to be: S[j] @ u inside the coordinate loop is \(O(p)\) per coordinate, so a sweep is \(O(p^2)\), whereas maintaining \(Su\) incrementally is \(O(p)\) per coordinate and \(O(p)\) per sweep. The demo's \(p = 30\) makes the difference invisible — the whole sparse fit is \(30\) ms — and the fix is a standard exercise that is not here, for the reason §4 of the walkthrough gives.

    Failure modes #

    1. \(p + q \ge n\) and the correlation is \(1\) by algebra. §7, measured at \(1.000000\) on two independent \(40\times20\) noise matrices. This is the failure that matters because it is silent: the fit reports a perfect correlation, the weights are large and dense, and there is no warning. The explicit refusal in _whiten catches the \(\mathrm{reg} = 0\) case, but with the default \(10^{-6}\) it does not fire and you get the same \(1.000000\) — the check that matters is on \(p + q\) against \(n\) in the pipeline, and the honest response to a genuinely wide problem is regularized_cca.

    2. A small ridge does not fix the wide-data problem. At \(n=40\), \(p=q=20\), a ridge of \(10^{-8}\) still returns \(1.000000\), because \(10^{-8}\) changes the answer by less than the estimation error it is meant to control. The ridge has to be large enough to be a model — the demo's sweep needs \(\lambda \approx 1\), the same order as the column variances themselves — and choosing it is a model-selection problem, not a numerical-hygiene setting.

    3. The ridge breaks the invariance, in proportion to the ridge. \(2.8\times 10^{-14}\) at \(\lambda = 10^{-8}\), \(3\times10^{-6}\) at \(10^{-4}\). If a pipeline depends on "CCA doesn't care about the units", it stops being true the moment a ridge is added, and the failure is a slow drift rather than an error.

    4. The weights are not identified, so a coefficient is not an effect. Anything in the span is an equally valid answer; the fitted \(w_x\) is one representative, and a linear map of \(X\) produces a different representative with the same correlation. Reporting "gene 412 has weight 0.4" is meaningful only because gene 412 is a coordinate someone chose, and averaging weights across fits is averaging different bases.

    5. Sparse CCA's support is a coordinate statement, and its \(\lambda\) is not scale-free. §8: the code standardises columns so \(\lambda\) is comparable across them, but the support is still "these coordinates, in this basis". The demo's \(\lambda = 0.02\) is interesting at \(n=800\) and collapses the solution at \(\lambda = 0.2\); the same \(\lambda\) on \(n=400\) data would be a different model. Read \(\lambda\) as a fraction of the scale of \(X^{\mathsf T}Y\), or tune it by held-out correlation, and do not reuse a value across data sets.

    6. Sparse CCA is greedy and non-convex. One component at a time, deflated from the residual cross-covariance: component 2's support depends on component 1's, and a different random start (seed) can land in a different local optimum. The demo uses one component for exactly this reason, and any multi-component sparse fit should report the objective across seeds rather than one number.

    7. Kernel CCA has \(n\) parameters and \(n\) samples. The complexity table: the model can fit any \(n\) points, so the ridge and the bandwidth are the model, and the median heuristic is a starting point that can be beaten badly. On the demo's near-deterministic harmonics, the median-heuristic bandwidth gives \(0.79\) held out while \(20\times\) that bandwidth gives \(0.95\) — the kernel wants to interpolate because the relation really is a function, and the heuristic is tuned for noisier data. There is no default that is right for both.

    8. The kernel ridge must scale with \(n\) and with the kernel. \(\kappa\) is added to the eigenvalues of \(K\), and those eigenvalues scale like \(n\) times the variance in that direction. So \(\kappa = 10^{-3}\) is a strong regulariser at \(n = 100\) and a weak one at \(n = 10^5\), and a pipeline that fixes \(\kappa\) and changes the sample size has silently changed its model.

    9. More than two views: "the" canonical correlation is an average, and the choice of average is a model. SUMCOR's joint number is the mean over pairwise agreement, so a view sharing nothing costs a third of the score without being discarded (\(0.40\) against \(0.71\)). MAXVAR, SSQCOR or a view-selection step is a different answer to a different question. With \(D=2\) every criterion agrees, which is why this file's two-view path is exact and its three-view path is one of five defensible choices.

    10. High canonical correlation is not evidence of a shared cause. Two views can correlate at \(0.9\) because of a confound measured in both — batch, age, site — and CCA will report the direction that encodes it, with a sparse support and a convincing plot. This is the same failure as a spurious regression, and it is worse here because the direction is chosen to maximise the correlation: there is always a direction, because the fit is searching over \(p + q\) coefficients for a single number.

    11. correlations_ is in-sample and the variates are linear. The reported number is what the fit maximised, so it is upward-biased by construction; the demo reports every result as train/held-out for that reason. And the whole file is linear in the rows of each view after centring: a relation that is quadratic in the features needs kernel_cca, and a relation that is linear only after a transformation of the response is not this atom at all.

    Applications #

    • Cross-modal retrieval and matching. Image features and text features of the same items, projected to a shared space where the paired embeddings correlate. CCA is the linear ancestor of every contrastive multi-modal model; the difference is that a neural version learns the features and CCA takes them as given.
    • Multi-omics integration. Gene expression, methylation, proteomics on the same samples: the first canonical variate is a candidate shared pathway, and the sparse variant is used precisely because a pathway is a short list of genes. This is where sparse_cca's coordinate-dependence bites hardest and where the biology supplies the coordinates that make it meaningful.
    • Comparing neural representations. SVCCA and PWCCA use CCA between the activations of two layers or two networks to measure how similar their representations are, with the invariance of §4 as the justification: the comparison must not depend on the basis the network happened to learn. This is the most active current use of the atom.
    • A test statistic, not just an estimator. Wilks' \(\Lambda\) and Bartlett's approximation turn the canonical correlations into a likelihood-ratio test of "are these two sets independent", which is the classical multivariate-statistics use and still the right one when the question is confirmatory rather than predictive.
    • EEG, audio and sensor fusion. Two recordings of the same event, one per modality; the canonical variate is the shared component and the residual is the modality-specific one, which is a cheap alternative to ICA when the two views are known to be time-aligned.
    • As a lens on other atoms. CCA with class-indicator columns on \(Y\) is Fisher's linear discriminant; CCA between \(X\) and \(Y\) with the covariance replacing the correlation is PLS; CCA restricted to one view is PCA. Reading those three identities off (2) is the fastest way to see what each of them is actually optimising.
    • Seismic and remote sensing multiset analysis. Nielsen's multiset CCA is the standard tool for fusing many co-registered image channels, and it is the practical reason the multi-view formulation exists rather than being a generalisation for its own sake.

    Where it is a trap: fitting CCA with more columns than rows and reporting the correlation; adding a ridge and still claiming invariance; reading a weight as an effect; choosing \(\lambda\) or \(\kappa\) on the training correlation; and treating a high canonical correlation as evidence that the two views measure the same thing rather than that they share a confound.

    • Bach & Jordan (2002), Kernel independent component analysis — the relationship between kernel CCA and dependence measures, and the reason kernel CCA with a small ridge can report correlation \(1\) on dependent data.
    • Witten, Tibshirani & Hastie (2009), A penalized matrix decomposition, with applications to sparse principal components and canonical correlation analysis — the PMD formulation of §8, including the alternating lasso structure and the deflation.
    • Witten & Tibshirani (2009), Extensions of sparse canonical correlation analysis with applications to genomic data — the variant that penalises one side only, and the discussion of why the support is coordinate-dependent.
