    06 · Latent structure & factorisation/tier 3/4 variants/source

    Sparse coding

    Intuition #

    One signal, a few atoms, from a dictionary bigger than the signal is long.

            d = 40                          K = 80
   ┌─────────────┐            ┌───────────────────────┐
   │             │            │ ▮   ▮ ▮     ▮   ▮     │   atoms (columns of D)
   │      x      │     =      │ ▮   ▮ ▮     ▮   ▮     │
   │             │            └───────────────────────┘
   └─────────────┘                  only 4 of 80 carry weight

    An orthogonal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^{40}\) has 40 atoms and can represent anything with 40 non-zero coefficients. Drop the orthogonality requirement and the same 40-dimensional space can be spanned by 80, or 8000, atoms — and a natural signal then needs only a handful of them. That is the whole trade: overcompleteness buys sparsity, and an overcomplete dictionary has no inverse, so "the coefficients" are whatever your solver decides.

    Fitting a single signal is a convex program once you replace the count of non-zeros with its \(\ell_1\) norm:

    \[\min_{\alpha}\; \tfrac12\lVert x - D\alpha\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert\alpha\rVert_1. \qquad (1)\]

    The figure below is the whole reason \(\ell_1\) works. The \(\ell_1\) ball is a polytope with corners on the coordinate axes, and the first point where an expanding level set touches it is generically a vertex — which is a sparse vector. The \(\ell_2\) ball is round and the contact point is dense.

            ℓ1 ball (corners ⇒ sparsity)          ℓ2 ball (round ⇒ dense)
             ▲                                      ▲
        ─────┼─────                             ╭───┼───╮
       ╱     │     ╲                           │    │    │
      ╱      │      ╲                          │    │    │
   ───┼──────┼──────┼───                    ───┼────┼────┼───
      ╲      │      ╱                          │    │    │
       ╲     │     ╱                           ╰───┼───╯
             ▼                                      ▼

    Everything else in this file is a solver for (1), plus one greedy alternative that is not a solver for (1) at all. The four variants exist because "solve (1)" has four genuinely different costs: lasso_sc is exact and forms a \(K\times K\) Gram, fista_sc is exact-ish and never forms it, group_sc solves a different program with a block structure, and omp abandons convexity for a greedy fit that is exact on the model it assumes and unpredictable off it.

    The one number that governs all of it is the dictionary's coherence, the largest absolute inner product between two distinct atoms. The demo takes a dictionary whose atoms are all near-orthogonal and recovers a 2-sparse code from 40-dimensional signals every time; then it adds a single atom at cosine \(0.994\) to another and support recovery falls to \(0.86\), with every one of the 27 misses substituting the twin. The signal is still explained to within \(0.11\). Nothing in the output says anything is wrong — that is what a coherent dictionary does to a sparse code, and it is not a bug in the solver.

    Variants #

    Variant Solves Cost per signal Where it wins
    omp not (1): greedy, then least squares \(O(k^2d)\) tiny \(k\); no penalty to tune
    lasso_sc (1) exactly \(O(K^2d)\) once, \(O(K^2)\) per sweep \(K\) in the hundreds
    fista_sc (1) to tolerance \(O(Kd)\) per step \(K\) in the thousands
    group_sc (1) with \(\ell_{2,1}\) in place of \(\ell_1\) same as FISTA atoms that come in families

    omp is orthogonal matching pursuit. At each step take the atom most correlated with the current residual, add it to the support, and re-solve a least squares problem on the support so far. On the demo's 20 noiseless signals built from 2 of 80 atoms it recovers the exact support 20 times out of 20 and leaves \(5\times10^{-16}\) of the signal unexplained. It is not solving (1): with no noise and an exactly \(k\)-sparse signal, OMP recovers the sparsest solution, and with noise it does something for which no objective is being maximised. The \(2\%\)-noise case still recovers the support exactly and leaves a residual of \(0.106\), which is the noise, not an error.

    lasso_sc is (1) solved exactly by cyclic coordinate descent. It builds \(G = D^{\mathsf T}D\) once, \(K\times K\), and then each coordinate update is a closed-form soft threshold. On the demo's signal — 4 non-zeros out of 80 atoms — the sweep over \(\lambda\) walks the support \(5 \to 5 \to 3 \to 2 \to 0\) and the residual \(0.12 \to 0.48 \to 0.66 \to 1.25 \to 1.28\). Two of those points are exact, not empirical: at \(\lambda = 0\) the residual is zero to \(2\times10^{-8}\) (the solver's tolerance) but the code has all 80 coordinates non-zero and \(\ell_2\) norm \(1.255\) against the minimum-norm solution's \(1.030\), and at \(\lambda \ge \lVert D^{\mathsf T}x\rVert_\infty\) the solution is exactly \(\alpha = 0\).

    fista_sc is the same optimum reached from matvecs alone. It reaches coordinate descent's answer to \(3\times10^{-17}\) and is not monotone — the objective rises on 9 of the first 40 steps, because momentum overshoots and the convergence guarantee is on the smoothed sequence, not on every iterate. Which solver is faster is purely a question of \(K\): at \(K = 80\) one coordinate sweep costs \(K^2 = 6400\) against \(2Kd = 6400\) for a FISTA step, so they are the same price and exactness wins; at \(K = 1024\), \(d = 64\) a sweep is \(1.0\)M against \(131\)k, one sweep buys 8 steps, and at a matched budget of 10.5M multiply-adds FISTA is \(0.12\) lower on the objective.

    group_sc replaces the penalty with \(\lambda\sum_g\lVert\alpha_g\rVert_2\), the sum of group norms. The prox is a block soft threshold — shrink a whole group by a factor, or zero it entirely — so a family switches on or off together. On the demo's 30 atoms in 10 families of 3 near-parallel members (mean cosine \(0.92\) inside a family) with a signal built from families 1 and 6, the group penalty selects exactly those two families and keeps all 6 coefficients; the lasso at the same \(\lambda\) keeps 5 and silently drops atom 4, which costs it almost nothing because atoms 3 and 5 reproduce it. With one atom per group the two objectives agree to \(2\times10^{-13}\) — the group penalty is the \(\ell_1\) penalty, plus a decision about what counts as a coordinate.

    Derivation #

    1. Why \(\ell_1\) and not \(\ell_0\) #

    The honest objective counts non-zeros, \(\min \tfrac12\lVert x - D\alpha\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert\alpha\rVert_0\), and it is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard with a non-convex feasible set that no local method can navigate. The relaxation is \(\lVert\alpha\rVert_1 = \sum_j|\alpha_j|\), the largest convex function below the count on \([-1, 1]\). It is the tightest convex relaxation in a precise sense, and its geometry is what buys the sparsity: the \(\ell_1\) ball is the convex hull of the signed coordinate vectors, so its extreme points are sparse, and minimising a smooth function over it lands on faces of low dimension.

    Two consequences to keep straight. First, \(\ell_1\) sparsity and \(\ell_0\) sparsity are different answers: on the demo's own data the \(\ell_1\) solution at \(\lambda = 0.05\) keeps 5 atoms where the truth has 4. Second, the relaxation is exact only under conditions on \(D\) — §3 gives the one that is checkable — and the demo's twin experiment is what failure looks like.

    2. The optimality conditions, and the two exact limits #

    (1) is convex and non-smooth at the origin. A minimiser \(\alpha^\star\) is characterised by the subdifferential condition

    \[D^{\mathsf T}(x - D\alpha^\star) \in \lambda\,\partial\lVert\alpha^\star\rVert_1, \qquad \partial\lVert\alpha\rVert_1 = \{g : g_j = \mathrm{sign}(\alpha_j) \ \text{if}\ \alpha_j \ne 0,\ \ |g_j| \le 1\ \text{otherwise}\}. \qquad (2)\]

    Read coordinate by coordinate, with \(G = D^{\mathsf T}D\) and \(b = D^{\mathsf T}x\):

    \[\alpha_j \ne 0 \;\Rightarrow\; (G\alpha)_j - b_j = -\lambda\,\mathrm{sign}(\alpha_j), \qquad \alpha_j = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; |(G\alpha)_j - b_j| \le \lambda. \qquad (3)\]

    Two exact statements fall out, and the demo asserts both.

    \(\lambda = 0\) gives an exact representation. Then (3) says \(G\alpha = b\), i.e. \(D^{\mathsf T}(x - D\alpha) = 0\): the residual is orthogonal to every atom, which for \(K > d\) is a system with infinitely many solutions — a \(K\)-dimensional affine space of them, \(K - d\) of whose dimensions are free. The residual is zero and the coefficients are one point in that space, selected by the solver's update order. The demo's \(\lambda = 0\) fit has all \(80\) coordinates non-zero, \(\ell_2\) norm \(1.255\), against the minimum-norm solution \(D^{+}x\) at \(1.030\): coordinate descent from a zero start is nowhere near a sparse answer, and nothing in the output says so.

    \(\lambda \ge \lVert b\rVert_\infty\) gives \(\alpha = 0\). With \(\alpha = 0\) the condition is \(|b_j| \le \lambda\) for every \(j\), so it holds exactly when \(\lambda\) is at least the largest correlation any atom has with the signal. The demo computes \(\lVert D^{\mathsf T}x\rVert_\infty = 0.741\) and fits at \(0.741 \times 1.001\), getting exact zeros rather than small numbers.

    3. Coordinate descent, and why it is exact here #

    Fixing every coefficient except \(j\), (1) is a one-dimensional problem and its minimiser is a soft threshold:

    \[\alpha_j \leftarrow \frac{1}{G_{jj}}\, \mathcal{S}\!\left(b_j - \sum_{k\ne j} G_{jk}\alpha_k,\ \lambda\right), \qquad \mathcal{S}(z,\lambda) = \mathrm{sign}(z)\max(|z| - \lambda, 0). \qquad (4)\]

    This is (3) solved for \(\alpha_j\): the partial residual scaled by the curvature of that coordinate. Each sweep is \(O(K^2)\), the iterates are monotone in objective because each coordinate step minimises the exact objective along a coordinate line, and convergence to the unique minimiser (for \(\lambda > 0\)) is linear. The demo's 80-atom problem converges in 22 sweeps to tolerance; the large one needs tens more.

    The one thing that makes this a cheap method rather than a nice one is that \(G\) is formed once. The alternative — recomputing \(D\alpha\) and \(D^{\mathsf T}(\cdot)\) per coordinate — is the same arithmetic at \(K\) times the cost, and it is the standard first implementation.

    4. Why the Gram is the problem #

    \(G\) is \(K\times K\). For \(K = 1024\) that is \(8\) MB and \(K^2d = 6.7\times10^7\) multiply-adds to build; for \(K = 10^5\) it is 80 GB and the method is unavailable, not merely slow. FISTA avoids it by touching only \(D\) and \(D^{\mathsf T}\), and pays a rate.

    Split (1) into a smooth part \(f(\alpha) = \tfrac12\lVert x - D\alpha\rVert^2\) and a non-smooth part \(g(\alpha) = \lambda\lVert\alpha\rVert_1\). The proximal gradient step is

    \[\alpha_{k+1} = \mathrm{prox}_{g/L}\!\left(\alpha_k - \tfrac{1}{L}\nabla f(\alpha_k)\right), \qquad L = \lVert D\rVert_2^2, \qquad (5)\]

    and the prox of \(\lambda\lVert\cdot\rVert_1\) is the soft threshold of (4), applied elementwise with threshold \(\lambda/L\). \(L\) is the smallest step that makes the step a contraction; anything smaller converges, anything larger diverges, so it is the one hyper-parameter and it has a formula. FISTA adds Nesterov momentum,

    \[y_{k+1} = \alpha_{k+1} + \frac{t_k - 1}{t_{k+1}}(\alpha_{k+1} - \alpha_k), \qquad t_{k+1} = \tfrac12\left(1 + \sqrt{1 + 4t_k^2}\right), \qquad (6)\]

    and the step is taken from \(y\) rather than \(\alpha\). The objective bound improves from \(O(1/k)\) to \(O(1/k^2)\), which is the entire difference between the two solver rows in the table.

    Momentum is also why FISTA's objective is not monotone: the bound is on \(f(\alpha_k) + g(\alpha_k) - f^\star\) being \(O(1/k^2)\), not on every step being a descent step. The demo measures 9 increases in the first 40 steps. A monotone variant (MFISTA, or adaptive restart) fixes this by rejecting a step that increases the objective, and it is what you use when the iterates are being watched rather than just the final point.

    5. Group sparsity #

    Atoms often arrive in families — three shifted copies of a wavelet, the one-hot columns of a categorical variable, the RGB of a patch. The penalty that encodes "a family is on or off" is the sum of group norms,

    \[\min_\alpha\; \tfrac12\lVert x - D\alpha\rVert^2 + \lambda\sum_{g}\lVert\alpha_g\rVert_2, \qquad (7)\]

    with prox

    \[\mathrm{prox}_{t\lVert\cdot\rVert_2}(v) = \max\!\left(0,\ 1 - \frac{t}{\lVert v\rVert_2}\right)v, \qquad (8)\]

    a block soft threshold: the whole vector is either zeroed or scaled towards zero along its own direction. There is no coordinate-wise thinning, which is the modelling statement — within a family, the relative sizes are never questioned.

    Two facts make (7) more than a variant. With singleton groups, \(\lVert\alpha_g \rVert_2 = |\alpha_g|\) and (7) is (1); the demo asserts the two objectives agree to \(2\times10^{-13}\). And the group penalty is invariant to rotations within a group, so its answer does not depend on how a redundant family is parameterised, which is exactly what the lasso's answer does depend on: in the demo it drops one of three atoms at cosine \(0.92\) to the others and there is no principled reason it is atom 4 rather than atom 3.

    6. Greedy: OMP, and the condition it needs #

    OMP picks \(j_k = \arg\max_j |d_j^{\mathsf T}r_{k-1}|\) among unused atoms, then sets \(\alpha_S = (D_S^{\mathsf T}D_S)^{-1}D_S^{\mathsf T}x\) on the support and recomputes the residual. It is exact on the model — a \(k\)-sparse signal, no noise — provided the dictionary is incoherent enough.

    The checkable condition is in terms of the coherence

    \[\mu(D) = \max_{i\ne j} |d_i^{\mathsf T}d_j|. \qquad (9)\]

    If \(k < \tfrac12(1 + 1/\mu)\) then a \(k\)-sparse representation is the unique sparsest one and OMP finds it; basis pursuit finds it too. For the demo's random \(40\times80\) dictionary \(\mu \approx 0.55\) gives a bound of \(k < 1.4\), and the experiment runs at \(k = 2\) and succeeds anyway — the bound is sufficient, not necessary. What breaks OMP is not a threshold but an ambiguity: two atoms at cosine \(0.994\) are interchangeable, so the greedy choice between them is a coin flip that the objective cannot see. All 27 failures in the demo are that coin flip, and the residual afterwards is \(0.11\) because the substitute atom explains almost the same signal.

    The standard fix is a dictionary fix, not a solver fix: merge the twins, or penalise the pair jointly with group_sc. This is why dictionary learning keeps atoms apart explicitly.

    7. Identification, or what "the" sparse code means #

    With \(K > d\) and \(\lambda = 0\) the solution set is the affine space \(\{\alpha : D\alpha = x\}\), of dimension \(K - d\). Every point in it is a perfect fit, and the objective cannot distinguish them. As \(\lambda\) grows the solution becomes unique (strict convexity for \(\lambda > 0\)), and the path is continuous and piecewise linear in \(\lambda\).

    The sentence to carry away: a sparse code is a statement about atoms, not about the signal. Change the dictionary and the code changes, including its support, and no amount of residual-checking detects it. That is why the twin experiment is in the demo, and why dictionary_learning has to exist.

    Code walkthrough #

    SparseCoding(nnz=5, *, variant="lasso_sc", lam=0.1, n_groups=4, n_iter=1000, tol=1e-8) with fit(X, D), transform, fit_transform, reconstruct, and the attributes codes_ \((n, K)\), dictionary_ \((d, K)\), groups_, n_iter_.

    fit normalises the dictionary once, then dispatches one signal at a time. The object is a solver: it stores no learned statistics, and fitting a new dictionary means fitting again.

    1. Normalisation, once per dictionary #

        nrm = np.linalg.norm(D, axis=0)
    if np.any(nrm < EPS):
        raise ValueError("a dictionary atom is all zeros — drop it or replace it")
    return D / nrm

    Every penalty in this file is a threshold on coefficients, so it is only comparable across atoms of equal norm. A dictionary handed in with columns of wildly different scale produces a code that is sparse in number and not in information: the short atoms get penalised 10× as hard as the long ones and are dropped arithmetically. Normalising here rather than asking the caller to do it means lam means one thing everywhere in the file.

    The zero-atom check is not politeness. G_jj = 0 makes the coordinate update in (4) a division by zero, which produces an inf, which quietly poisons the whole vector and then the whole batch of codes.

    2. The objective everyone shares #

        fit = 0.5 * float(np.sum((x - D @ a) ** 2))
    if groups is None:
        return fit + lam * float(np.abs(a).sum())
    return fit + lam * float(sum(np.linalg.norm(a[g]) for g in groups))

    Written in terms of the residual, not in terms of the Gram, so the same function scores a code produced by any of the four variants — including omp, which never builds a Gram. The demo uses it to compare group_sc against lasso_sc at \(2\times10^{-13}\) on singleton groups and to compare FISTA against coordinate descent to \(3\times10^{-17}\), which are the two checks that keep four solvers honest about solving anything at all.

    3. Greedy selection and the least-squares refit #

        for _ in range(min(nnz, D.shape[1])):
        corr = np.abs(D.T @ res)
        corr[support] = -1.0                             # never re-select an atom
        support.append(int(np.argmax(corr)))
        sol, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(D[:, support], x, rcond=None)
        res = x - D[:, support] @ sol

    The selection step of §6 with one detail that matters: corr[support] = -1.0 rather than skipping the index. Without it a correlated dictionary can select the same atom twice, the support matrix becomes rank-deficient, and lstsq returns a solution anyway, so the loop terminates with a coefficient vector of the right shape and the wrong content.

    np.linalg.lstsq rather than an explicit inverse of \(D_S^{\mathsf T}D_S\): for a coherent dictionary the Gram of the support is ill-conditioned, and a pseudo-inverse minimum-norm answer is a much better thing to return than a blow-up. It is also the one place solver= would matter: lstsq on a \(40\times4\) system is dominated by the SVD, and a Cholesky solve on the normal equations is faster and less stable.

    4. The coordinate update #

        G, b = D.T @ D, D.T @ x
    d = np.diag(G).copy()
    a = np.zeros(G.shape[0])
    used = n_iter
    for it in range(n_iter):
        a_old = a.copy()
        for j in range(G.shape[0]):
            partial = b[j] - (G[j] @ a - d[j] * a[j])
            a[j] = _soft(partial, lam) / d[j]

    (4), with the partial residual written as b[j] - (G[j] @ a - d[j] * a[j]), because G[j] @ a already contains the \(j\)-th term and subtracting it is cheaper than forming the sum over \(k \ne j\). d = np.diag(G).copy() is hoisted: the diagonal is constant and, after normalisation, positive.

    a_old = a.copy() then a max-absolute-difference test rather than a norm test: after a few sweeps only a handful of coordinates move, and the max difference is the quantity that converges linearly. The relative tolerance divides by the largest coefficient so that a signal with amplitude 1000 and one with amplitude \(10^{-3}\) converge at the same relative accuracy.

    5. The step size, by power iteration #

        rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    v = rng.standard_normal(D.shape[1])
    v /= np.linalg.norm(v)
    top = 0.0
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        u = D.T @ (D @ v)
        top = float(v @ u)
        nrm = float(np.linalg.norm(u))

    \(\lVert D\rVert_2^2\) is the Lipschitz constant of \(\nabla f\) and therefore the step size in (5). The obvious way to get it is eigvalsh(D.T @ D)[-1], which costs \(O(K^2 d + K^3)\) and forms the very matrix FISTA exists to avoid. Power iteration costs 60 matvecs — four orders of magnitude less at \(K = 10^4\) — and is accurate here because the eigenvalue ratio of a random dictionary's Gram is far from 1.

    The rng is seeded, so the step size is deterministic across runs. It is a stochastic estimate of a fixed quantity, and an unseeded one would make two runs of the demo produce different iteration counts, which is exactly the kind of non-determinism the repository's convention forbids.

    6. The momentum loop #

            a_new = _soft(y - (D.T @ (D @ y - x)) / L, lam / L)
        t_new = 0.5 * (1.0 + math.sqrt(1.0 + 4.0 * t * t))
        y = a_new + ((t - 1.0) / t_new) * (a_new - a)
        step = float(np.max(np.abs(a_new - a)))
        a, t = a_new, t_new
        if step <= tol * max(1.0, float(np.max(np.abs(a)))):
            used = it + 1
            break

    (5) and (6) in five lines. D.T @ (D @ y - x) is the gradient in two matvecs and never forms \(D^{\mathsf T}D\); lam / L folds the step size into the threshold so the prox is a plain _soft.

    The subtle part is the convergence test: it measures the movement of \(\alpha\), not the size of the gradient, because the gradient at the optimum is Dᵀ(x − Dα) which vanishes only in the \(\lambda = 0\) case. For \(\lambda > 0\) the gradient at the optimum is exactly \(\pm\lambda\) on the support, so a gradient test would need to know the support. The coefficient movement is the right proxy and it is what the monotone variants test too.

    7. The block threshold #

        nrm = float(np.linalg.norm(v))
    if nrm < EPS:
        return np.zeros_like(v)
    return v * max(0.0, 1.0 - t / nrm)

    (8), and the difference between it and _soft is the whole variant: _soft scales each coefficient by a different factor, this scales a whole block by one factor along its own direction. The nrm < EPS branch handles an exactly zero block, where \(t/\lVert v\rVert\) is infinite and the max would return nan.

    Note what is not here: no normalisation by group size. A group of 10 and a group of 2 are penalised by the same \(\lambda\) applied to their norms, so the larger group is easier to keep switched on. Group-size weighting (\(\lambda\sqrt{|g|}\)) is the standard fix and it changes the model, which is why it is not silently built in.

    8. The group solver #

        for it in range(n_iter):
        trial = a - (D.T @ (D @ a - x)) / L
        a_new = trial.copy()
        for g in groups:
            a_new[g] = _block_soft(trial[g], lam / L)
        step = float(np.max(np.abs(a_new - a)))
        a = a_new

    Plain ISTA with the block prox, no momentum. The trial vector is computed once and every group is thresholded from it; writing a_new[g] = _block_soft(a_new[g], ...) in the loop instead would compound the shrinkage across groups, since each update would then read a partially-updated vector. That bug produces a solution that is sparse, converges, and is wrong by a factor that depends on the group order — which is the definition of a hard bug.

    9. The dispatch #

            if self.variant == "omp":
            return _omp(D, x, self.nnz, self.tol)
        if self.variant == "lasso_sc":
            return _lasso_cd(D, x, self.lam, self.n_iter, self.tol)
        if self.variant == "fista_sc":
            return _fista(D, x, self.lam, self.n_iter, self.tol)
        return _group_pg(D, x, self.groups_, self.lam, self.n_iter, self.tol)

    One signal at a time, because that is the only shape all four solvers share: coordinate descent would batch naturally (it is the same Gram for every signal), and this is the one place the file gives up a factor of \(n\) for uniformity. Each _one returns the code and the inner-iteration count, and fit keeps the maximum over the batch in n_iter_ so that a fit which hit the iteration cap is visible rather than silently truncated.

    10. The demo's generator #

        a = np.zeros(K)
    a[rng.choice(K, nnz, replace=False)] = np.linspace(1.0, 0.4, nnz)
    return D, D @ a, a

    A signal from 4 atoms with coefficients from 1.0 down to 0.4. The decay matters: with equal coefficients the \(\lambda\) path drops all four at once and the staircase the demo reports disappears. A graded signal is what makes "5 → 5 → 3 → 2 → 0" a measurement of the path rather than of a threshold.

    Complexity #

    \(d\) ambient dimension, \(K\) atoms, \(k\) the greedy support size, \(T\) iterations.

    Operation Time Memory Note
    _normalise \(O(dK)\) \(O(dK)\) once per dictionary
    _omp \(O(k^2d)\) \(O(kd)\) the support least squares dominates
    DᵀD in _lasso_cd \(O(K^2d)\) \(O(K^2)\) the row that rules out large \(K\)
    one CD sweep \(O(K^2)\) \(O(K)\) 22 sweeps here at \(K = 80\)
    _lipschitz \(O(60Kd)\) \(O(K)\) 60 matvecs, replaces an \(O(K^3)\) eigendecomposition
    one FISTA step \(O(Kd)\) \(O(K)\) no Gram
    one group step \(O(Kd + K)\) \(O(K)\) the prox is \(O(K)\) in total
    fit, 20 signals, \(d{=}40\), \(K{=}80\) — — 2 ms omp, 46 ms fista, 56 ms lasso, 113 ms group

    The single row that matters is DᵀD against one FISTA step: \(K^2d\) versus \(Kd\), a factor of \(K\). At \(K = 1024\) the measured wall-clock is 105 ms for coordinate descent and 17 ms for FISTA on the same signal to the same tolerance — the 6× that survives the constant factors. At \(K = 80\) the two are within noise of one another and lasso_sc is the better choice because it is exact, and at \(K = 10\) neither matters.

    Memory is the constraint that arrives first, and it is quadratic in lasso_sc: \(K = 10^5\) is 80 GB for the Gram. FISTA's footprint is \(O(K)\), which is what makes it the default in the dictionary-learning literature, where \(K\) is chosen to be large.

    Failure modes #

    1. lam = 0 returns an arbitrary point of an affine space. §2: residual zero, coefficients chosen by the coordinate order. The demo's \(\lambda = 0\) fit is dense — 80 non-zeros, norm \(1.255\) against the minimum-norm \(1.030\) — and a different update order gives a different vector with the same zero residual. If a pipeline uses lam=0 "to get the exact fit", it is reading a least-squares solution and calling it sparse.

    2. A coherent dictionary makes the support unidentifiable, silently. The twin experiment: cosine \(0.994\), recovery \(0.86\), all misses substituting the twin, signal still explained to \(0.11\). There is no residual check that catches this, because both codes are equally good fits. The diagnostic is \(\mu(D)\) — compute it — and the remedy is a different dictionary or a group penalty over the collinear set.

    3. \(\ell_1\) is not \(\ell_0\), and it is biased. At \(\lambda = 0.05\) on data whose truth is 4-sparse, the solution has 5 atoms. The extra atom is not noise: \(\ell_1\) shrinks all coefficients towards zero, so the large ones are underestimated as well, a bias that debiasing (refit by least squares on the support) removes at the cost of no longer being the solution of (1).

    4. \(\lambda\) has no scale-free setting. Its useful range is \([0, \lVert D^{\mathsf T}x\rVert_\infty]\), which depends on the signal's norm, the dictionary's norm and the noise. lam=0.05 on unit-normalised atoms and unit-scale signals is a different model from lam=0.05 on data scaled by 1000. The only portable parameterisation is \(\lambda/\lVert D^{\mathsf T}x\rVert_\infty\).

    5. FISTA's objective is not monotone, and its iterates can look divergent. 9 rises in the first 40 steps on the demo's own problem. Iterating until the objective stops improving therefore stops at the wrong place, and monitoring the objective to detect a bug will detect a real property instead. Monitor the coefficient movement, or use a monotone variant.

    6. K² is a wall, not a slowdown. Coordinate descent at \(K = 10^5\) is not slow, it is unavailable. Code that "works" on the demo's 80 atoms and is pointed at an 80000-atom dictionary will allocate 51 GB and die — inside D.T @ D, a long way from the line that chose the solver.

    7. Group structure is a claim about the dictionary, and a wrong claim is invisible. Grouping contiguous columns assumes the atoms were ordered meaningfully. With a permuted dictionary the same code clusters noise, and the result is a sparse, stable, reproducible answer to a question nobody asked. The demo's families are contiguous by construction and shuffled in practice.

    8. OMP's budget is not a sparsity guarantee. nnz=1 on a 2-sparse signal leaves \(0.45\) of it unexplained and no error; nnz=10 on a 2-sparse signal returns 10 atoms that fit noise. The budget has to come from the problem's physics or from cross-validation, not from a default.

    9. Every solver here assumes the dictionary is right. A bad atom is fitted as faithfully as a good one, and the residual is the only warning — which is zero when \(K > d\) and \(\lambda = 0\), and small whenever the dictionary spans the signal. That assumption is exactly what dictionary_learning relaxes.

    Applications #

    • Image and audio compression. JPEG's DCT and MP3's filterbank are fixed dictionaries; matching pursuit over an overcomplete one (wavelets plus sinusoids plus transients) is the standard for sparse audio coding, and it is where omp earns its keep: a few dozen atoms per second of signal, chosen greedily, is fast enough to be a codec.
    • Denoising and inpainting. Fit (1) to a corrupted signal, keep reconstruct(). The reason it works is §2's optimality condition: the residual of a sparse fit is uncorrelated with every atom, so it looks like noise, and a structured corruption does not.
    • Compressed sensing. If \(\alpha\) is \(k\)-sparse and \(D\) obeys (9), then \(m \gtrsim k\log(K/k)\) measurements determine \(x\) exactly, and (1) is the recovery algorithm. This is the application that made the \(\ell_1\) relaxation famous, and the whole theory is a statement about \(\mu\) or its cousins.
    • Feature selection as a special case. With \(D = X\) and \(x = y\), (1) is the lasso, and sparse_cca's inner loop is this file's _lasso_cd verbatim. The same solver, a different story about what the columns are.
    • Hierarchical and structured penalties. Gene pathways, wavelet trees, multi-task rows (the \(\ell_{2,1}\) norm applied along the wrong axis): every one of these is (7) with a different group definition, and the block prox of (8) is the only thing that changes.
    • A building block for sparse PCA and dictionary learning. Sparse PCA is (1) inside an eigenvector iteration; dictionary_learning alternates (1) with a dictionary update. Both call exactly this solver, which is why it is an atom and not a step inside one.

    Where it is a trap: reading a support as a set of "true" variables when the dictionary is coherent; using lam=0 and calling the result sparse; choosing \(\lambda\) on the training residual; and pointing lasso_sc at a dictionary with \(10^5\) atoms and expecting a slowdown rather than a memory error.
