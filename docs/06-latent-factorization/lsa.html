    06 · Latent structure & factorisation/tier 2/4 variants/source

    Latent semantic analysis

    Intuition #

    A corpus is a matrix. Rows are documents, columns are terms, entries are counts. It is the least exotic data structure in machine learning, and it has one awkward property: it is sparse and literal. Two documents about the same subject can share no words at all.

       X (240 documents × 60 terms, 31% non-zero)

   "the car needs a new engine"    →  the 3, car 1, need 1, new 1, engine 1
   "my automobile requires a motor" →  my 1, automobile 1, require 1, motor 1

   XᵀX for those two rows: 0.     No shared term, no similarity, no match.

    LSA's answer is that the two sentences do share terms indirectly — car and automobile both co-occur with drive, engine, motorway — and that indirect sharing shows up as soon as the matrix is approximated by a low-rank one. Terms that never appear together get mapped to nearby points because they appear with the same third terms.

    \[\underbrace{X}_{n \times d} \;\approx\; \underbrace{U_k S_k}_{n \times k}\, \underbrace{V_k^\top}_{k \times d}, \qquad k \ll \min(n, d). \qquad (1)\]

    Each of the \(k\) rows of \(V_k^\top\) is a topic: a weighted list of terms. Each row of \(U_k S_k\) says how much of each topic a document contains. A document is then a point in \(\mathbb{R}^k\), and similarity is a dot product there.

    The important thing about this atom is that (1) is the easy part. The SVD is exact, unique, and available in one library call; there is nothing to tune and nothing to get wrong. What is interesting is which matrix you hand it. Raw counts, \(\log(1 + \mathrm{tf})\), tf-idf and log-entropy all produce a different \(X\) and therefore a different decomposition, and the demo measures the difference at the only place it shows up — retrieval accuracy on documents the model never saw:

    \(k\) raw counts tf-idf log-entropy
    1 25% 25% 25%
    2 55% 90% 72%
    3 88% 100% 100%
    4 100% 100% 100%

    At the natural rank every model is perfect and the choice is invisible. Cut one step below it and the weighting is the entire model: raw counts get the topic right for barely half the held-out documents, tf-idf for nine in ten. Meanwhile the two solvers — exact SVD and a randomized sketch — give the same retrieval number to the last digit, because they compute the same decomposition. That asymmetry is the thesis of this file.

    Two further things are worth knowing before the derivation, because they are where implementations go wrong.

    It is not PCA. PCA centres the columns before decomposing; LSA does not. The demo's mean count is \(0.67\), and subtracting it would be a modelling decision, not a cleanup — a row of zeros means "empty document", not "average document". Centring would make the first component the mean document, which for counts is mostly document length, precisely the nuisance the weighting exists to remove.

    The weights are part of the model. idf and the entropy weight \(g\) are fitted on the training corpus, stored, and reused verbatim for every query. Recomputing them on a batch of one gives \(\log(1/1) = 0\) for every term in it, so the query vector is zero and it has no cosine with anything. The demo measures \(\lVert z \rVert = 0.87\) for a single-document query with the fitted weights, and \(0\) without — a failure that is silent, because a zero vector still returns some argmax.

    Variants #

    Variant Solver Weighting What it is for Measured
    truncated_svd full SVD raw \(\mathrm{tf}\) the reference, and the exact answer residual \(= \sqrt{\Sigma_{i>k}\sigma_i^2}\)
    randomized sketch + power iteration raw \(\mathrm{tf}\) same answer, fewer flops on a large \(X\) subspace gap \(10^{-9}\) in 8 steps
    tfidf_lsa full SVD \(\mathrm{tf}\cdot\log(n/\mathrm{df})\) downweight ubiquitous terms 90% at \(k=2\)
    log_entropy full SVD \(\log(1+\mathrm{tf})\cdot g\) downweight evenly spread terms 72% at \(k=2\)

    The four ids are not four algorithms. There is one solver decision (exact or sketched) and one weighting decision (three choices), and the catalog exposes them as a T rather than a full cross:

    raw tf tf-idf log-entropy
    full SVD truncated_svd tfidf_lsa log_entropy
    randomized randomized — —

    Any two variants on the top row differ only in the weighting, and the left column differs only in the solver. That is enough to separate the two effects — the demo's randomized fit reproduces the raw-count accuracy to the last digit, and the weighting moves it by 35 points — but it does mean the solver comparison is always made under raw counts, and nobody has measured whether the sketch is as accurate on tf-idf'd data. It should be: the sketch approximates the range of whatever matrix it is given, and the weighting is applied before the solver. The table is encoded once so the two decisions cannot drift apart:

    WEIGHTING = {"truncated_svd": "tf", "randomized": "tf",
             "log_entropy": "log_entropy", "tfidf_lsa": "tfidf"}

    truncated_svd is numpy.linalg.svd followed by a slice. It is the definition of the atom: the exact best rank-\(k\) approximation in the Frobenius norm, computed directly. It is also the honest baseline every other variant has to be compared against, and the reason the demo can assert an identity (residual\(^2\) equals the discarded spectrum) rather than a tolerance.

    randomized is the Halko–Martinsson–Tropp range finder: draw a Gaussian sketch of the range, sharpen it with power iterations, orthonormalise, and solve the SVD on a matrix that is only \((k+p) \times d\). On a \(240 \times 60\) matrix this is not faster than the exact SVD — the constant dominates — but it is the algorithm that makes LSA possible at \(n = 10^6\), where a full SVD is \(O(nd\min(n,d))\) and out of reach. Its accuracy is a choice (n_iter), not a hope, and the demo measures the choice.

    tfidf_lsa is what most people mean by "LSA". The weighting is the model: it rescales each column by \(\log(n/\mathrm{df}_j)\), so a term that appears in every document gets weight zero and a term unique to one document gets \(\log n\). That is a statement about informativeness, and it is deliberately crude — it only asks whether a term appears, never how often or how the appearances are distributed.

    log-entropy is Deerwester et al.'s original weighting, and it asks the second question. A term's distribution over documents has an entropy; if the entropy is maximal the term is spread perfectly evenly and cannot distinguish documents, so its weight is zero; if the term is concentrated, it can. The difference from tf-idf is visible on a term that appears in every document but mostly in one: tf-idf cannot see it, log-entropy can. The demo's tiny \(3 \times 4\) matrix is constructed to make that difference exact.

    Derivation #

    1. Eckart–Young, with the inequality written out #

    Let \(X = U S V^\top\) with \(\sigma_1 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_r > 0\). For any matrix \(B\) with \(\mathrm{rank}(B) \le k\),

    \[\lVert X - B \rVert_F^2 \;\ge\; \sum_{i>k} \sigma_i^2, \qquad (2)\]

    with equality at \(B = U_k S_k V_k^\top\). Expanding the left side,

    \[\lVert X - B \rVert_F^2 = \lVert X \rVert_F^2 - 2\langle X, B\rangle + \lVert B \rVert_F^2, \qquad (3)\]

    and both inner products are controlled by the singular values. Von Neumann's trace inequality gives

    \[\langle X, B \rangle = \mathrm{tr}\!\left(X^\top B\right) \;\le\; \sum_i \sigma_i(X)\,\sigma_i(B) \;=\; \sum_{i \le k} \sigma_i\,\tau_i, \qquad (4)\]

    where \(\tau_i = \sigma_i(B)\) and the last equality is \(\mathrm{rank}(B) \le k\). Also \(\lVert B \rVert_F^2 = \sum_{i\le k}\tau_i^2\). Substituting into (3),

    \[\lVert X - B \rVert_F^2 \;\ge\; \sum_i \sigma_i^2 - 2\sum_{i\le k}\sigma_i\tau_i + \sum_{i\le k}\tau_i^2 \;=\; \sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2 + \sum_{i\le k}(\sigma_i - \tau_i)^2. \qquad (5)\]

    The right side is a sum of squares, so it is minimised by \(\tau_i = \sigma_i\) and equals \(\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2\) there. That is the whole theorem, and it is why the demo can write

        assert abs(resid ** 2 - tail) < 1e-10, (resid ** 2, tail)

    rather than comparing two approximations: the residual of the truncated SVD is not approximately the discarded spectrum, it is the discarded spectrum. On the demo's matrix, \(7.5624^2 = 57.19\), and the 56 discarded singular values account for exactly that.

    Making "best" non-vacuous needs the right comparison, and the obvious one is wrong. A random Gaussian \(\tilde{U}\tilde{V}^\top\) has a random scale, so beating it says nothing about the subspace; what matters is the best rank-\(k\) approximation in a random \(k\)-dimensional subspace. Over Haar-random \(Q\), \(\mathbb{E}[QQ^\top] = \frac{k}{d}I\), so a typical subspace keeps exactly \(k/d\) of the energy and its residual is

    \[\sqrt{\left(1 - \frac{k}{d}\right)\sum_i \sigma_i^2}, \qquad (6)\]

    which is \(14.97\) for the demo's \(k=4\), \(d=60\). The mean of 200 draws is \(14.96\) and the smallest is still larger than \(7.56\). That is the theorem in the form worth remembering: the truncation is not slightly better than a typical subspace, it is better by exactly the amount the spectrum says it should be.

    One consequence that is easy to miss: (2) is a statement about the matrix you passed in. Change the weighting and you change the objective. The tf-idf model has a larger reconstruction error than raw counts (\(8.84\) against \(7.56\) at \(k=4\)) and retrieves better. The SVD is optimal for its own criterion; if that criterion is not the one you care about, optimality is worth nothing.

    2. tf-idf, and what \(\log\) is doing there #

    Raw counts encode two things at once: what a document is about, and how long it is. A 2 000-word document has larger counts than a 200-word one for every term it shares, so its vector is longer and it wins every cosine by default. The term side has the mirror problem: the occurs in every document, so it has a large count everywhere and contributes a constant to every similarity.

    tf-idf addresses the second one. With \(\mathrm{df}_j = \lvert\{i : x_{ij} > 0\}\rvert\),

    \[w_j = \log\frac{n}{\mathrm{df}_j}, \qquad \tilde{x}_{ij} = x_{ij}\, w_j. \qquad (7)\]

    The \(\log\) is not derived from anything; it is chosen so that the weight is bounded and zero at the useless end. Without it, \(w_j = n/\mathrm{df}_j\) grows without bound as \(\mathrm{df}_j \to 1\) and a single rare term can dominate a document's vector. With it, \(w_j = 0\) exactly when a term is in every document, \(w_j = \log n\) when it is in one, and the scale is the log of the informativeness ratio. Smoothed variants (\(\log(1 + n/\mathrm{df})\), \(\log((1+n)/(1+\mathrm{df})) + 1\)) exist to keep \(w_j > 0\); the unsmoothed form is what this file uses because the zero is the feature.

    The important implementation point is that (7) is a corpus statistic. \(\mathrm{df}_j\) is a property of the training set. Fit it once, store it, reuse it. _term_weights is a separate function from _local_weight for exactly this reason: one is a per-document transform, the other is a fitted parameter.

    3. Log-entropy: presence is not the whole story #

    Let \(p_{ij} = x_{ij} / \sum_{i'} x_{i'j}\) be the fraction of term \(j\)'s occurrences that fall in document \(i\). Its entropy over documents,

    \[H_j = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_{ij}\log p_{ij} \;\in\; [0, \log n], \qquad (8)\]

    is maximal when the term is spread perfectly evenly and zero when it lives in one document. The global weight is the normalised complement,

    \[g_j = 1 - \frac{H_j}{\log n} \;\in\; [0, 1], \qquad (9)\]

    and the weighted matrix is \(\tilde{x}_{ij} = \log(1 + x_{ij})\, g_j\).

    Two design choices are bundled here, and they are independent:

    • the global weight \(g_j\) replaces \(\log(n/\mathrm{df}_j)\), and it is a strictly finer statistic: \(\mathrm{df}\) is the support of \(p_{\cdot j}\), \(H_j\) is its whole shape;
    • the local weight is \(\log(1+\mathrm{tf})\) rather than \(\mathrm{tf}\), which is a within-document decision and needs no corpus: the twentieth occurrence of a term says much less than the second.

    The difference matters on a term that appears in every document. Consider the demo's tiny matrix,

    \[T = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 & 3\\ 1 & 1 & 2 & 3\\ 0 & 3 & 1 & 3\end{pmatrix}, \qquad (10)\]

    whose second column is \((1,1,3)\) and fourth is \((3,3,3)\). Both have \(\mathrm{df} = n = 3\), so tf-idf gives both weight zero and deletes them. But the fourth is flat — it says nothing about which document you are reading — while the second is concentrated in document 3, which is a real signal. The demo measures \(g = (0.421,\,0.135,\,0.421,\,0.000)\): the fourth is deleted, the second is kept, and the two weightings disagree about a term both of them can see.

    One edge case is worth stating because it is the degenerate-corpus failure mode in this file. For \(n = 1\), \(\log n = 0\) and (9) is \(0/0\). The guard divides by \(1\) instead, which makes a one-document corpus have all weights zero — correct in the sense that there is nothing to distinguish, and a reminder that these weights are corpus statistics, not per-document ones.

    4. Row normalisation, and why L2 #

    Cosine similarity between documents \(a\) and \(b\) is

    \[\cos(a, b) = \frac{a^\top b}{\lVert a \rVert\, \lVert b \rVert}, \qquad (11)\]

    so if every row is scaled to unit norm, similarity is the dot product and the retrieval step is one matrix multiply. That is the only reason norm=True exists, and it is the second half of the length normalisation: tf-idf stops ubiquitous terms from dominating, row normalisation stops long documents from doing it.

    L2 rather than L1 because the SVD's geometry is Euclidean. (2) minimises a Frobenius norm, so "close" in the latent space means "close in \(\ell_2\)"; if the rows were L1-normalised, the space in which similarity was measured and the space in which the approximation was optimal would not be the same one, and the low-rank model's guarantee would not apply to the quantity being used.

    With unit rows, the leading singular direction is the axis of largest scatter of the document cloud. When the corpus shares a background vocabulary — which almost every corpus does — that axis is close to the mean document direction, which is why the first component reads as "general English" and is often discarded. This is the uncentred analogue of PCA's first component landing on the mean: PCA removes the mean and does not have the problem, LSA keeps it and has to throw the component away instead.

    5. The randomized SVD, and what power iteration buys #

    Draw \(\Omega \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times (k+p)}\) with i.i.d. Gaussians and form

    \[Y = X\Omega \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (k+p)}. \qquad (12)\]

    With probability at least \(1 - 3p^{-p}\), the range of \(Y\) captures the top \(k\) singular directions up to an error of order \(\sigma_{k+1}\). A Gaussian sketch is not the cheapest choice — a subsampled random Fourier transform or a sparse sign matrix works — but it is the one with the cleanest bound and no structural assumption on \(X\), which for a count matrix with a handful of very large entries is worth the constant.

    The sketch is sharpened by alternating products:

    \[Y_q = \left(XX^\top\right)^q X\Omega, \qquad (13)\]

    and in the SVD basis this multiplies the \(i\)-th direction's amplitude by \(\sigma_i^{2q+1}\). The ratio between the last wanted and the first unwanted direction goes from \(\sigma_k/\sigma_{k+1}\) to

    \[\left(\frac{\sigma_k}{\sigma_{k+1}}\right)^{2q+1}, \qquad (14)\]

    which is the entire reason a few iterations are enough. On the demo's spectrum \((\sigma_4, \sigma_5) = (4.41, 1.91)\), a ratio of \(2.3\): after eight power steps the gap is \(2.3^{17} \approx 1.4 \times 10^6\), and the measured subspace error drops from \(3 \times 10^{-1}\) to \(10^{-9}\).

    The rest is bookkeeping. Orthonormalise the sketch, \(Y = QR\); project the data, \(B = Q^\top X\), which is \((k+p) \times d\); decompose \(B = U_b S V^\top\); then \(X \approx Q U_b S V^\top\) and the factors are \((QU_b, S, V^\top)\). Q @ Ub is exactly the orthogonal factor the exact SVD would have produced in the range of the sketch, which is why the two agree to machine precision once the sketch is good.

    Oversampling \(p\) is not cosmetic. The sketch has \(k+p\) columns, so even a perfect rank-\((k+p)\) approximation has room for \(p\) directions of leakage. The standard bound has the shape \(\left[1 + c\sqrt{k+p}\,(p-1)^{-1}\sqrt{\min(n,d)}\right]^{1/(2q+1)}\) for an absolute constant \(c\) of order one, and the two appearances of \(p\) pull in opposite directions — a wider sketch is a better subspace, but the concentration constant degrades as \(p\) shrinks. \(p = 10\) is the usual compromise for dense data, and it is what this file's default is.

    6. What the decomposition does and does not identify #

    \(X = U S V^\top\) is unique when the singular values are distinct, but the pair of factors is not. For any diagonal \(D\) with entries \(\pm 1\),

    \[(U S D)(D V)^\top = U S D D V^\top = U S V^\top, \qquad (15)\]

    so the sign of each component is arbitrary — a convention, not an estimate. When \(\sigma_i = \sigma_j\) the ambiguity is larger: any rotation in that \(2 \times 2\) block gives the same \(X\), which is the same non-identifiability factor analysis has, in a setting where it is harmless. The demo's singular values are distinct (\(9.1\) against \(1.9\) for the first discarded one), so the only freedom is sign.

    Sign is invisible to everything the model is used for. Cosine similarity is invariant under \(a \mapsto -a\), so retrieval does not see it; the reconstruction is invariant under the paired flip of (15), so inverse_transform does not see it either. What does see it is interpretation: a topic component and its negation have the same set of large-magnitude terms, but opposite signs, so "topic 3 is about finance" is a statement about the row's absolute values. The sign is the one bit of an LSA component you have to fix by convention before writing the row down.

    The split between documents_ and components_ is a second convention. \(U S\) and \(V^\top\) together reconstruct \(X\), but the distribution of the singular values between them is a choice. This file puts \(S\) on the documents, which is the standard LSA convention and the one that makes transform and documents_ agree: \(X_w V_k = U_k S_k\).

    7. Why this is not PCA #

    PCA decomposes the centred matrix: \(X_c = X - \mathbf{1}\mu^\top\), then \(X_c = U S V^\top\). LSA decomposes \(X\) itself. Three reasons, in increasing order of importance:

    1. The origin is meaningful. A row of zeros is a document with no words in it, not a document of average length. Centring moves that row to \(-\mu\), which is not a document.
    2. The mean is an artefact of corpus composition. Add a thousand documents about sport to a corpus of recipes and the mean moves; LSA's factors move with it only through the shared terms, PCA's first component moves to follow the new mean.
    3. Centring and weighting do not commute. tf-idf is already a nonlinear transform of the counts; centring after it produces negative entries, which is fine for PCA's algebra and wrong for the model — a negative count is not a count. PCA on tf-idf vectors is a legitimate technique, but it is a different atom, and it is not in this file.

    The demo asserts the mean is not zero (\(0.67\)), which is the numerical form of the same statement.

    Code walkthrough #

    LSA(n_components=100, *, variant="tfidf_lsa", seed=0, n_iter=4, oversample=10, norm=True) with fit, transform, fit_transform and inverse_transform, and the attributes components_ \((k, n_{\text{terms}})\), documents_ \((n_{\text{docs}}, k)\), singular_values_, weights_, explained_variance_ratio_ and reconstruction_error_.

    Two conventions are load-bearing and consistent with the rest of the repo. components_ is (k, n_terms) — one row per latent dimension — so a document vector is Xw @ components_.T, and documents_ is U S, not U, so that the two live in the same coordinates. weights_ is exposed because it is the part of the model a reader can inspect and argue with: the topic matrix is opaque, the fact that idf gave a term weight zero is not.

    norm=True is the default because raw counts plus no normalisation is the model that gets 55% in the table above. n_iter=4 is the randomized solver's power-iteration count and is ignored by the exact one.

    1. The two halves of a weighting #

    def _local_weight(X: np.ndarray, kind: str) -> np.ndarray:
    """The term-frequency part: raw counts, or `log(1 + tf)` for log-entropy.

    The log is there to stop one term that a document happens to repeat from
    dominating its vector; it is a within-document decision and needs no corpus.
    """
    return np.log1p(X) if kind == "log_entropy" else X

    The split between _local_weight and _term_weights is the design decision that makes the atom honest. A weighting has a per-document factor and a per-corpus factor; convolving them into one function is how implementations end up recomputing the corpus statistic at query time. Here the local half takes only X and a string, and the corpus half is a separate function that returns a vector to be stored.

    2. The corpus half: idf #

        n, d = X.shape
    if kind == "tf":
        return np.ones(d)
    if kind == "tfidf":
        df = np.count_nonzero(X > 0.0, axis=0)
        return np.log(n / np.maximum(df, 1))

    (7). np.count_nonzero(X > 0.0, axis=0) is \(\mathrm{df}_j\) and the np.maximum(df, 1) guards the all-zero column, which a term that appears in no document at all would produce — log(n/0) is \(+\infty\), and an infinite weight on a column of zeros is harmless in exact arithmetic and a NaN factory in floating point. The axis=0 is the one that matters: documents are rows, so "how many documents contain term \(j\)" is a count down a column.

    3. The corpus half: entropy #

        total = X.sum(axis=0)
    p = X / np.maximum(total, EPS)                    # term j's mass over documents
    with np.errstate(divide="ignore", invalid="ignore"):
        plogp = np.where(X > 0.0, p * np.log(np.maximum(p, EPS)), 0.0)
    entropy = -plogp.sum(axis=0)                      # in nats, in [0, log n]
    # a one-document corpus has entropy 0 by definition and nothing to weigh
    return 1.0 - entropy / (math.log(n) if n > 1 else 1.0)

    (8) and (9). The axis is the same as above and the roles are swapped: total is per term, and p distributes each term over the documents. np.where(X > 0.0, ...) implements the \(0\log 0 = 0\) convention explicitly rather than relying on a masked nan; np.errstate silences the division for the zero entries, which would otherwise warn on every call. The np.maximum(p, EPS) inside the log is what stops those zero entries from taking \(\log 0 = -\infty\) before where gets a chance to discard them; the resulting entropy is not clipped, because \(0 \log 0\) is taken as zero by the where and \(p = 1\) gives \(\log 1 = 0\).

    The single-document branch is the \(n = 1\) problem from the derivation's §3, and it is in the code rather than in a comment because the alternative is a NaN that propagates into every weight and turns the whole fit into nan.

    4. Fitting: two decisions and their composition #

            X = self._check(X)
        n, d = X.shape
        k = min(self.n_components, n, d)
        self.weights_ = _term_weights(X, self.weighting)
        Xw = self._weighted(X)
        if self.variant == "randomized":
            rng = np.random.default_rng(self.seed)
            U, s, Vt = _randomized_svd(Xw, k, self.n_iter, self.oversample, rng)
        else:
            U, s, Vt = _svd(Xw, k)
        self.components_ = Vt
        self.singular_values_ = s
        self.documents_ = U * s

    The order is the point: weights first, then the weighted matrix, then the solver. self.weights_ is stored before _weighted runs, so a caller can recover the corpus statistic even though it is no longer separable from the product. k = min(...) is a silent truncation: asking for 100 components from a \(240 \times 60\) matrix gives 60, the smaller dimension, and components_ is honest about its own shape.

    U * s rather than U is the LSA convention and it makes the two coordinate systems agree: the reconstruction is (U S) Vᵀ, and a query is projected as Xw Vᵀ, which is the same map. The demo asserts the two agree on the training matrix to \(2 \times 10^{-15}\).

    5. The weighting at transform time #

            Xw = _local_weight(X, self.weighting) * self.weights_
        if self.norm:
            # rows on the same scale, which makes cosine similarity a dot product
            nrm = np.linalg.norm(Xw, axis=1, keepdims=True)
            Xw = Xw / np.maximum(nrm, EPS)
        return Xw

    self.weights_, not _term_weights(X, ...). Two characters separate a model from a normalisation, and the difference is measurable: the demo's single-document query has norm \(0.87\) with the stored weights and would have norm \(0\) with recomputed ones. The np.maximum(nrm, EPS) is the empty-document guard; a row of zeros stays a row of zeros rather than becoming a NaN.

    6. The sketch, the power loop, and the small SVD #

        n, d = X.shape
    width = min(k + oversample, min(n, d))
    omega = rng.standard_normal((d, width))
    Y = X @ omega
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        Y = X @ (X.T @ Y)
    Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(Y)                            # orthonormal basis of the sketch
    Ub, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Q.T @ X, full_matrices=False)
    return (Q @ Ub)[:, :k], s[:k], Vt[:k]

    (12) and (13). width = min(k + oversample, min(n, d)) is the only line that is not straight from the paper: a sketch wider than the matrix cannot have full rank, so the extra columns are wasted work and the small SVD they produce has guaranteed zero singular values at the bottom. Clamping is cheaper than explaining them. The power loop is written as X @ (X.T @ Y) and not (X @ X.T) @ Y because the former is two matrix-vector-block products and the latter forms an \(n \times n\) matrix — at \(n = 10^6\) that is the difference between 8 GB and a few megabytes.

    Q.T @ X is \((k+p) \times d\), so the second SVD is a small one; the returned Q @ Ub is the left factor the exact solver would have given, restricted to the sketch's range. The demo checks that this converges to the exact subspace rather than merely claiming it: \(3 \times 10^{-1}\) with no power iteration, \(2 \times 10^{-3}\) with two, \(10^{-9}\) with eight.

    7. Eckart–Young, as an identity #

        recon = U[:, :k] * s[:k] @ lsa.components_
    tail = float(np.sum(s[k:] ** 2))
    resid = float(np.linalg.norm(Xw - recon))
    assert abs(resid ** 2 - tail) < 1e-10, (resid ** 2, tail)

    The demo does not compare the truncated SVD against a random matrix and declare victory; it checks (5)'s equality case, which is a much stronger statement. The random comparison is there too, but against the right thing: 200 random rank-4 subspaces, each projected exactly, average \(14.96\) against the predicted \(\sqrt{(1-k/d)\sum\sigma^2} = 14.97\), and none of them reaches \(7.5624\). A Gaussian random matrix would have been a meaningless baseline — its scale is arbitrary, so beating it proves nothing.

    U[:, :k] * s[:k] @ lsa.components_ is (U S) Vᵀ written with the same orientation as the model, so the check is comparing the model's own reconstruction with the one computed from a fresh SVD. If components_ were transposed, or if documents_ were U instead of U S, this line would fail.

    8. The single-document query #

        q = LSA(k, variant="tfidf_lsa").fit(X[:200]).transform(X[205:206])
    assert float(np.linalg.norm(q)) > 0.5, q
    assert np.all(_term_weights(X[205:206], "tfidf") == 0.0)

    Two assertions that are the same fact from both sides. The first says the fitted model embeds a one-document query with a real direction; the second says the unfitted weighting gives every term in that document weight zero, because \(\mathrm{df}_j = n = 1\) makes \(\log(n/\mathrm{df}_j) = \log 1 = 0\). If transform called _term_weights instead of using self.weights_, the first assertion would be 0.0 > 0.5 and the second would still pass — which is precisely the kind of bug that survives a test suite that only checks shapes.

    9. The sign, and the ambiguity that cancels #

        sig = np.array([-1.0, 1.0, 1.0, -1.0])
    rec = first.inverse_transform(first.documents_)
    flipped = (first.documents_ * sig) @ (first.components_ * sig[:, None])
    assert np.max(np.abs(flipped - rec)) < 1e-12

    (15), as an experiment. sig is a diagonal of \(\pm 1\); multiplying the columns of documents_ by it and the matching rows of components_ by it cancels in the product, so the reconstruction is unchanged to \(10^{-12}\) while the factors differ by a sign. The demo also checks the stronger statement that a negated component is still an exact eigenvector — both signs satisfy \(V X^\top X = \lambda V\) to \(3 \times 10^{-9}\) — so there is no equation in the model that could pick between them.

    10. Back to term space, and what the check refuses #

            Z = np.asarray(Z, dtype=float)
        if Z.ndim != 2 or Z.shape[1] != self.components_.shape[0]:
            raise ValueError(f"expected (m, {self.components_.shape[0]}), got {Z.shape}")
        return Z @ self.components_

    inverse_transform is one product and one shape check, and it is worth having because it makes the model's claim testable: inverse_transform(fit_transform(X)) is the rank-\(k\) approximation of the weighted matrix, so the residual can be compared against (2). The shape check is not decoration — it catches the transposed-components_ convention error at the boundary rather than three functions later.

            if np.isnan(X).any() or (X < 0.0).any():
            raise ValueError("LSA needs a non-negative, complete count matrix")

    The refusal is the model, not a limitation. Log weights are undefined for negative entries, np.log1p is not monotone below \(-1\), and idf's df is a count of presence, which a negative entry is not. A centred matrix — the thing PCA would happily take — is rejected here, and the error says why.

    Complexity #

    \(n\) documents, \(d\) terms, \(k\) components, \(p\) oversampling, \(q\) power iterations.

    Operation Time Memory Note
    _term_weights \(O(nd)\) \(O(nd)\) one pass for df, one for the entropy
    _weighted \(O(nd)\) \(O(nd)\) materialises the weighted matrix
    _svd \(O(nd\min(n,d))\) \(O(nd)\) one full decomposition
    _randomized_svd \(O(nd(k+p)q)\) \(O(n(k+p) + nd)\) \(q\) passes over \(X\)
    transform \(O(mdk)\) \(O(mk)\) one product per query batch
    inverse_transform \(O(mdk)\) \(O(md)\) back to term space

    The interesting comparison is the first two rows against the third and fourth. Weighting is linear in the size of the data and cannot be avoided: you have to touch every entry once to know how often each term appears. The solver is where the asymptotics differ, and the randomized one wins only when \(k+p \ll \min(n,d)\) and the matrix is too large for a full SVD — for \(240 \times 60\) the exact decomposition is microseconds and the sketch is pure overhead.

    Memory is the harder constraint in practice. _weighted materialises a dense \(n \times d\) matrix, which for a million documents and a \(10^5\)-term vocabulary is \(8 \times 10^{11}\) bytes. This file is deliberately dense; the sparse version of LSA is a different piece of engineering (sparse df, sparse matvec, Lanczos on the normal equations), and the algebra is unchanged.

    The whole five-section demo runs in \(0.08\) s, most of it in the SVDs and the randomized fits. That is the point of a \(240 \times 60\) example: the algorithms are not the bottleneck, the modelling choices are.

    One line in fit is worth calling out as a small piece of the same lesson. explained_variance_ratio_ needs \(\lVert X_w\rVert_F^2\), and the obvious way to get it is a second SVD; np.sum(Xw ** 2) is the same number in \(O(nd)\) with no decomposition at all. The reference implementation had the SVD, which would have made the randomized variant pointless at scale — it would have paid the full cost to measure how much it had saved.

    Failure modes #

    1. Recomputing the weights at query time silently zeroes the query. The demo measures \(\lVert z \rVert = 0.87\) with the fitted idf and exactly \(0\) with the recomputed one, because a single document has \(\mathrm{df} = n\) for every term it contains. Nothing raises; a zero vector has an argmax, so a retrieval pipeline built this way returns document 1 for every query and looks like a bad model rather than a bug. The fix is structural: fit the weights, store them, apply them.

    2. There is no natural \(k\), and the spectrum's cliff is a property of the data. The demo's singular values are \(9.1, 6.9, 5.8, 4.4, 1.9, 1.8, 1.7, 1.6, \ldots\): a factor of \(2.3\) between the fourth and fifth, then ratios near \(1.06\). The cliff is real — the corpus was generated from four topics — but a real corpus has no such guarantee, and a flat tail gives no defensible \(k\). Choosing \(k\) by explained variance is choosing it by reconstruction, which is not the task: the demo's table shows the same \(k\) giving 55% and 90% under different weightings. Choose \(k\) on a held-out retrieval or classification metric, or fix it at a budget.

    3. Dense only. Every operation here is \(O(nd)\) in memory. A term-document matrix from a real corpus is 99.9% zeros, and the dense representation is the wrong data structure by four orders of magnitude. This is not a tuning issue; it is the difference between a demo and a system.

    4. tf-idf deletes terms that are informative. \(\mathrm{df}\) is binary, so a term in every document gets weight zero regardless of how it is distributed. The \(3 \times 4\) demo's second column, \((1,1,3)\), is exactly that case: tf-idf removes it, log-entropy keeps it, and there is no way to fix it inside tf-idf without switching to the entropy form. On a corpus where the distinction matters, the two weightings give materially different topics.

    5. A one-document corpus is degenerate. \(\log n = 0\) makes (9) undefined; the guard returns all-zero weights, which is the correct limit and a warning that entropy weighting needs a corpus. It is also the shape of a more common mistake: fitting on a sample of one class from each category and then applying the weights to the full data.

    6. LSA is linear and bag-of-words. It cannot represent word order, negation, or two senses of one word. bank gets one vector, which is a mixture of the river and the finance senses, and every document containing it is pulled towards both. This is the limitation that dense contextual embeddings exist to address, and no amount of rank tuning removes it.

    7. Randomized without power iteration is a different algorithm. With n_iter=0 the subspace gap is \(3 \times 10^{-1}\) and the singular values are wrong in the first decimal; the fit still returns a plausible-looking topic matrix and a plausible-looking accuracy. The iteration is not an optimisation — it is what makes the sketch an approximation of the top of the spectrum rather than of a random subspace.

    8. Sign and scale are not identified. §6: a component and its negation are both exact solutions, and two fits can differ by one. Anything that compares components across fits — "topic 1 in model A is topic 2 in model B" — has to align signs first, and anything that treats a loading's sign as meaningful has to fix a convention. Cosine-based retrieval is immune; interpretation is not.

    9. The reconstruction is not a document. inverse_transform returns real numbers, including negative ones, because a low-rank approximation of a non-negative matrix is not non-negative. Rounding it to counts is not "denoising"; it is a different model (non-negative matrix factorisation, the neighbouring atom).

    10. Explained variance and retrieval quality disagree. The raw-count model reconstructs its own matrix better (\(7.56\) against \(8.84\) at \(k=4\)) and retrieves worse (\(55\%\) against \(90\%\) at \(k=2\)). Those are different matrices, so the numbers are not a like-for-like comparison — which is exactly the point: the SVD is optimal for whichever matrix it is given, and the matrix is a modelling choice. A pipeline that tunes the solver and not the weighting has optimised the wrong thing.

    11. Truncating at the smaller dimension is silent. n_components=100 on a \(240 \times 60\) matrix yields 60, and every downstream index that assumed 100 is off by 40. The attribute is right; the constructor argument is not a promise.

    Applications #

    • Information retrieval. The original application (Deerwester et al. 1990): match a query against documents that share no query terms, by projecting both into the latent space. It matched lexical search on some TREC collections and lost on others, which is the honest summary of the evaluations; it is the textbook example of a retrieval method that works because of a modelling insight rather than a better index.
    • Cross-language retrieval. Build the term-document matrix over a parallel corpus with both languages' terms as columns; the latent dimensions align translations, and a query in one language retrieves documents in the other. The alignment is a consequence of shared context, and it is the cleanest demonstration that the low-rank structure is semantic rather than lexical.
    • Document clustering and classification. The \(k\)-dimensional coordinates are a feature map: cheap, dense, and often as good as a bag-of-words model with a hundred times the dimension. Standard preprocessing before a linear classifier.
    • Topic segmentation and summarisation. A document's row in \(U_k S_k\) is a point in topic space, and consecutive documents trace a trajectory through it; a change point in that trajectory is a section boundary. Crude, and it works.
    • Recommendation. Treat users as documents and items as terms, or the reverse; the SVD of a rating or interaction matrix is the same algebra, and the "latent factors" are the same latent factors. The difference from a proper recommender is that the missing entries are zeros here, which biases everything towards popular items.
    • Duplicate and near-duplicate detection. Cosine in the latent space is robust to paraphrase in a way that lexical overlap is not, and the sketch is cheap enough to run over a large corpus.
    • As the ancestor of embeddings. Levy & Goldberg (2014) showed that word2vec's skip-gram with negative sampling is implicitly factorising a shifted pointwise-mutual-information matrix. LSA is the explicit version of that with a different matrix: the modern method learns the matrix and the factorisation together, and inherits the same geometry.

    Where it is a trap: tuning \(k\) on reconstruction error; fitting the weighting on a query batch; comparing topics across two fits without aligning signs; reading a component's sign as meaningful; and using LSA on data that is not counts at all — the idf and entropy weights are statistics of a count matrix, and applying them to, say, tf-idf'd embeddings is a category error.
