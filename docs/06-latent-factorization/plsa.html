    06 · Latent structure & factorisation/tier 3/3 variants/source

    Probabilistic LSA

    Intuition #

    A document × term matrix of counts is the most common object in text analysis, and it has two obvious low-rank stories. The first is linear algebra: truncate an SVD and call the result latent semantic analysis. That works, and it produces factors with negative entries that are not probabilities of anything. The second is probabilistic: say that each document is a mixture over topics, each topic is a distribution over words, and the word distribution of a document is the mixture.

                     terms (V = 60)
              ┌───────┬───────┬───────┐
   d  ┌───────┼───────┼───────┼───────┤
   o  │       │  ···  │       │       │   each row: counts of 80 tokens
   c  │       │       │  ···  │       │   each row is a mixture of 3 blocks
   s  │       │       │       │  ···  │
      └───────┴───────┴───────┴───────┘
                 z=1     z=2     z=3

    The generative story is two lines. To produce one word of document \(d\): draw a topic \(z\) with probability \(p(z\mid d)\), then draw a word with probability \(p(w\mid z)\). Marginalise the topic you did not observe and the document's word distribution is

    \[p(w\mid d) \;=\; \sum_{z=1}^{Z} p(w\mid z)\,p(z\mid d). \qquad (1)\]

    Everything the model knows is in two stochastic matrices: doc_topic_ \((D\times Z)\), how much of each topic each document uses, and topic_word_ \((Z\times V)\), what each topic says. The counts are the only observed thing, and the likelihood is the only objective.

    That objective is where the trouble is. Maximum likelihood has no term that charges for parameters, so on the demo's corpus — 120 documents, 80 tokens each, 60 terms in three disjoint blocks, generated from exactly three topics — asking for twenty topics produces a higher likelihood per token than the three that generated the data (\(-3.47257\) against \(-3.58817\)) while recovering the planted topics at cosine \(0.5978\) instead of \(0.9971\). The extra topics are not describing anything; they are absorbing sampling noise, and the likelihood cannot tell the difference. That is the whole content of pLSA: a clean, identifiable, closed-form latent-variable model with no complexity control whatsoever.

    Variants #

    Variant What changes Update Cost per iteration
    em nothing — the model of (1) exact E and M steps \(O(DVZ)\)
    tempered_em responsibilities raised to \(\beta < 1\), annealed to 1 same M-step, tempered E-step \(O(DVZ)\)
    map_plsa Dirichlet prior on each topic's word distribution \(\alpha-1\) added to every count \(O(DVZ)\)

    em is the reference: alternate the exact posterior over topics with the closed-form maximisation. On the demo it recovers the three planted topics at mean cosine \(0.9971\) in 388 iterations, with the log-likelihood rising on every one of the 387 steps. That is EM's guarantee and the demo measures it rather than assuming it.

    tempered_em is a schedule, not a different estimator: with \(\beta = 1\) it must reproduce em exactly, and the demo checks that the two final likelihoods agree to \(0.0\). The reason to anneal is that the likelihood surface has local optima and EM walks downhill from wherever it starts; flattening the responsibilities early widens the basins, then raising the temperature sharpens them back to the true posterior. The demo's measurement is unambiguous at six topics: with \(\beta = 0.8\) climbing linearly to 1 over the first 150 iterations it beats plain em on all four starts, by \(15.6\) nats on average and \(22.0\) at best, from the same initialisation.

    The same code at \(\beta = 0.4\) is a catastrophe, and the demo shows exactly why. Responsibilities that flat hand every topic nearly the same counts; identical rows produce identical responsibilities; the map preserves that symmetry exactly, so the run stops in 23 iterations at a fixed point where all three topics agree to \(6.5\times10^{-6}\) in every entry and every document is \(0.3334\) of each. The likelihood falls on all 22 steps, to \(-38896.3\) against \(-34446.5\) for em. The temperature is a hyper-parameter with a cliff on the low side.

    map_plsa adds a symmetric \(\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\alpha)\) prior to every topic's word distribution, which adds \(\alpha-1\) to each count before normalising. At \(\alpha = 1\) that is exactly em (measured: \(0.0\) difference in likelihood), because concentration 1 is the uniform prior and the uniform prior is no prior. Above 1 it smooths: the demo's mean topic entropy climbs \(2.928 \to 3.367 \to 3.749\) nats for \(\alpha = 1, 5, 20\), the smallest entry of topic_word_ goes from exactly \(0\) to \(1.1\times10^{-3}\) and \(4.2\times10^{-3}\), and the training likelihood per token pays for it, \(-3.4771 \to -3.5103 \to -3.6041\). That price buys the one thing em cannot do: when the demo hands the model ten tokens of a term that appears in no training document, em's perplexity is inf, because one unseen word multiplies the likelihood by zero and no arithmetic recovers it, while \(\alpha = 5\) gives \(768.2\).

    Derivation #

    1. The likelihood #

    Documents are independent, words within a document are independent given the topics, so with \(n(d,w)\) the count of term \(w\) in document \(d\) the likelihood is

    \[\mathcal{L} \;=\; \prod_{d=1}^{D}\prod_{w=1}^{V} \Big[\sum_{z} p(w\mid z)\,p(z\mid d)\Big]^{n(d,w)}, \qquad (2)\]

    a product over observed tokens, not over cells. The constraint set is two simplices per document, \(\sum_z p(z\mid d) = 1\), and one per topic, \(\sum_w p(w\mid z) = 1\), and (2) is not concave on it: the sum inside the log is where the non-convexity lives.

    2. The complete-data view #

    Introduce a distribution \(q_{dwz}\) over which topic produced word \(w\) in document \(d\). Multiplying and dividing inside the log by \(q\) and applying Jensen gives, for any \(q\) on the simplex,

    \[\log \mathcal{L} \;\ge\; \sum_{d,w} n(d,w)\sum_z q_{dwz} \Big[\log p(z\mid d) + \log p(w\mid z) - \log q_{dwz}\Big], \qquad (3)\]

    with equality exactly when \(q\) is the posterior. Writing \(H(q)\) for the entropy term, the bound is the usual decomposition

    \[\log \mathcal{L} \;=\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q\big[\log p(d,w,z)\big] + H(q)}_{\text{ELBO}} \;+\; \mathrm{KL}\big(q \,\Vert\, p(z\mid d,w)\big), \qquad (4)\]

    and (4) is the entire algorithm in one line: the E-step sets the KL term to zero by choosing the posterior, the M-step maximises the ELBO, and neither step can decrease the left-hand side. EM is the coordinate ascent this identity forces.

    3. The E-step #

    The posterior of a topic given one observed word is Bayes applied to (1):

    \[q_{dwz} \;=\; \frac{p(z\mid d)\,p(w\mid z)} {\sum_{z'} p(z'\mid d)\,p(w\mid z')}, \qquad (5)\]

    and it depends on the cell \((d,w)\) only through the word — every occurrence of the same word in the same document has the same responsibility. That is why the whole E-step is \(D\times V\times Z\) numbers rather than a loop over 9600 tokens.

    4. The M-step #

    Maximise the ELBO part of (3) subject to the two simplex constraints. With multipliers \(\lambda_d\) and \(\mu_z\),

    \[\frac{\partial}{\partial p(w\mid z)}\Big[\sum_{d,w} n(d,w) q_{dwz}\log p(w\mid z) - \mu_z\Big(\sum_w p(w\mid z)-1\Big)\Big] = 0, \qquad (6)\]

    so \(p(w\mid z) \propto \sum_d n(d,w)q_{dwz} = N_{zw}\), and normalising,

    \[p(w\mid z) \;=\; \frac{N_{zw}}{\sum_{w'} N_{zw'}}, \qquad N_{zw} = \sum_{d} n(d,w)\, q_{dwz}. \qquad (7)\]

    The document side is the same computation with the roles of \(d\) and \(w\) exchanged, and since \(\sum_z q_{dwz} = 1\) it simplifies to

    \[p(z\mid d) \;=\; \frac{\sum_w n(d,w) q_{dwz}}{n(d,\cdot)}. \qquad (8)\]

    Both are weighted averages of the data: (7) is "a topic's word distribution is the counts it takes responsibility for", (8) is "a document's mixture is the fraction of its tokens each topic explains". Nothing is solved iteratively inside a step; the only iteration is the alternation.

    5. What monotonicity does not say #

    (4) guarantees that the likelihood never falls, and that is the only guarantee. It says nothing about which stationary point is reached, and it says nothing about the number of topics. The demo's 20-topic fit is a better optimum of a bigger model on the same data, so the guarantee is satisfied and the answer is worse. Model selection has to come from somewhere else — a prior, a held-out criterion that actually penalises parameters, or a hypothesis about the data.

    6. Tempering #

    The tempered E-step raises the posterior to a power,

    \[q^{(\beta)}_{dwz} \;=\; \frac{\big[p(z\mid d)\,p(w\mid z)\big]^{\beta}} {\sum_{z'}\big[p(z'\mid d)\,p(w\mid z')\big]^{\beta}}, \qquad (9)\]

    which is the stationary point of the tilted free energy

    \[F_\beta(\theta) \;=\; -\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{d,w} n(d,w) \log \sum_{z}\big[p(z\mid d)\,p(w\mid z)\big]^{\beta}. \qquad (10)\]

    At \(\beta = 1\), (10) is the negative log-likelihood and (9) is (5). At \(\beta \to 0\) the expression inside the log flattens towards the uniform average, the responsibilities stop discriminating, and the M-step sees every topic receiving the same counts. The M-step in the code is unchanged — only the \(q\) that feeds it is tempered — which is why \(\beta = 1\) is an exact identity rather than an approximation.

    There is a fixed point at the bottom of that road. If topic_word_ ever has identical rows, then \(q_{dwz} = 1/Z\) for every \((d,w,z)\) at any \(\beta\), the M-step returns identical rows, and the symmetry is preserved forever. The demo's \(\beta = 0.4\) run reaches it to \(6.5\times10^{-6}\) and stops, because there is nothing left to move.

    7. The MAP variant #

    Put an independent \(\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\alpha)\) prior on each row of topic_word_. The penalised objective is the ELBO plus

    \[\log p\big(p(\cdot\mid z)\big) \;=\; (\alpha-1)\sum_w \log p(w\mid z) + \text{const}, \qquad (11)\]

    so the same Lagrangian as (6) gives

    \[p(w\mid z) \;=\; \frac{N_{zw} + \alpha - 1}{\sum_{w'}\big(N_{zw'} + \alpha - 1\big)}, \qquad (12)\]

    which is (7) with every count incremented. For \(\alpha > 1\) that is add-\(\alpha-1\) smoothing: no entry can be zero, because a zero would cost infinite prior density. For \(\alpha < 1\) the increment is negative and the closed form can go below zero, so implementations clamp at zero — the code does, and the clamp is a projection onto the simplex rather than the exact MAP.

    The consequence that matters is visible only out of sample. Under (7), a term with \(N_{zw} = 0\) for every \(z\) gets probability exactly zero, so any document containing it has likelihood zero and perplexity infinite. Under (12) with \(\alpha > 1\) every term gets a floor, and "unlikely" becomes expressible.

    8. Perplexity, and folding in #

    The held-out measure is per-token perplexity,

    \[\mathrm{ppl}(X) \;=\; \exp\!\left(-\frac{\log p(X)}{\sum_{d,w} n(d,w)}\right), \qquad (13)\]

    the geometric mean inverse probability the model assigns to a token. For a new document the topics are fixed and only the mixture is free, so folding in solves

    \[\hat\theta \;=\; \arg\min_{\theta \in \Delta_Z} -\sum_w n(d^*,w)\log \sum_z \theta_z\, p(w\mid z), \qquad (14)\]

    by EM on that one vector. Note what (14) is: a maximum-likelihood fit of \(Z\) free parameters to one document, not a posterior. It has enough freedom to compensate for a bad topic matrix, which is why the demo's held-out perplexity also prefers twenty topics — \(34.20\) against \(36.24\) for three — even though recovery has collapsed. Fold-in does not measure the model; it measures the model plus a per-document refit.

    9. Symmetry, and what is identified #

    Relabel the topics and nothing observable changes:

    \[p(z\mid d) \mapsto p(\sigma(z)\mid d), \qquad p(w\mid z) \mapsto p(w\mid \sigma(z)), \qquad (15)\]

    for any permutation \(\sigma\). That is the only exact ambiguity in the model: pLSA is a mixture of conditionally independent unigrams, and generically its parameters are determined up to (15) — which is why a recovered topic can be compared to a planted one at all, and why the demo's cosine is a meaningful score rather than a score of one representative of a rotation family, as in factor_analysis or Tucker. What is not unique is the optimisation: many local optima exist, and which one you reach depends on the start, which is the practical cost of the non-convexity in (2).

    Code walkthrough #

    PLSA(n_topics=3, *, variant="em", n_iter=200, tol=1e-7, beta=0.5, alpha=2.0, seed=0) with fit(X), transform(X, n_iter=100), perplexity(X) and the attributes topic_word_, doc_topic_, log_likelihood_, n_iter_. X is a (documents, terms) array of non-negative counts.

    1. A row-stochastic matrix with a dead-row guard #

        s = P.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    dead = s[:, 0] < EPS
    if np.any(dead):
        return np.where(dead[:, None], 1.0 / P.shape[1], P / np.maximum(s, EPS))
    return P / s

    Every update in the file ends in this function, because both factors are stochastic matrices. The guard is not decoration: with \(\alpha < 1\) in (12) the clamped update can zero a whole row, and a topic that has died then produces 0/0 in the next E-step and a nan two functions away from the cause. Replacing the dead row by the uniform distribution keeps the arithmetic finite and makes the death visible in the output instead of in a traceback.

    2. The E-step, in logs #

        log_joint = (np.log(np.maximum(doc_topic, EPS))[:, None, :]
                 + np.log(np.maximum(topic_word, EPS)).T[None, :, :])
    log_joint *= beta
    top = log_joint.max(axis=2, keepdims=True)
    q = np.exp(log_joint - top)
    return q / np.maximum(q.sum(axis=2, keepdims=True), EPS)

    (5) and (9) in one expression. The broadcast produces the full \((D,V,Z)\) array of log joints; the max subtraction is the log-sum-exp shift, without which a probability of \(10^{-300}\) underflows to zero and a document of rare words gets nan responsibilities. The beta line is the entire difference between the plain and tempered variants, applied before the shift so the shift stays exact.

    3. The M-step, and the prior #

                counts_word = np.einsum("dw,dwz->zw", X, q)
            if self.variant == "map_plsa":
                # Dirichlet(alpha) prior on each p(·|z): the MAP solution adds
                # alpha − 1 to every count, which is smoothing above 1 and
                # sparsification below it
                counts_word = np.maximum(counts_word + self.alpha - 1.0, 0.0)
            self.topic_word_ = _normalise_rows(counts_word)
            self.doc_topic_ = _normalise_rows(np.einsum("dw,dwz->dz", X, q))

    (7), (8) and (12). Both M-steps are the same contraction of the same \((D,V,Z)\) responsibility array against the counts, one summing over documents and one over terms — that symmetry is why pLSA is cheap, and it is also why the array itself is the cost. The clamp is where the code stops being exact MAP: for \(\alpha < 1\) the true MAP has zeros in it, the clamp forces that, and the projection is not the maximiser of (11) but the nearest point in the simplex.

    4. The temperature schedule #

            if self.variant != "tempered_em":
            return 1.0
        span = max(1, self.n_iter // 2)
        return self.beta + (1.0 - self.beta) * min(1.0, it / span)

    (10)'s schedule: linear from beta to 1 over the first half of the iteration budget, then exactly 1. Deterministic, which is what makes the demo reproducible, and monotone, which is what makes \(\beta = 1\) reachable in finite time. The half is a choice: too long and the run spends its budget at a temperature that is not the model, too short and the flattened phase is over before the basins have merged. The demo's \(\beta = 0.4\) failure is not a schedule bug — it is the temperature itself, and a slower ramp does not fix it.

    5. The likelihood, exactly #

        with np.errstate(divide="ignore"):
        log_joint = (np.log(doc_topic)[:, None, :]
                     + np.log(topic_word).T[None, :, :])
    top = log_joint.max(axis=2, keepdims=True)
    top = np.where(np.isfinite(top), top, 0.0)
    with np.errstate(divide="ignore"):
        log_sum = top[:, :, 0] + np.log(np.exp(log_joint - top).sum(axis=2))
    nz = X > 0
    return float(np.sum(X[nz] * log_sum[nz]))

    (2) without a floor. topic_word may legitimately contain zeros under em, and the whole point of the map_plsa demo is that those zeros make the held-out likelihood infinite: a simulated \(-\infty\) would turn the headline into a finite number. top = np.where(np.isfinite(top), top, 0.0) handles the all-\(-\infty\) column (a word no topic can produce) so that the shift does not produce nan, and log_sum is then \(-\infty\) as it should be. Only the nonzero cells contribute, which is both correct and faster on a sparse count matrix.

    6. Folding in #

            X = np.atleast_2d(np.asarray(X, dtype=float))
        rng = np.random.default_rng(self.seed)
        doc_topic = _normalise_rows(rng.random((X.shape[0], self.n_topics)))
        for _ in range(n_iter):
            q = _e_step(X, self.topic_word_, doc_topic)
            doc_topic = _normalise_rows(np.einsum("dw,dwz->dz", X, q))
        return doc_topic

    (14) by the same two steps as fit, with topic_word_ frozen. n_iter=100 and no convergence test is honest rather than lazy: the objective in (14) is a \(Z\)-parameter problem for each document, 100 sweeps is far past what it needs, and a per-document stopping rule would make the reported perplexity depend on a tolerance. Note that atleast_2d is what lets perplexity accept a single document, which the demo uses to score one probe.

    7. The stopping rule #

                if (len(self.log_likelihood_) > 1
                    and abs(self.log_likelihood_[-1] - self.log_likelihood_[-2])
                    < self.tol * max(abs(self.log_likelihood_[-1]), 1.0)):
                self.n_iter_ = it + 1
                break

    A relative change, because the likelihood scales with corpus size and an absolute threshold would mean a different stopping point on every dataset. The max(..., 1.0) guards the scale when the likelihood is small. This rule is also the reason the \(\beta = 0.4\) run ends at 23 iterations: a symmetric fixed point has a flat likelihood, so the relative change falls below tolerance while the fit is still terrible — convergence of the objective is not convergence to the answer.

    8. Scoring a recovery that is only identified up to a permutation #

        ln = learned / np.maximum(np.linalg.norm(learned, axis=1, keepdims=True), EPS)
    tn = truth / np.maximum(np.linalg.norm(truth, axis=1, keepdims=True), EPS)
    return float(np.mean(np.max(ln @ tn.T, axis=1)))

    (15) is the only ambiguity, so the score is: normalise both sides, take the best cosine for each learned topic, average. Max over the truth handles the permutation; mean over the learned topics means one perfect topic out of three scores \(1/3\), not \(1\). The absolute value is deliberately absent — pLSA's topics are non-negative distributions, so a negative cosine is a genuinely bad match and should not be folded back.

    Complexity #

    \(D\) documents, \(V\) terms, \(Z\) topics, \(T\) iterations, \(N = \sum_{d,w} n(d,w)\) tokens.

    Operation Time Memory Note
    E-step \(O(DVZ)\) \(O(DVZ)\) the responsibility array dominates
    M-step \(O(DVZ)\) \(O(VZ + DZ)\) two contractions of the same array
    one iteration \(O(DVZ)\) \(O(DVZ)\) independent of \(N\)
    full fit \(O(T\,DVZ)\) \(O(DVZ)\) \(T\) decided by the stopping rule
    fold-in, one document \(O(VZ)\) per sweep \(O(VZ)\) 100 sweeps
    parameters \(DZ\) (document mixtures) + \(ZV\) (topics) — see the failure modes
    full demo — — 20 fits, 2.7 s

    Two facts decide whether this runs on your data. The first is the memory of the responsibility array: \(D V Z\) floats, which is 43 thousand for the demo and \(10^9\) for a million documents over a thousand-term vocabulary at ten topics. Every serious implementation either streams over documents or keeps the responsibilities implicit — the M-step only needs their contractions, so a chunked \(O(VZ)\)-memory version is a few lines away, and the demo's version is the clear one because the demo's matrices are small.

    The second is the parameter count in the table's last row: \(DZ + ZV\), which grows with the number of documents. That is not an accounting detail, it is the reason pLSA overfits small corpora and the reason LDA replaces the per-document parameters with a distribution over them. A pLSA model of a corpus is not a generative model of the corpus's documents; it is a model with one free vector per document, fitted jointly.

    Convergence is linear, with a rate that degrades as the topics overlap — the fraction of missing information in the E-step is large exactly when a word is compatible with several topics. The demo's three disjoint blocks are the easy case and still take 388 iterations at a relative tolerance of \(10^{-9}\); real text with overlapping topics takes several times that, which is why practical fits use loose tolerances and compare restarts rather than waiting for the last digit.

    Failure modes #

    1. The likelihood cannot count parameters. The demo's 20-topic fit is better by every number the objective can see — likelihood per token \(-3.47257\) against \(-3.58817\) — and recovers the three planted topics at \(0.5978\) instead of \(0.9971\). The extra topics split the noise, and the model is not penalised for it. Choose \(Z\) by a prior, by a held-out criterion that does not refit per document, or by what you claim the data is.

    2. Held-out perplexity is not that criterion. On 60 training documents and 60 held out, the demo gets \(36.24\) with three topics and \(34.20\) with twenty: the usual defence prefers the wrong model. Fold-in (14) fits a fresh \(Z\)-vector to each held-out document, and that freedom compensates for topic estimates that have nothing to do with the truth. This is a known weakness of the metric, not a bug in this implementation — and it is measured here rather than assumed.

    3. Zero is absorbing. Under em, a term that no topic takes responsibility for keeps probability exactly zero forever: the E-step multiplies by it, the M-step gets no counts back, and the state reproduces itself. Out of sample this is a single word turning perplexity into inf. The prior in map_plsa is the fix, and it is a real cost — \(-3.4771 \to -3.5103 \to -3.6041\) per token as \(\alpha\) rises through 5 and 20.

    4. The wrong temperature collapses the model to one topic. At \(\beta = 0.4\) the demo's run dies in 23 iterations with all three topics agreeing to \(6.5\times10^{-6}\) and every document at \(0.3334\) of each. This is a fixed point, not a transient: identical rows give uniform responsibilities, which give identical rows. If a tempered run converges suspiciously fast to a suspiciously bad likelihood, check the rows for agreement before blaming the data.

    5. Local optima are the norm, and the start is the only exploration. Two restarts from different seeds land on different likelihoods; the demo's tempered_em gain of \(15.6\) nats on average is precisely the size of that spread. Report the best of several runs and the spread, and compare recovered topics across runs — the likelihood alone will not tell you whether you are in the same basin.

    6. The mixture is per document, so "the model of the corpus" is a fiction. doc_topic_ has \(DZ\) entries, one vector per document, all fitted. pLSA does not define a distribution over documents, so you cannot sample a new one from it; transform is a fit, not a draw. This is the structural difference from LDA and the reason pLSA's perplexity numbers are not comparable to LDA's.

    7. The responsibilities are \(D\times V\times Z\). Memory, not time, is the constraint at scale, and it fails as an allocation far from the line that chose the algorithm. Chunk over documents; the contractions do not need the whole array at once.

    8. Overlapping topics make the cosine score look worse than the model is. The demo's planted topics are disjoint blocks, so recovery is a clean number. Real topics overlap, a fitted topic can be a blend of two true ones, and the mean-best cosine then understates the fit. Compare topic co-occurrence structure or downstream task performance when the ground truth is not known.

    Applications #

    • Information retrieval. pLSA was introduced as probabilistic latent semantic indexing: query and document are projected into the same topic simplex and matched there, which handles synonymy and polysemy that a term-matching baseline cannot. The original paper's retrieval results are still the cleanest demonstration of why the probabilistic formulation is worth the trouble.
    • Text classification and clustering with few labels. Topics as features, doc_topic_ as the representation. The per-document mixture is a compact summary that a small classifier can use, and it is fitted unsupervised.
    • Image and audio annotation. Any co-occurrence matrix of "documents" × "terms" works: images × visual words, tracks × audio tokens, users × items. The model does not know what a document is, which is a strength and a trap.
    • A baseline for topic models. Every LDA paper compares against pLSA, and reproducing it is the fastest way to check a new inference routine: the likelihood is exact in closed form at every iteration, so a wrong E-step shows up immediately as a non-monotone likelihood.
    • Teaching the ELBO. Equations (3)–(4) are the cleanest example of the variational decomposition in the field: a discrete latent variable, an exact posterior, and a coordinate ascent whose two steps both have closed forms.
    • Segmentation and change detection. Fit topics to windows of text and track doc_topic_ across the windows; the mixture vector is a low-dimensional signal and a shift in it is a topic change.

    Where it is a trap: choosing \(Z\) by likelihood; trusting held-out perplexity with fold-in; reporting topics when the model has \(DZ\) parameters and the corpus is small; using a tempered schedule with a low starting temperature; and expecting pLSA to generate documents, which it structurally cannot.

    References #

    • Hofmann (1999), Probabilistic latent semantic indexing — the aspect model, the EM algorithm, and the retrieval results that made the probabilistic reading of LSA credible.
    • Hofmann (2001), Unsupervised learning by probabilistic latent semantic analysis — the journal treatment, including the tempered variant and the discussion of local optima that the annealing is meant to address.
    • Dempster, Laird & Rubin (1977), Maximum likelihood from incomplete data via the EM algorithm — the monotonicity result behind equation (4), and the reason a rising likelihood is a guarantee about the objective only.
    • Neal & Hinton (1998), A view of the EM algorithm that justifies incremental, sparse, and other variants — the ELBO reading of (3)–(4), and the framework that makes MAP-EM and tempered EM look like the same algorithm with a different bound.
    • Ueda & Nakano (1998), Deterministic annealing EM algorithm — the tempered E-step of (9), with the free-energy schedule of (10) and the conditions under which it escapes local optima.
    • Blei, Ng & Jordan (2003), Latent Dirichlet allocation — the model that replaces pLSA's per-document parameters with a Dirichlet prior, which is the fix for failure modes 1, 2 and 6.
    • Lee & Seung (1999), Learning the parts of objects by non-negative matrix factorization — the multiplicative-update view of the same factorisation, and the comparison that makes pLSA's probabilistic constraints visible as normalisation rather than non-negativity.
    • Gaussier & Goutte (2005), Relation between PLSA and NMF and implications — the exact correspondence, including the equivalence of the PLSA likelihood and a specific NMF divergence up to a constant.
    • Wallach, Murray, Salakhutdinov & Mimno (2009), Evaluation methods for topic models — the systematic account of why held-out perplexity with fold-in rewards the wrong models, which is failure mode 2 measured on real corpora.
    • Arora, Ge, Halpern, Mimno, Moitra, Sontag, Wu & Zhu (2013), A practical algorithm for topic modeling with provable guarantees — identifiability of the topic-word matrix from moments, which is what makes equation (15) the only ambiguity rather than one of many.
    • Blei & Lafferty (2009), Topic models — the survey that places pLSA, LDA and their descendants in one framework, and the honest account of what each buys and costs.
