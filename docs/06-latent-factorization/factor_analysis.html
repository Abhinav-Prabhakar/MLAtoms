    Factor analysis

    Intuition #

    Probabilistic PCA says every feature is measured with the same noise. Factor analysis says each feature has its own noise level, and that is the entire difference:

    \[x = \Lambda f + \mu + \varepsilon, \qquad f \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Phi), \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Psi), \qquad (1)\]
    \[x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma), \qquad \Sigma = \Lambda \Phi \Lambda^\top + \Psi, \qquad \Psi = \mathrm{diag}(\psi_1, \ldots, \psi_d). \qquad (2)\]

    PPCA is the case \(\Phi = I\), \(\Psi = \sigma^2 I\). Dropping the second constraint is what makes the model honest about instruments: a microphone has one noise floor, a thermometer another, and a model that averages them fits neither. The loadings then have to arrange themselves around a fixed noise profile instead of absorbing it.

    The cost is one parameter per feature, which is exactly the parameter that is hardest to estimate from a small sample.

    PCA PPCA Factor analysis
    parameters in the covariance \(dk - k(k-1)/2\) \(dk + 1 - k(k-1)/2\) \(dk + d - k(k-1)/2\)
    noise none one \(\sigma^2\) one \(\psi_j\) per feature
    likelihood no yes yes
    a partial row cannot project it \(E[x_{\text{miss}} \mid x_{\text{obs}}]\) no (see below)
    a rotation worth choosing no no yes, \(k \ge 3\)

    The last row is the second half of the story. For any orthogonal \(R\),

    \[\Lambda R R^\top \Lambda^\top = \Lambda \Lambda^\top, \qquad (3)\]

    so the likelihood is completely flat along the \(k(k-1)/2\) rotation directions. With \(k = 2\) that is a single angle and the fitted basis is arbitrary; with \(k = 3\) it is three angles. The model is identified in \(\Sigma\) and not in \(\Lambda\), and a loading matrix is only as interpretable as the rotation you chose. That is why two of the five variants are not estimators at all.

    Variants #

    Variant Optimises Algorithm What it is for
    ml_fa the likelihood Jöreskog fixed point on \(\psi\) the estimate, and the likelihood
    pca_fa nothing one eigendecomposition the fast moment estimator
    minres off-diagonal residual iterated principal axes the least-squares alternative
    varimax loadings simplicity polar/SVD iterations an orthogonal, readable basis
    promax fit to a simple target Procrustes to \(\lvert \Lambda \rvert^p\) an oblique, readable basis

    ml_fa is the maximum-likelihood estimator, and it is a fixed point rather than a gradient ascent: at fixed \(\psi\) the optimal \(\Lambda\) is available in closed form from an eigendecomposition of the scaled sample covariance, and at fixed \(\Lambda\) the optimal \(\psi\) is the diagonal residual. Alternating the two exact steps gives a monotone iteration that converges in 30–40 sweeps on the demo's data. Every claim in the demo is one of its stationarity conditions.

    pca_fa is the principal-component method: take the top \(k\) eigenvectors of \(S\), scale them by the square roots of the eigenvalues, and set the uniquenesses to whatever the diagonal leaves over. It is one eigendecomposition, it never iterates, and it is not a likelihood estimator — its \(\psi\) can come out negative and has to be clipped, which is why its fitted covariance is not an optimum of anything. It exists because it is what people ran before computers, and because it makes a clean baseline: on the demo's wide-noise data it loses 0.20 nats per row and leaves an off-diagonal residual ten times larger.

    minres minimises the off-diagonal residual directly. Given \(\psi\), the best \(\Lambda\) is the top \(k\) eigenpairs of the reduced matrix \(S - \mathrm{diag}(\psi)\) — the sample covariance with its diagonal replaced by communalities — and \(\psi\) is then updated from the new diagonal residual. It is the same iteration as pca_fa with the diagonal replaced at every step, and it converges to the least-squares solution. The demo's measured result is worth stating plainly: ml_fa and minres are the same estimator in effect on this data. Their held-out likelihoods agree to \(10^{-4}\) and their residuals to 0.015. They are different objectives with the same answer, which is the asymptotic equivalence the theory predicts.

    varimax fits ml_fa and then rotates. Kaiser's criterion is the variance of the squared loadings within each column, summed over columns:

    \[V = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \mathrm{Var}_i\left( (\Lambda R)_{ij}^2 \right), \qquad (4)\]

    which is maximised exactly when the squared loadings within a column are as spread out as possible — a few large, the rest near zero. That is the formal version of "simple structure". It is a local method: it ascends a non-convex quartic, and the demo verifies local optimality (200 random rotations, none improves) rather than global optimality.

    promax relaxes orthogonality. It fits the varimax solution to its own fourth power — a Procrustes problem with a closed-form answer — and reads the result as an oblique pattern \(\Lambda = P\) together with the factor correlation \(\Phi\):

    \[\Sigma = P \Phi P^\top + \Psi, \qquad P \Phi P^\top = \Lambda_v \Lambda_v^\top. \qquad (5)\]

    The identity on the right is exact, and it is the reason an oblique rotation is a reparameterisation rather than a new model: the same \(\Sigma\), the same likelihood, a different basis. What \(\Phi\) is not is an estimate of the population factor correlation. It is a diagnostic of how far the orthogonal solution had to be pushed.

    Derivation #

    1. Why the diagonal is the whole difference #

    Both models match the off-diagonals with \(\Lambda \Lambda^\top\), so any difference between them is confined to the diagonal — and the diagonal is exactly where a covariance has to stay positive definite. Write \(h_j^2 = \sum_i \lambda_{ij}^2\) for the communality of feature \(j\), the variance it shares with the others. Then

    \[S_{jj} = h_j^2 + \psi_j, \qquad (6)\]

    so \(\psi_j\) is the part of feature \(j\)'s variance that no factor explains. In PPCA all \(d\) of those residuals are tied to one number; in factor analysis they are free. That is why factor analysis is the model you reach for when the features have visibly different noise, and why it needs \(d\) more parameters.

    2. Profiling \(\Lambda\) out: the scaled spectrum #

    Fix \(\psi\). Maximising the likelihood over \(\Lambda\) is a regression problem in disguise. Whiten the data by \(\Psi^{-1/2}\), which makes the noise the identity:

    \[\Psi^{-1/2} S \Psi^{-1/2} = G\, \Theta\, G^\top, \qquad \theta_1 \ge \theta_2 \ge \cdots \ge \theta_d. \qquad (7)\]

    The transformed model is \(\tilde S = \tilde\Lambda \tilde\Lambda^\top + I\), and the top-\(k\) eigenpairs of \(\tilde S\) are the answer:

    \[\tilde\Lambda = G_k\left(\Theta_k - I\right)^{1/2}, \qquad \Lambda = \Psi^{1/2} \tilde\Lambda. \qquad (8)\]

    This is the same statement as PPCA's closed form with \(\sigma^2 = 1\) in the whitened coordinates. The \(\theta_j - 1\) is the signal above the noise: a direction whose scaled eigenvalue is below 1 has less variance than the noise floor, so its loading is clipped to zero (the code's np.maximum(..., 0.0)).

    Substituting (8) back gives the update for the noise,

    \[\psi_j = S_{jj} - \sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_{ij}^2, \qquad (9)\]

    which is (6) rearranged. Iterating (7)–(9) is Jöreskog's algorithm, and it is what _fit_ml does.

    3. The three stationarity conditions #

    The demo does not assert (9) — it could only recompute it. It asserts the deeper conditions, each of which is a different expression:

    (i) \(\Sigma\) is optimal over \(\Lambda\) at fixed \(\psi\). This is where \(\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}S) = d\) comes from, and it makes the profile likelihood possible:

    \[\ell(\psi) = -\frac{n}{2}\left[ d\log 2\pi + \sum_j \log \psi_j + \sum_{j \le k} \log \theta_j + d \right]. \qquad (10)\]

    The determinant identity behind it is clean: \(\lvert \Sigma \rvert = \lvert \Psi \rvert \cdot \lvert I + \tilde\Lambda\tilde\Lambda^\top \rvert = \prod_j \psi_j \cdot \prod_{j\le k}\theta_j\), because the whitened loadings have eigenvalues \(\theta_j - 1\) on \(k\) directions and zero elsewhere.

    (ii) \(\psi\) is optimal given \(\Lambda\). Differentiating (10) gives

    \[\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \psi_j} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[ (\Sigma^{-1} S \Sigma^{-1})_{jj} - (\Sigma^{-1})_{jj} \right], \qquad (11)\]

    and the demo measures its max-abs at \(3.7 \times 10^{-8}\) over the eight features. That is a genuine derivative check: score_samples forms \(\Sigma^{-1}\) and never differentiates anything.

    (iii) the scaled spectrum keeps its weight. From (8), \(\mathrm{tr}(\Lambda^\top \Psi^{-1} \Lambda) = \sum_{j\le k}(\theta_j - 1)\), so

    \[\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\Psi^{-1/2} S \Psi^{-1/2}\right) = \sum_{j\le k}\theta_j + (d - k) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_{j>k}\theta_j = d - k. \qquad (12)\]

    The tail eigenvalues are near 1 but not equal to it — the demo's are 1.081, 1.040, 1.012, 0.999, 0.942, 0.926 — and yet their sum is exactly \(d - k = 6.000000\). That is a sharper check than "they are all around 1", and it is an identity, not a tolerance.

    4. The fitted covariance is the identity in units of the noise #

    Multiply (2) by \(\Psi^{-1/2}\) on both sides. With \(\Phi = I\),

    \[\Psi^{-1/2}\Sigma\Psi^{-1/2} = I + \tilde\Lambda \tilde\Lambda^\top, \qquad (13)\]

    whose eigenvalues are \(\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_k\) on the factor directions and exactly 1 on the other \(d - k\). The demo prints them: \((10.7806, 6.4830)\) then 1.000 six times. This is the geometric statement of the whole model — the factors are the only directions in which a feature's variance exceeds its noise, and everything else sits at the noise level by construction.

    5. Rotations are reparameterisations #

    Because (3) holds, the likelihood cannot prefer \(R\). So pick \(R\) by a criterion about the loadings. Varimax's is (4), and the standard algorithm is a sequence of exact orthogonal Procrustes steps. Write \(B = A R\) for the row-normalised loadings at the current rotation. The derivative of the criterion with respect to \(R\), evaluated and then projected onto the orthogonal group, says the next rotation is the orthogonal polar factor of

    \[M = B^\top\left(B^{\circ 3} - B\,\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\overline{B^2}\right)\right), \qquad M = U \Sigma V^\top \;\Longrightarrow\; R \leftarrow R\,(UV^\top). \qquad (14)\]

    \(B^{\circ 3}\) is the elementwise cube and \(\overline{B^2}\) the column means of the squared loadings. Each step increases the criterion; because the criterion is bounded above on a compact group, it converges. It can converge to a local maximum, which is why the demo checks local and not global optimality.

    The row normalisation matters for a reason that is easy to miss. Without it, a feature with communality 0.9 dominates the variance being maximised and the rotation is decided by the noisiest variables. Dividing each row by its communality turns the criterion into a statement about shares, and since an orthogonal rotation preserves row norms, the communalities are unchanged when the rotation is applied to the unnormalised matrix — the demo asserts that to \(10^{-10}\).

    Promax starts from the varimax solution \(\Lambda_v\), chooses a target \(T = \lvert \Lambda_v \rvert^p\) with \(p = 4\), and solves the least-squares problem \(\Lambda_v A \approx T\) with the constraint \(\mathrm{diag}(A) = 1\). With \(A\) in hand,

    \[P = \Lambda_v A^{-\top}, \qquad \Phi = A^\top A, \qquad P\Phi P^\top = \Lambda_v A^{-\top} A^\top A A^{-1} \Lambda_v^\top = \Lambda_v\Lambda_v^\top. \qquad (15)\]

    \(A\) has a unit diagonal by construction but \(\Phi = A^\top A\) does not, so a final rescaling makes \(\Phi\) a correlation matrix:

    \[P \leftarrow P\,\mathrm{diag}(s), \qquad \Phi \leftarrow \mathrm{diag}(1/s)\,\Phi\,\mathrm{diag}(1/s), \qquad s_j = \sqrt{\Phi_{jj}}, \qquad (16)\]

    and the two rescalings cancel inside \(P\Phi P^\top\). That cancellation is exactly what a wrong transpose in (15) destroys: the first version of this file computed \(P = \Lambda A^{-1}\), and the identity failed at the third decimal while everything else looked fine.

    6. Why a pattern loading can exceed 1 #

    In the orthogonal model a loading is a correlation-like quantity: \(\lambda_{ij}^2 \le h_j^2 \le S_{jj}\) after standardisation. In the oblique model the pattern entry \(p_{ij}\) is a regression weight — the effect of factor \(i\) on variable \(j\) holding the other factors fixed. Collinearity among the factors inflates regression weights, so \(p_{ij}\) can exceed 1 while the communality stays below \(S_{jj}\). The demo's promax pattern reaches 1.05 where the varimax solution stopped at 0.93, and that is not a bug; it is the difference between a coefficient and a correlation. The structural loading is \((P\Phi)_{ij}\), and that is bounded.

    7. The Heywood boundary #

    Maximise (10) and nothing stops \(\psi_j\) from reaching 0. At \(\psi_j = 0\) the log-likelihood has a \(\log 0\) in it, so the boundary is not a finite optimum — the likelihood is unbounded as \(\psi_j \to 0\) whenever the data can be fitted exactly at that feature. That happens when one feature is (nearly) a linear combination of the others, or when \(k\) is too large for the sample. The estimate is then said to be a Heywood case: a feature with no unique variance, an improper solution, and \(d\)-dimensional standard errors that do not exist.

    The code treats it as a boundary event rather than a numerical accident:

    • the iteration stops as soon as a \(\psi_j\) falls below \(10^{-6} S_{jj}\),
    • the offending entries are set to that floor so the arithmetic stays finite,
    • and heywood_ is set so a caller can find out.

    The floor is relative to each feature's own variance, which makes the test scale-free: a feature measured in millimetres and one in metres are compared on the same footing.

    Code walkthrough #

    FactorAnalysis(n_components, variant=..., max_iter=..., tol=..., power=...) with fit, transform (\(E[f \mid x]\)), fit_transform, reconstruct, score_samples, and the attributes loadings_ \((d,k)\), noise_variance_ (\(\psi\)), uniquenesses_ (the same array under its factor-analysis name), communalities_, covariance_, rotation_, phi_, heywood_, loglik_, loglik_curve_, n_iter_.

    The orientation is (d, k) — feature by factor — because a loading is a regression coefficient of a feature on a factor, and pca.PCA.components_ uses the transpose of that. Stating which one is meant is not pedantry: every transpose bug in this file was an orientation bug.

    1. The fixed point, in seven lines #

            for it in range(1, self.max_iter + 1):
            # Ψ^{-1/2} S Ψ^{-1/2}: the likelihood in coordinates where the
            # uniquenesses are 1, which is where the closed form lives
            C = S / np.sqrt(np.outer(psi, psi))
            theta, G = _eigh_desc(C)
            keep = np.maximum(theta[:k] - 1.0, 0.0)     # the signal above the noise
            L = np.sqrt(psi)[:, None] * (G[:, :k] * np.sqrt(keep)[None, :])
            psi_new = diag - np.sum(L * L, axis=1)

    That is (7), (8), (9) in order. np.outer(psi, psi) broadcast through a square root is the whitening matrix without forming it: dividing entry \((j,l)\) by \(\sqrt{\psi_j\psi_l}\) is exactly \(\Psi^{-1/2} S \Psi^{-1/2}\). _eigh_desc sorts descending because np.linalg.eigh does not, and theta[:k] - 1.0 is the whitened signal. The np.maximum is the clip that makes a factor with less variance than the noise contribute nothing rather than a nan.

    Note what is not built: no \(d \times d\) inverse of \(\Sigma\), no gradient, no step size, no line search. Each sweep is one symmetric eigendecomposition and two matrix products.

    2. The profile likelihood, which never inverts \(\Sigma\) #

            d = S.shape[0]                                # S is a covariance: (d, d)
        psi = np.maximum(psi, 1e-12)
        theta, _ = _eigh_desc(S / np.sqrt(np.outer(psi, psi)))
        keep = np.maximum(theta[:self.n_components], 1.0)
        return float(-0.5 * (d * math.log(2.0 * math.pi) + np.sum(np.log(psi))
                             + np.sum(np.log(keep)) + d))

    (10), per row of data. keep = max(theta, 1) rather than theta - 1 because this is \(\log \lvert \Sigma \rvert\), where a clipped factor contributes \(\log 1 = 0\): the model does not claim that direction. score_samples computes the same quantity by inverting \(\Sigma\) and forming a quadratic form. The two agree to \(3 \times 10^{-10}\) relative on the demo's fit, which is the point of having both: a wrong constant in either one shows up immediately.

    There is a trap here worth spelling out, because the first version of this file fell into it. (10) is valid only at the fixed point, since it uses \(\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}S) = d\) to replace a quadratic form it never computes. Evaluating it at a perturbed \(\psi\) therefore returns a number that is not the likelihood of anything, and a "perturb and check it does not improve" test built on it is meaningless. The demo tests stationarity with (11) instead.

    3. The two moment estimators #

            lam, V = _eigh_desc(S)
        L = V[:, :k] * np.sqrt(np.maximum(lam[:k], 0.0))
        self.loadings_ = L
        self.noise_variance_ = S.diagonal() - np.sum(L * L, axis=1)

    This is pca_fa: the principal-component method, which is PPCA's closed form with \(\sigma^2 = 0\). It is one line of real work, and its \(\psi\) is whatever the diagonal has left over — possibly negative when \(k\) is large relative to \(d\) or the data has redundant features, which is why _finish exists.

                lam, V = _eigh_desc(S - np.diag(psi))
            L = V[:, :k] * np.sqrt(np.maximum(lam[:k], 0.0))
            psi_new = diag - np.sum(L * L, axis=1)

    This is minres: the same two lines with \(S\) replaced by the reduced matrix \(S - \mathrm{diag}(\psi)\). Given \(\psi\), that is the exact least-squares solution for \(\Lambda\) against the off-diagonals — the diagonal of the reduced matrix holds the communalities, which is precisely the part of \(S\) that \(\Lambda\Lambda^\top\) is supposed to reproduce on the diagonal. Substituting the diagonal back in and iterating is principal-axis factoring, whose fixed point is the MINRES solution.

    The difference between the two is one subtraction, and it is the difference between a consistent estimator and a biased one: pca_fa never updates \(\psi\), so its loadings are computed as if the noise were zero on the diagonal, and the bias grows with the noise-to-signal ratio. On the demo's 40× noise spread that is 0.20 nats per row.

    4. The model, assembled from its parts #

            return self.loadings_ @ self._phi() @ self.loadings_.T + np.diag(self.noise_variance_)

    (2) with \(\Phi = I\) for the orthogonal variants and the fitted \(\Phi\) for promax. np.diag rather than a diagonal matrix stored as an attribute keeps the memory at \(O(d^2 + d)\) and makes the structure of the model visible in one line: low rank plus diagonal.

    Everything downstream — score_samples, covariance_, the residual checks — goes through this expression, so a wrong orientation in loadings_ cannot hide. It is also why promax's identity (15) is checkable: both variants must return the same matrix to \(10^{-10}\), and they do.

    5. Factor scores are a ridge regression #

            L, psi = self.loadings_, self.noise_variance_
        # E[f|x] = (I + Λᵀ Ψ⁻¹ Λ)⁻¹ Λᵀ Ψ⁻¹ (x − μ)   -- Thomson's scores
        A = np.eye(L.shape[1]) + (L.T / psi) @ L
        return np.linalg.solve(A, ((X - self.mean_) / psi) @ L).T

    The posterior of \(f\) given \(x\) under (1). Dividing by psi is \(\Psi^{-1}\) on both sides, and np.linalg.solve keeps the \(k \times k\) system on the left instead of forming its inverse. This is Thomson's score; the alternative, Bartlett's, is the weighted least-squares estimate that ignores the prior on \(f\) and is unbiased but has a larger variance. Thomson's is the conditional mean, which is the one that belongs in a probabilistic model.

    Note that the scores are well defined even when \(\Psi\) has a tiny entry. The ridge term \(I\) is the prior on \(f\), so the system is always positive definite; what is not well defined is the interpretation of a score on a factor that a nearly noise-free feature determines exactly.

    6. Varimax, and why the rotation accumulates #

            B = A @ R
        crit = float(np.sum(np.var(B * B, axis=0)))
        if crit - best <= tol * max(abs(crit), 1.0):
            break
        best = crit
        M = B.T @ (B ** 3 - B * (B * B).mean(axis=0))
        u, _, vt = np.linalg.svd(M)
        R = R @ (u @ vt)                     # accumulate: B was already rotated

    (14). B ** 3 is the elementwise cube, (B * B).mean(axis=0) is the per-column mean of the squared loadings, and the SVD of the resulting \(k \times k\) matrix gives its orthogonal polar factor \(UV^\top\).

    The accumulation is the subtle line. \(B\) already contains \(R\), so the step from \(B\) is a further rotation; applying it to \(R\) rather than replacing \(R\) is what makes the iteration converge instead of oscillate. Returning R as well as the rotated loadings costs nothing and makes the whole thing reproducible: the demo uses rotation_ to check the criterion under arbitrary extra rotations, and a later fit can be aligned to an earlier one with it.

    7. Promax in nine lines #

        target = np.abs(L) ** power
    A = np.linalg.solve(L.T @ L, L.T @ target)          # least squares, L A ≈ target
    A = A @ np.diag(1.0 / np.diag(A))                   # unit diagonal, by column
    P = np.linalg.solve(A, L.T).T                       # P = L A^{-T}
    phi = A.T @ A
    # rescale so that Φ is a correlation matrix and P Φ Pᵀ is untouched:
    # P -> P diag(s) and Φ -> diag(1/s) Φ diag(1/s) cancel exactly
    s = np.sqrt(np.diag(phi))
    return P * s[None, :], phi / np.outer(s, s)

    Line 1 is the target, line 2 the unconstrained least-squares solution of \(\Lambda A \approx T\), line 3 the column rescaling that gives \(A\) a unit diagonal — last because rescaling columns of \(A\) is what moves the diagonal. Line 4 is (15) for the pattern, and at this point \(\Phi = A^\top A\) is a covariance rather than a correlation. Lines 6–9 are (16): rescale so the diagonal is 1, in the only way that leaves \(P\Phi P^\top\) alone.

    The transpose in np.linalg.solve(A, L.T).T is the whole reason this file had a bug. P = L A^{-\top} wants to solve with \(A\); writing np.linalg.solve(A.T, L.T).T computes \(L A^{-1}\), which is a different matrix whenever \(A\) is not symmetric, and the identity in (15) then fails at the third decimal while the loadings still look plausible. The demo's assertion on \(P\Phi P^\top\) is what catches it.

    8. Which variants rotate, and in what order #

            if self.variant == "varimax":
            self.loadings_, self.rotation_ = _varimax(self.loadings_)
            self.phi_ = None
        else:
            varimax_L, rot = _varimax(self.loadings_)
            self.loadings_, self.phi_ = _promax(varimax_L, self.power)
            self.rotation_ = rot

    varimax and promax fit by maximum likelihood and then rotate, so variant="varimax" is not a different estimator: its covariance_ and score_samples are identical to ml_fa's to machine precision. That is the design, and the demo asserts it. Only loadings_ changes, by an orthogonal map that leaves \(\Sigma\) alone.

    Promax rotates twice — varimax first, then the oblique Procrustes — which is why rotation_ records the orthogonal part. The oblique map is not a rotation of the loadings in the same sense; it is the pair \((P, \Phi)\).

    9. The clipping, and the flag #

            floor = 1e-6 * S.diagonal()             # per feature, so the test is scale-free
        self.heywood_ = self.heywood_ or bool(np.any(self.noise_variance_ <= floor))
        self.noise_variance_ = np.maximum(self.noise_variance_, floor)

    Two lines: detect, then clip. The detection is against each feature's own variance rather than a global threshold, so the test means the same thing after a unit change. or rather than assignment because _finish is called after _fit_ml has already had a chance to set the flag on the boundary.

    Clipping is the smaller half of the job. The fitted \(\Sigma\) is then positive definite by construction and score_samples returns a finite number — which is exactly the danger: a Heywood solution looks like a good fit and has a slightly better likelihood than the honest one. heywood_ is the only warning.

    10. Two refusals that keep the API honest #

            if self.n_components > d - 1:
            k = self.n_components
            n_par = d * k + d - k * (k - 1) // 2     # loadings + uniquenesses − rotations

    At \(k = d\) the parameter count is \(d^2 + d - d(d-1)/2 = (d^2 + 3d)/2\) against \(d(d+1)/2\) entries in \(\Sigma\): exactly \(d\) more parameters than the covariance has numbers, so \(\psi\) is not identified and no amount of data fixes it. Refusing with a pointer to probabilistic_pca — which does have a \(d\)-component model, with \(\sigma^2 = 0\) — is more useful than returning something.

            if np.isnan(X).any():
            raise ValueError("factor analysis needs a complete matrix; "
                             "use probabilistic_pca with variant='missing_data'")

    Missing data is not a missing feature here: the M-step needs \(\psi\) per feature and the E-step needs a complete row. Silently dropping rows changes the sample without saying so; silently imputing biases \(\psi\) towards zero and manufactures Heywood cases. The sibling atom has the correct machinery, so the error message names it.

    Complexity #

    \(n\) rows, \(d\) features, \(k\) factors, \(T\) sweeps.

    Operation Time Memory Note
    ml_fa \(O(nd^2 + Td^3)\) \(O(d^2)\) one covariance, then \(T\) eigendecompositions
    pca_fa \(O(nd^2 + d^3)\) \(O(d^2)\) one eigendecomposition, no iteration
    minres \(O(nd^2 + Td^3)\) \(O(d^2)\) same cost as ml_fa
    _varimax \(O(T_r d k^2 + T_r k^3)\) \(O(dk)\) SVD of a \(k \times k\) matrix per step
    _promax \(O(dk^2 + k^3)\) \(O(dk)\) one Procrustes solve, no iteration
    transform \(O(mdk + k^3)\) \(O(mk)\) Thomson's scores for \(m\) rows
    score_samples \(O(d^3 + md^2)\) \(O(d^2)\) inverts \(\Sigma\) once

    Two things follow. First, factor analysis is linear in \(n\) only through the covariance: everything after that is \(O(d^3)\) per sweep, so the method's cost is set by \(d\), not by the number of rows — the opposite of what a gradient method would give, and the reason it is feasible on \(d = 10^3\) response matrices but not on \(d = 10^5\). Second, the rotation costs are negligible compared with the fitting, which is why varimax and promax are offered as variants rather than as a separate post-processing atom.

    The demo fits \(d = 8\) in 35 sweeps and the whole six-section script runs in 0.13 s.

    Failure modes #

    1. ML and MINRES are not distinguishable at this sample size. The demo's numbers, on a 40× noise spread: held-out likelihood \(-11.8504\) for ml_fa against \(-11.8503\) for minres, off-diagonal residual 0.142 against 0.127. The earlier version of this demo asserted ml > minres on a narrower noise profile and it is false at the third digit — the two criteria are asymptotically equivalent, and reporting a winner from a \(10^{-4}\) gap is reporting noise. What is distinguishable is pca_fa: 0.20 nats per row and a residual ten times larger. Comparisons between the two proper estimators need either much more data or a criterion-specific argument.

    2. Varimax cannot undo a correlated \(\Phi\). On the demo's oblique truth the recovered simple structure is off by 0.24 at the largest entry — better than the unrotated 0.61 share of mid-range loadings, but nowhere near exact. An orthogonal rotation is a rotation within the same \(k\)-space, and a simple structure generated by correlated factors is simply not reachable by one. If the factors are really correlated, the honest options are promax, a targeted rotation, or a confirmatory model; treating varimax output as the truth systematically overstates the distinctness of the factors.

    3. Promax's \(\Phi\) is a rotation artefact. It is real as a reparameterisation — the demo verifies \(P \Phi P^\top = \Lambda_v \Lambda_v^\top\) to \(10^{-10}\) — and it is not an estimate of the population factor correlation. On the demo's truth \((\lvert \phi_{12} \rvert, \lvert \phi_{13} \rvert, \lvert \phi_{23} \rvert) = (0.40, 0.20, 0.00)\), promax returns \((0.40, 0.23, 0.07)\) sorted: right in magnitude, wrong in correspondence, and with a small correlation invented where the truth has none. The magnitudes come back because the Procrustes target is built from the data; the correspondence does not, because which factor is which is decided by the rotation. Report \(\Phi\) as "how oblique the simple structure is", never as "the correlation between factor 1 and factor 2".

    4. The rotation is local, and the basis is arbitrary. Varimax ascends a non-convex quartic. The demo checks local optimality — 200 random rotations of the fitted basis, none improves the criterion — which is the strongest statement available without an exhaustive search. Different starting rotations, different implementations and different software can land on different local optima, and factor order and sign are arbitrary in every one of them: two fits of the same model are only comparable after a Procrustes match. The _match helper in the demo exists for exactly this, and the atom exposes rotation_ so a fit can be reproduced exactly rather than approximately.

    5. Heywood cases are not errors, and that is the problem. The demo builds one deliberately: 11 features, one of which is an exact linear combination of two others, fitted with \(k = 4\). One of 12 uniquenesses is driven to the floor (\(3.0 \times 10^{-6}\)) and heywood_ is set. No exception is raised, no convergence warning fires, the likelihood is finite, and the model is not a model. The tell is the flag; the sanity check is that \(\psi_j\) should be positive and not absurdly small relative to \(S_{jj}\). In published work the Heywood case is often a sign that \(k\) is too large, that two variables are collinear, or that the sample is too small for the parameter count.

    6. \(k\) is bounded by the data, not by ambition. The code refuses \(k \ge d\), but the real bound is Ledermann's: the parameter count must not exceed the number of distinct covariance entries,

    \[d k + d - \frac{k(k-1)}{2} \le \frac{d(d+1)}{2} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad (d-k)^2 \ge d + k, \qquad (17)\]

    which for \(d = 12\) allows at most \(k = 7\), and for \(d = 8\) at most \(k = 4\). Beyond that the model is not identified however well the iteration converges — a \(d = 8\), \(k = 5\) fit will return eight numbers for \(\psi\) and they will all be meaningless. The code's guard only refuses \(k \ge d\), so the counting bound is not enforced; it is stated here so that a fit inside the guard but outside (17) is not mistaken for an estimate.

    7. It needs complete data. The E-step is a conditional mean over a complete row and the M-step updates \(\psi\) per feature; there is no observed-block version of either. probabilistic_pca's missing_data variant has the machinery, at the cost of the per-feature noise. Silently mean-imputing instead biases \(\psi\) towards zero — imputation creates a feature with no unique variance — and manufactures Heywood cases out of missingness.

    8. Scale changes the answer. Loadings, uniquenesses and communalities are in the units of the data. A feature in metres and the same feature in millimetres produce the same \(\Sigma\) up to the change of basis and visibly different loadings, and the varimax criterion, which averages over features, is sensitive to that. Standardise before fitting unless the units are meaningful, and if they are meaningful, say so — the whole reason to prefer factor analysis to PPCA is that the per-feature noise is interpretable.

    Applications #

    • Psychometrics and educational testing. The origin of the method: a battery of test items, a handful of latent abilities, and loadings you want to read. Varimax is the default rotation in that literature precisely because the goal is a table a human can interpret.
    • Finance. Barra-style risk models are factor models of returns with correlated style factors; the oblique parameterisation, not the orthogonal one, is the one that matches how exposures actually co-move.
    • Genomics. Expression modules: genes that load on the same factor are co-regulated, and the uniqueness is the measurement noise of that probe. The Heywood case is common with near-duplicate probes, and flagging it is part of standard QC.
    • Sensor fusion and metrology. Per-instrument noise is the model's stated purpose. If you know the noise levels from calibration, fixing \(\psi\) and solving for \(\Lambda\) is a smaller, better-conditioned problem, and the likelihood in (10) gives a test of whether the calibrated values are consistent with the data.
    • Structural equation modelling. Factor analysis is the measurement model half of SEM; the identification condition \(k \le d - 1\), the rotation problem and the Heywood case all reappear there with different names.
    • Dimension reduction before regression. Factor scores are a lower-variance summary than principal components when the features have unequal noise, which is why they are used as predictors in econometrics and chemometrics.
    • A testbed for identifiability. The rotation non-identifiability is the cleanest example in statistics of a likelihood that is flat along a manifold of parameters — the same phenomenon as label switching in mixtures and sign flips in matrix factorisation, in its most tractable form.

    Where it is a trap: reading an unrotated ML solution as interpretable (it is not — the basis is whatever the eigendecomposition returned); reading a rotated solution as a hypothesis test (it is a description, chosen after seeing the data); and reading \(\psi\) as measurement noise when it is really everything the factors do not explain, including model misspecification.
