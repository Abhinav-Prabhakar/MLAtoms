    PCA

    Intuition #

    PCA answers one question: which direction, if you had to throw everything else away, loses the least? Centred data \(X_c \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\), a unit vector \(v\), and the variance of the projection \(X_c v\) is \(v^\top \Sigma v\) with \(\Sigma = X_c^\top X_c/(n-1)\). Maximise it, then repeat in the orthogonal complement:

    \[\max_{V^\top V = I_k}\ \mathrm{tr}(V^\top \Sigma V), \qquad (1)\]

    whose solution is the top \(k\) eigenvectors of \(\Sigma\) — the right singular vectors of \(X_c\). That is the whole algorithm. Everything else in this file is a different way to compute the same \(V\), or a deliberate change to the objective.

    The reason it is worth seven variants is that "the same \(V\)" can be very different in cost and in what it can express:

      full_svd          X_c ──SVD──► U S Vᵀ        O(nd²), exact
  randomized        X_c ──sketch──► Q ──► SVD   O(ndk), exact whp
  kernel_pca        Φ(X) ──Gram──► K_c ──► eig  O(n²d), nonlinear
  incremental       [X; B] ──update──► (V, S)   O(bd²) per block
  robust_pca        X = L + S, minimise a mix   O(nd²) per iteration
  sparse_pca        add λ‖v‖₁, lose orthogonality
  whitening         V, then divide by √λ

    The first four compute the same subspace and the demo asserts that they agree to \(10^{-10}\). The last three change the answer on purpose, and the demo asserts what each change buys: robust PCA survives corruption that moves plain PCA by 26%, sparse PCA puts 69% of its loadings at exactly zero while keeping 99% of the variance, and whitening makes the covariance exactly \(I\).

    Eckart–Young is what makes truncation meaningful. Among all rank-\(k\) matrices, \(X_c V_k V_k^\top\) is the closest to \(X_c\) in Frobenius norm, and the error is the sum of the eigenvalues you discarded:

    \[\min_{\mathrm{rank}(M) \le k} \|X_c - M\|_F^2 = \sum_{j>k} \lambda_j, \qquad (2)\]

    with \(\lambda_j\) the eigenvalues of \(\Sigma\). This is not an approximation statement — it is an equality, and the demo checks it to \(10^{-8}\). It is also the only honest way to choose \(k\) without a validation set: look at the tail of the spectrum, not at a round number.

    Variants #

    Variant Computes Cost Exact? When
    full_svd all \(V\), truncate \(O(nd\min(n,d))\) yes the default; \(n, d \lesssim 5000\)
    randomized top \(k\) from a sketch \(O(ndk)\) whp, with power iterations \(d\) large, \(k \ll d\)
    kernel_pca top \(k\) of \(\Phi(X)\) \(O(n^2d + n^3)\) yes, in feature space the manifold is nonlinear
    incremental \(V\) updated per block \(O(bd^2)\) per block yes, if the mean is fixed data arrives in batches
    robust_pca \(L\) in \(X = L + S\) \(O(nd^2)\) per iteration convex, not exact corrupted cells, outliers
    sparse_pca \(V\) with zeros \(O(Tnd)\) no, non-convex interpretable loadings
    whitening \(V \Lambda^{-1/2}\) same as full_svd yes downstream models want \(I\) covariance

    full_svd is the definition: centre, call np.linalg.svd, keep the top rows of \(V^\top\). np.linalg.svd on an \(n \times d\) matrix costs \(O(nd\min(n,d))\) and is the right choice until the matrix does not fit in memory. The sign of each component is arbitrary; _flip fixes it so that the largest-magnitude loading is positive, which is what makes two runs comparable.

    randomized exploits the fact that you do not need the whole spectrum, only the top \(k\). Draw a Gaussian sketch \(\Omega \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times (k+p)}\), form \(Y = (X_c X_c^\top)^q X_c \Omega\), orthonormalise \(Y\) to get \(Q\), and SVD the small matrix \(Q^\top X_c\). With \(q = 0\) the sketch sees the spectrum weighted by the Gaussian, and the error is governed by \(\sigma_{k+1}/\sigma_1\); each power iteration replaces that ratio by its \((2q+1)\)-th power. On a rank-3 signal buried in full-rank noise, with a sketch of width 8, \(q=0\) leaves 8% error in the singular values, \(q=2\) leaves \(10^{-7}\), and \(q=4\) leaves \(4\times10^{-13}\). The cost is \(O(ndk)\) plus \(q\) passes over the data, which is the whole point when \(d = 10^6\) and \(k = 50\).

    kernel_pca replaces \(x\) with \(\Phi(x)\) and never forms \(\Phi\). The covariance in feature space is an \(n \times n\) Gram matrix, and its top eigenvectors give the components through the kernel trick:

    \[K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j) = \exp(-\gamma\|x_i - x_j\|^2), \qquad K_c = HKH, \qquad H = I - \tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top. \qquad (3)\]

    The centring is not optional: without \(H\), the first eigenvector of \(K\) is approximately the all-ones vector, which measures nothing but the mean of the feature map. The demo's two moons are the standard illustration — a linear rotation cannot separate them, so linear PCA cannot either, but one threshold on the first kernel component reaches a Fisher ratio of 9.9 against linear PCA's 1.55.

    incremental keeps \((V, S)\) and folds in a block \(B\) of new rows without touching the old ones. The identity is

    \[[X; B] = \begin{bmatrix} U & 0 \\ 0 & I \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} S & 0 \\ P & R_q^\top \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} V & 0 \\ 0 & Q \end{bmatrix}^\top, \qquad (4)\]

    with \(P = BV\), \(R = B - PV^\top\), and \(R^\top = QR_q\). The middle matrix is \((k+b) \times (k+l)\), so the SVD that matters is small; the update is exact up to the truncation you choose. The demo recovers the full SVD of 400 rows from eight 50-row blocks to \(1.6\times10^{-15}\) — provided the mean is fixed. Left to itself the variant freezes the first block's mean, and that alone moves the subspace by \(3\times10^{-4}\) rad, which the demo also asserts.

    robust_pca changes the objective because squared error is not robust: one cell of magnitude 10 will rotate a component to accommodate it. The decomposition \(X = L + S\) with \(L\) low-rank and \(S\) sparse is convex:

    \[\min_{L, S}\ \|L\|_* + \lambda\|S\|_1 \quad \text{subject to}\quad X = L + S, \qquad \lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\max(n,d)}}. \qquad (5)\]

    The nuclear norm is the convex envelope of rank, the L1 norm is the convex envelope of sparsity, and the constraint forces the two to add up to the data. The demo corrupts 2% of the cells with \(\pm10\), and the decomposition recovers \(L\) to \(1.1\times10^{-2}\) where rank-5 PCA misses by \(0.26\) — an order of magnitude. That is the variant to reach for when "outlier" means a bad sensor reading rather than a heavy tail.

    sparse_pca relaxes orthogonality to get zeros. The objective becomes

    \[\max_{v}\ v^\top \Sigma v - \lambda\|v\|_1 \quad \text{subject to}\quad \|v\|_2 = 1, \qquad (6)\]

    which is non-convex but has a simple algorithm: power iteration where each step soft-thresholds the gradient. Components are extracted one at a time with deflation. The price is that they are no longer orthogonal and the explained variance is no longer additive across components; the demo's "99% of dense variance" is a statement about the sum of the deflated singular values, not about a partition.

    whitening is a post-processing, not a new estimator: after the projection, divide each score by its standard deviation. The transformed covariance is the identity, the transform is invertible on the \(k\)-subspace, and any downstream model that assumes isotropic features (k-means, Gaussian mixtures, linear regression with a spherical prior) now gets it. The cost is noise amplification: the smallest eigenvalue's square root is in the denominator.

    Derivation #

    The eigenproblem, and why SVD is the same thing #

    Lagrange multipliers on (1) with the constraint \(V^\top V = I\):

    \[\Sigma V = V\Lambda, \qquad V^\top \Sigma V = \Lambda. \qquad (7)\]

    So the columns of \(V\) are eigenvectors and the variances are the eigenvalues. For centred data, \(\Sigma = X_c^\top X_c/(n-1)\) and the SVD \(X_c = USV^\top\) gives

    \[X_c^\top X_c = VS^2V^\top, \qquad \lambda_j = \frac{s_j^2}{n-1}. \qquad (8)\]

    The code computes \(V\) from the SVD, never forms \(\Sigma\), and never eigendecomposes a \(d \times d\) matrix. That matters: forming \(\Sigma\) squares the condition number, and for \(d > n\) it is a \(d \times d\) matrix of rank at most \(n\).

    The scores are \(Z = X_c V_k = U_k S_k\), and reconstruction is \(\hat X = ZV_k^\top + \mu\). The residual \(X_c - ZV_k^\top\) has squared norm \(\sum_{j>k} s_j^2\), which divided by \(n-1\) is exactly the discarded variance in (2). The demo checks the two sides separately: the residual against the discarded eigenvalues, and orthogonality of the residual to \(V\), which is what makes it a projection rather than merely a low-rank approximation.

    Randomized range finding #

    Write the SVD as \(X_c = U\Sigma V^\top\) and sketch with a Gaussian \(\Omega \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times \ell}\), \(\ell = k + p\). The claim is that \(\mathrm{range}(X_c\Omega)\) captures the top \(\ell\) left singular vectors with high probability, because a random vector has a nonzero component along every direction. The quality is governed by how much of the spectrum is left outside:

    \[\|X_c - QQ^\top X_c\|_2 \lesssim \sigma_{k+1} + \text{(a term } \propto \sigma_1 \sqrt{k+p}\,/\,\text{gap}\text{)}. \qquad (9)\]

    Power iterations change the underlying matrix from \(X_c\) to \((X_cX_c^\top)^qX_c\), whose singular values are \(s_j^{2q+1}\): the same ordering, with the top amplified relative to the rest. Two iterations are usually enough because they square the ratio three times. The demo's assertion is deliberately sharp — \(4\times10^{-16}\) in the singular values, zero angle in the subspace on a rank-3 signal — and it fails at \(q=0\) as soon as the data has a noise tail (8% error on the same construction), which is the point of the variant.

    Kernel PCA, in four lines of algebra #

    In feature space the covariance is \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \phi(x_i)\phi(x_i)^\top\) after centring, and its eigenvector \(w\) lies in the span of the data: \(w = \sum_i \alpha_i \phi(x_i)\). Substituting into the eigenproblem and multiplying by \(\phi(x_j)^\top\) turns it into

    \[K_c\alpha = n\lambda\alpha, \qquad (10)\]

    an \(n \times n\) eigenproblem whose matrix is the centred Gram matrix. The projection of a new point is the kernel between it and the training set, times \(\alpha\):

    \[z(x) = \sum_i \alpha_i k(x, x_i) = K_c(x, X)\,\alpha. \qquad (11)\]

    The code stores \(\alpha\) (scaled by \(\sqrt{\lambda}\)) as components_, which is why the shape is \((k, n_{\text{fit}})\) rather than \((k, d)\): there is no vector in input space to return. Centring a test block needs the training means, not its own:

    \[K_c^{\text{test}} = K^{\text{test}} - \mathbf{1}\bar K_{\text{row}}^\top - \bar K_{\text{col}}\mathbf{1}^\top + \bar K_{\text{grand}}, \qquad (12)\]

    with the three means computed on the training Gram matrix. Centring the test block by its own mean is the classic kernel-PCA bug: the scores of training and test points land in different coordinate systems and nothing downstream works.

    Incremental SVD #

    Equation (4) is a change of basis, not an approximation. Starting from \(X = USV^\top\), the new rows are split into the part the old basis already spans (\(P = BV\)) and the residual \(R\). Factor the residual as \(R = R_q^\top Q^\top\) and the augmented matrix is a product of three matrices, the middle one small. Truncating the small SVD to \(k\) is the only approximation, and it is the same truncation a full refactorisation would make. The demo's exactness claim is therefore conditional on keeping the full rank of the first block: throw away directions on block one and no later block can bring them back.

    Robust PCA: the IALM updates #

    The augmented Lagrangian for (5) is

    \[\mathcal{L} = \|L\|_* + \lambda\|S\|_1 + \langle Y, X - L - S\rangle + \frac{\mu}{2}\|X - L - S\|_F^2. \qquad (13)\]

    Minimising over \(L\) and \(S\) in turn gives two proximal operators, each with a closed form:

    \[L \leftarrow \mathrm{SVT}_{1/\mu}\!\left(X - S + Y/\mu\right), \qquad S \leftarrow \mathrm{soft}_{\lambda/\mu}\!\left(X - L + Y/\mu\right), \qquad (14)\]

    and the multiplier update \(Y \leftarrow Y + \mu(X - L - S)\) with \(\mu\) increased geometrically. \(\mathrm{SVT}\) is singular-value soft thresholding: SVD, subtract \(\tau\) from each singular value, clip at zero, reassemble. The threshold is what sets the rank, and the L1 threshold is what sets the sparsity, so the two matrices negotiate over which part of each cell is signal.

    The choice \(\lambda = 1/\sqrt{\max(n,d)}\) comes from the recovery guarantee: it balances the nuclear-norm penalty (which grows like \(\sqrt{n}\)) against the L1 penalty (which grows like \(\sqrt{\text{number of corrupted cells}}\)). It is not a tuning knob, and changing it trades rank against sparsity in a way that is usually wrong.

    Sparse PCA's iteration #

    For fixed \(\|v\|=1\), the objective (6) is the Lagrangian of a lasso problem in \(v\), and the standard power-method-with-thresholding iterates

    \[v \leftarrow \frac{\mathrm{soft}(X_c^\top X_c v,\ \lambda\,\|X_c^\top X_c v\|_\infty)} {\|\cdot\|} \qquad (15)\]

    until the direction stops moving. The threshold is relative to the largest entry of the gradient, so lam=0.1 means "zero anything below 10% of the strongest loading", which is scale-free. After convergence the component is deflated, \(X_c \leftarrow X_c - (X_c v)v^\top\), so the next one explains what is left. Deflation is what keeps the components from collapsing onto the same direction once orthogonality is gone.

    Code walkthrough #

    PCA(n_components, variant=..., seed=..., gamma=..., lam=..., ...) is the whole API: fit, transform, inverse_transform, and partial_fit for the streaming variant. The variants share the bookkeeping — mean, explained variance, ratios — and differ only in how components_ is produced.

    1. Centre, SVD, truncate #

    def _reduce(X: np.ndarray, k: int) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Centre, SVD, truncate; return `(mean, components, singular_values)`."""
    mean = X.mean(axis=0)
    _, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X - mean, full_matrices=False)
    k = min(k, s.size)
    return mean, _flip(Vt[:k]), s[:k]

    Three details are load-bearing. full_matrices=False is the economy SVD: with True, \(U\) is \(n \times n\) and a \(10^6\)-row matrix allocates \(10^{12}\) floats. k = min(k, s.size) is what makes n_components > min(n, d) legal rather than a broadcast error. And _flip is determinism: LAPACK is free to return either sign for a singular vector, so without it two runs can disagree on every entry of a component while representing the same subspace.

    def _flip(V: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Sign-flip rows so the largest-magnitude entry of each is positive."""
    idx = np.argmax(np.abs(V), axis=1)
    signs = np.sign(V[np.arange(V.shape[0]), idx])
    signs[signs == 0.0] = 1.0
    return V * signs[:, None]

    The signs == 0 guard is not paranoia: a component whose largest entry is exactly zero gets sign 0 and the whole row is annihilated. That happens on one-hot data, which is where PCA is used most.

    2. The incremental update is a small SVD #

                V, S = self._V, self._S                 # d×r, r
            kc = V.shape[1]
            P = Xc @ V                              # b×r: what the old basis sees
            R = Xc - P @ V.T                        # b×d: what it does not
            Q, Rq = np.linalg.qr(R.T)               # Q: d×l, Rq: l×b
            lq = Q.shape[1]
            M = np.zeros((kc + Xc.shape[0], kc + lq))
            M[:kc, :kc] = np.diag(S)
            M[kc:, :kc] = P
            M[kc:, kc:] = Rq.T
            _, S2, V2t = np.linalg.svd(M, full_matrices=False)

    This is (4) written out. The subtle line is M[kc:, kc:] = Rq.T: Rq comes from qr(R.T), so it is \(l \times b\) and its transpose is the \(b \times l\) block the identity needs. Writing Rq instead transposes the residual and the singular values drift by percent-level amounts — exactly the bug this demo's \(1.6\times10^{-15}\) assertion exists to catch.

    Keeping self._V at full rank rather than at \(k\) is the other non-obvious choice, and the comment says why: truncating on the first block discards directions that later blocks may need, and no update can recover them. The public components_ is truncated to \(k\) afterwards.

    3. The sketch, and two passes that matter #

            width = min(k + self.oversample, d, n)
        Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(Xc @ rng.standard_normal((d, width)))
        for _ in range(self.power_iter):            # sharpen the spectrum
            Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(Xc.T @ Q)
            Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(Xc @ Q)
        B = Q.T @ Xc
        _, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(B, full_matrices=False)

    The loop alternates \(X_c^\top\) and \(X_c\), which is \((X_cX_c^\top)^qX_c\) applied to \(\Omega\); each trip re-orthonormalises, which is what keeps the sketch from overflowing on large data. B = Q.T @ Xc is \(O(nd\ell)\), and the SVD of \(B\) is \(O(n\ell^2)\) — with \(\ell \approx k\), that is the \(O(ndk)\) claim.

    One line that is easy to get wrong: the sketch matrix is (d, width), so Xc @ Omega is \(n \times \text{width}\). Transposing the sketch gives an \(n \times n\) matrix and a different algorithm.

    4. Kernel PCA: centre the Gram matrix, not the data #

            K = self._rbf(X, X)
        H = np.eye(n) - np.ones((n, n)) / n
        Kc = H @ K @ H
        w, A = np.linalg.eigh(Kc)
        order = np.argsort(w)[::-1][: self.n_components]
        w, A = np.maximum(w[order], 0.0), A[:, order]

    H @ K @ H is \(O(n^3)\) if written as three matrix products, and eigh is \(O(n^3)\) anyway, so for \(n\) above a few thousand the whole variant is the bottleneck. np.linalg.eigh rather than eig because \(K_c\) is symmetric: it returns real eigenvalues in ascending order, and the reversal is the sort. The np.maximum(w, 0.0) clip is for the eigenvalues that come out as \(-10^{-15}\) from roundoff and would otherwise poison sqrt.

        def _rbf(self, A, B) -> np.ndarray:
        d2 = (np.sum(A * A, axis=1)[:, None] + np.sum(B * B, axis=1)[None, :]
              - 2.0 * A @ B.T)
        return np.exp(-self.gamma * np.maximum(d2, 0.0))

    The expansion \(\|a-b\|^2 = \|a\|^2 + \|b\|^2 - 2a^\top b\) turns an \(n \times m \times d\) tensor into one matrix product. The maximum(d2, 0.0) is not cosmetic: for nearby points the three terms cancel and d2 can come out as \(-10^{-14}\), and exp(+10^{-14}) is slightly greater than one, which breaks the positive-semidefiniteness of \(K\).

            if self.variant == "kernel_pca":
            K = self._rbf(X, self.X_fit_)
            Kc = (K - K.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True) - self._col_mean_
                  + self._grand_mean_)
            return Kc @ self.components_.T

    This is (12): K.mean(axis=1) centres each test row by its own kernel mean, self._col_mean_ subtracts the training mean of each column, and self._grand_mean_ adds it back once. Getting the signs right is the difference between a transform and a projection; the demo checks the training set against eigh because that is the only case where both sides are computable.

    5. Robust PCA: two proximal operators and a multiplier #

                L = _svt(X - S + Y / mu, 1.0 / mu)
            S = _soft(X - L + Y / mu, lam / mu)
            residual = X - L - S
            Y += mu * residual
            mu = min(mu * 1.5, 1e12)
            if np.linalg.norm(residual) <= self.tol * np.linalg.norm(X):
                break

    That is (14) in five lines. The Y / mu term is the dual variable, and the mu growth is what makes the constraint \(X = L + S\) bind: early iterations are a smooth penalty, late ones are a hard constraint. The initial mu = 1.25 / max|X| keeps the first soft threshold from swallowing the whole matrix on data with a large offset.

    def _svt(A: np.ndarray, tau: float) -> np.ndarray:
    """Singular-value soft threshold: the proximal operator of the nuclear norm."""
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)
    s = np.maximum(s - tau, 0.0)
    return (U * s) @ Vt

    (U * s) scales the columns of \(U\) by the singular values before the product, which is the whole trick: the reassembled matrix is \(\sum_i (s_i-\tau)_+ u_iv_i^\top\), the closest matrix with nuclear norm reduced by \(\tau\). Writing U @ np.diag(s) @ Vt is the same thing with an extra allocation, and it is the version that appears in most write-ups.

    6. Sparse PCA: threshold the gradient, then deflate #

                for _ in range(self.n_iter):
                u = Xc.T @ (Xc @ v)
                u = _soft(u, self.lam * np.abs(u).max())
                nrm = np.linalg.norm(u)
                if nrm < 1e-12:
                    break
                v_new = u / nrm
                if np.linalg.norm(v_new - v) < self.tol:
                    v = v_new
                    break
                v = v_new
            comps[c] = v
            Xc = Xc - np.outer(Xc @ v, v)           # deflate one rank-one term

    The threshold is relative to np.abs(u).max() inside the loop, which makes lam a fraction rather than an absolute value — otherwise the same lam would mean different things on standardised and unstandardised data. The nrm < 1e-12 break is the degenerate case where the threshold has zeroed the entire gradient; returning that as a component would divide by zero.

    Deflation uses np.outer(Xc @ v, v) rather than np.outer(u, v): after thresholding, \(u\) is no longer \(X_c^\top X_c v\), and deflating by the thresholded vector would subtract the wrong rank-one term and lose variance that the next component should have seen.

    7. Whitening, and the refusal that keeps kernel PCA honest #

            Z = (X - self.mean_) @ self.components_.T
        if self.variant == "whitening":
            Z = Z / np.sqrt(self.explained_variance_)
        return Z

    One branch, no second class. explained_variance_ is \(s^2/(n-1)\) from the same SVD, so the division makes the covariance exactly \(I\) — the demo checks \(5\times10^{-16}\). Note that it divides after the projection, so the components themselves are unchanged; whitening is a diagonal rescale in score space, not a different basis.

            if self.variant == "kernel_pca":
            raise NotImplementedError(
                "kernel PCA has no exact preimage in input space; a preimage can be "
                "approximated by gradient descent on ||Φ(x) − z V^T||², which is not "
                "the same object and not implemented here"
            )

    Kernel PCA has no inverse_transform, and the message says precisely why: a preimage is an optimisation, not an inverse, and its answer depends on the initialisation. Returning the nearest training point would look like an inverse and be wrong in a way that is hard to detect downstream.

    Complexity #

    \(n\) rows, \(d\) columns, \(k\) components, \(T\) iterations.

    Variant Time Memory Dominant term
    full_svd \(O(nd\min(n,d))\) \(O(nd)\) the SVD
    randomized \(O(q\,nd(k+p) + n(k+p)^2)\) \(O(nd)\) two passes per power iteration
    kernel_pca \(O(n^2d + n^3)\) \(O(n^2)\) the Gram matrix, then eigh
    incremental \(O(bd^2 + b^2d)\) per block \(O(dk + bd)\) the QR of the residual
    robust_pca \(O(Tnd^2)\) \(O(nd)\) one SVD per iteration
    sparse_pca \(O(T\,k\,nd)\) \(O(nd)\) \(T\) passes per component
    whitening same as full_svd \(O(nd)\) the SVD

    Two entries deserve a note. robust_pca is not competitive for large \(n\): each iteration is a full SVD, and the demo's 200×40 matrix converges in ~100 iterations, but a 4K video frame (the classic background-subtraction use case) needs a truncated SVT rather than a full one. kernel_pca is \(O(n^2)\) in memory, so it is a small-data method; the \(n^3\) eigendecomposition is why the practical limit is a few thousand points.

    Failure modes #

    1. Not centring, or centring the wrong thing. np.linalg.svd(X) without subtracting the mean gives components that point at the mean rather than at the variance. The symptom is that the first component is nearly constant across rows and the reconstruction is dominated by the offset. The code centres inside _reduce for this reason. The subtler version is scaling: PCA on features in different units is dominated by whichever has the largest variance, and the "principal component" is that feature. Standardise first unless the units are comparable, and say which you did.

    2. Reading explained variance as model quality. For \(k\) equal to the rank of the data, the ratio is exactly 1, no matter how much noise there is — the ratio is a property of the spectrum, not of the fit. On the demo's rank-3 data, three components explain 100% of the variance and the discarded variance is still \(0.0177\). If you need to know how much of the signal you kept, you need a noise estimate (noise_variance_ is the mean of the discarded ones) or a held-out reconstruction error. A scree plot is a heuristic, not a test.

    3. Randomized PCA with no power iterations on a flat spectrum. The sketch sees the spectrum through the Gaussian, so when \(\sigma_{k+1}/\sigma_k\) is close to 1 the top-\(k\) subspace is not separated from the rest and the sketch returns a mixture. The diagnostic is the singular values: if the \(k\)-th and \((k+1)\)-th are within 10%, increase power_iter or use full_svd. Power iterations do not help when the sketch width is too small — the fix there is oversample.

    4. Kernel PCA with a badly scaled bandwidth. \(\gamma\) sets the length scale of the RBF. Too small and \(K \approx \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top\), so the centred Gram matrix is numerical noise; too large and \(K \approx I\), so every point is its own cluster and the components are arbitrary. The default \(\gamma = 1/d\) is a convention, not a calibration; the diagnostic is the effective rank of \(K_c\) (count eigenvalues above \(10^{-6}\lambda_{\max}\)). It should be well above \(k\) and well below \(n\).

    5. Test-time centring in kernel PCA. Covered above and repeated here because it is the most common bug in from-scratch implementations: a test block centred by its own mean has scores in a different coordinate system from the training scores. Every downstream comparison (nearest neighbour, classifier, clustering) is then invalid, and nothing crashes. The demo checks the training case explicitly because that is where the two conventions coincide.

    6. Robust PCA on dense corruption. The recovery guarantee needs \(S\) to be sparse — the constant in \(\lambda = 1/\sqrt{\max(n,d)}\) comes from a sparse-corruption model. At 5% corrupted cells with magnitude 12, the demo's own decomposition misses \(L\) by 30%; at 2% it is 1%. If a large fraction of the data is corrupted, robust PCA is not the tool; a model of the corruption is.

    7. Sparse PCA's variance bookkeeping. After deflation the components are not orthogonal, so "component 2 explains 20% of the variance" does not mean what it means in PCA: the percentages do not add to 100 across a partition. Report the reconstruction error of the \(k\)-component fit instead, or orthogonalise the components afterwards if downstream code needs it.

    Applications #

    • Visualisation and exploratory structure. Two components of standardised features are the default first plot of any tabular dataset. It is a rotation, so it preserves distances and shows the dominant directions; it does not show clusters that are not linearly separable, and reading clusters off a PCA plot is the most common over-interpretation in the literature.
    • Denoising and compression. Keep \(k\) components, reconstruct, and the discarded variance is gone — this is lossy compression with an \(L_2\) optimal basis. Image patches, sensor arrays, and spectra are the usual cases.
    • Anomaly detection. The reconstruction error of a new point against a fitted subspace is a score; large error means the point does not lie in the learned low-dimensional structure. This is the basis of most "unusual-behaviour" detectors, and it fails exactly when the anomaly is low-rank too.
    • Factor extraction in finance. The first principal component of a returns matrix is the market, the second is a curve/slope factor, and the residual is idiosyncratic. This is a descriptive decomposition, not a causal model, and the 2008 crisis was partly a story about people treating it as one.
    • Background subtraction. Frames of a video form a matrix whose columns are pixels; robust PCA puts the static background in \(L\) and moving objects in \(S\). This is the canonical application of the nuclear-norm + L1 decomposition.
    • Preprocessing. Whitening before k-means, Gaussian mixtures, or any model with an isotropic prior removes the need to learn the covariance. Kernel PCA as a feature map before a linear classifier is the nonlinear version.
    • Sparse loadings for interpretation. In genomics and chemometrics, a component with 400 non-zero loadings is unreadable; sparse PCA gives one with 20, at the cost of a slightly worse fit. When the goal is a named factor rather than a variance-maximising direction, this is the right trade.

    Where PCA is a trap: any claim about which features matter. The loadings of a component are a rotation of the original basis, and if two features are correlated, PCA is free to split their contribution arbitrarily. Use sparse PCA or a supervised method if the question is "which feature drives the outcome", and use PCA only if the question is "which direction has the most variance".
