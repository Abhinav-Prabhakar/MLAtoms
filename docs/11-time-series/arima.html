    ARIMA — past values, past errors, differences and seasons

    Intuition #

    ARIMA is the statement that a series is explainable by its own history. Three mechanisms do the explaining, and the order triple \((p,d,q)\) says how much of each:

    • AR(\(p\)) — regress on the last \(p\) values. A series that tends to continue is captured by positive coefficients; one that oscillates by alternating signs.
    • I(\(d\)) — difference \(d\) times before modelling. A random walk is not stationary, but its first difference is white noise; a straight line becomes a constant.
    • MA(\(q\)) — regress on the last \(q\) forecast errors. This is how the model represents shocks that persist for a few steps and then fade.

    The seasonal versions multiply each polynomial by a copy in \(B^m\), which adds lags at \(m,2m,\dots\) instead of shifting the whole model by a season. Exogenous regressors add a linear part, turning the model into a regression with autocorrelated errors.

    Estimation is where ARIMA gets its reputation for being fiddly, and the reason is that the errors in the MA part are unobserved: they are defined recursively from the data and the parameters. The trick used here is conditional sum of squares: assume the first few errors are zero, then the likelihood is a sum of squares, and for fixed ARMA coefficients the regression part is a linear least-squares problem that can be solved exactly. Only the short ARMA coefficient vector is searched. In the AR-only case there is nothing to search at all: the whole fit is one lstsq, and the demo checks it against OLS to machine precision.

    The second idea that makes the family coherent is that differencing is a linear filter. Forecasts are produced on the differenced scale and then integrated back up; forecast variances are the squared psi-weights of the integrated model. That single mechanism handles a random walk (whose band grows like \(\sqrt h\)), a seasonal random walk (whose forecast repeats the last cycle exactly) and a regression with AR errors, and the demo validates it against 3 000 simulated paths to 1.4%.

    Variants #

    variant model estimation demo result
    ar AR(\(p\)), no difference one least squares \(\phi=(0.5341,-0.3085)\)
    ma MA(\(q\)) CSS search \(\theta=0.6094\)
    arma ARMA(\(p,q\)) CSS search —
    arima ARIMA(\(p,d,q\)) CSS on \(\Delta^d y\) random walk exact
    sarima seasonal ARIMA polynomials in \(B^m\) ACF 0.824 to \(-0.158\)
    arimax regression + ARMA errors profiled least squares \(\beta=1.5854\)
    auto_arima order search KPSS then AICc \((2,0,0)\)

    ar is the model that is just a regression on lags. It is the only variant with a closed-form fit, and it is the right baseline for any autocorrelated series: if an AR(1) explains the data, nothing more complicated is justified.

    ma models the error process. For an MA(1) the autocovariance gives a method-of-moments estimate from \(\rho_1=\theta/(1+\theta^2)\), which is the demo's cross-check: on 400 points the CSS estimate is 0.6094 against a true 0.6, and the moment estimator gives 0.6563 from \(\rho_1=0.4587\). The two agree in magnitude but not exactly, which is the usual story: moments are consistent but inefficient, least squares is efficient but needs a search.

    arma combines them. It is the workhorse for stationary series with both persistence and short-lived shocks, and it is where the CSS search earns its keep.

    arima adds differencing. With \(p=q=0\) it is a random walk or a random walk with drift, and with \(p,q>0\) it is the Box-Jenkins model proper. The demo fits \((0,1,0)\) without a mean and gets exactly the last value, with a band whose width grows as \(\sqrt h\): 4.148 at \(h=1\) and 9.274 at \(h=5\).

    sarima multiplies the AR and MA polynomials by seasonal copies. On the demo's period-12 series with AR noise, one seasonal AR term and one seasonal difference cut the lag-12 autocorrelation from 0.824 to \(-0.158\). A seasonal random walk is exact: the forecast is the last observed cycle, because that is what \((1-B^{12})y_t=e_t\) says.

    arimax adds regressors. The demo's \(\beta\) is 1.5854 against a true 1.5 — a reminder that 300 points of AR(1) noise with \(\phi=0.6\) is an effective sample of about 75 — but the exogenous model's held-out MAE is 1.0902 against 1.5880 for an AR model that cannot see \(x\).

    auto_arima chooses \(d\) with a KPSS test and then \(p,q\) by AICc over nine candidates. On a stationary AR(2) draw it returns \((2,0,0)\) with AICc 1118.81, followed by \((1,0,2)\) at 1119.04 and \((2,0,1)\) at 1120.23; on a random walk it differences once. Order selection on a near-tie is not a decision worth defending, which is why the demo asserts only the minimum-AICc property.

    Derivation #

    The model #

    Let \(y_t\) be the observed series and

    \[w_t=\Delta^d\Delta_m^D\,y_t \qquad (1)\]

    its regular and seasonal differences. On the differenced scale the model is

    \[A(B)\,(w_t-\mu-x_t^\top\beta)=B(B)\,e_t,\qquad e_t\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2), \qquad (2)\]

    where \(A\) and \(B\) are polynomials in the backshift \(B\),

    \[A(B)=\phi(B)\Phi(B^m),\qquad B(B)=\theta(B)\Theta(B^m), \qquad (3)\]
    \[\phi(B)=1-\sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_iB^i,\quad \Phi(B^m)=1-\sum_{k=1}^{P}\Phi_kB^{km}, \qquad (4)\]

    and similarly \(\theta\) and \(\Theta\) with plus signs. Expanding the product in (3) produces cross terms: for \(p=P=1\) the AR polynomial is \(1-\phi B-\Phi B^m+\phi\Phi B^{m+1}\), so the recursion needs lag \(m+1\) as well as lags \(1\) and \(m\). That expansion is why _poly exists and why the number of AR coefficients in the recursion is not \(p+P\).

    Conditional sum of squares #

    Write \(u_t=w_t-\mu-x_t^\top\beta\) and expand (2) into a recursion:

    \[u_t=\sum_{l\ge1}a_l\,u_{t-l}+\sum_{l\ge1}b_l\,e_{t-l}+e_t, \qquad (5)\]

    where \(a\) and \(b\) are the expanded coefficients of \(-A\) and \(B\) excluding the leading one. Conditioning on \(e_1=\dots=e_L=0\), with \(L\) the largest lag, gives residuals for \(t>L\) and the objective

    \[\mathrm{SSE}(\phi,\Phi,\theta,\Theta,\mu,\beta) =\sum_{t=L+1}^{n}e_t^2 . \qquad (6)\]

    The important structural fact is that (5) is linear in \(u\), so the map from \(u\) to \(e\) is a linear filter. Therefore

    \[e(w-Z\gamma)=e(w)-\sum_k\gamma_k\,e(Z_k), \qquad (7)\]

    and for fixed ARMA coefficients the minimisation over \(\gamma=(\mu,\beta)\) is an ordinary least-squares problem on the filtered data:

    \[\hat\gamma=\arg\min_\gamma\bigl\lVert e(w)-e(Z)\gamma\bigr\rVert^2 . \qquad (8)\]

    That is the whole estimation strategy: search the short ARMA vector, solve (8) in closed form at every candidate, and keep the best. It reduces a \((p+P+q+Q+\text{regressors})\)-dimensional problem to a \((p+P+q+Q)\)-dimensional one and makes arimax no harder than arma.

    The AR case is OLS #

    When \(q=Q=0\) the filter is pure AR, and (5) becomes

    \[w_t=\sum_{l\ge1}a_l\,w_{t-l}+c+e_t,\qquad c=\mu\Bigl(1-\sum_l a_l\Bigr) \qquad (9)\]

    for a model with a constant. That is exactly the regression of \(w_t\) on its own lags and an intercept, so one lstsq gives both the AR coefficients and \(c\); the mean is recovered as \(\mu=c/(1-\sum a_l)\). The demo checks all three against an independent OLS: the coefficients, the residual sum of squares, and the implied constant. On the AR(2) draw the implied constant is \(-0.017038\), matching the OLS intercept to every printed digit.

    Forecasting #

    With future errors set to zero, (5) gives the forecast recursion

    \[\hat u_{T+h}=\sum_{l\ge1}a_l\,\hat u_{T+h-l} +\sum_{l\ge1}b_l\,\hat e_{T+h-l}, \qquad (10)\]

    where \(\hat u\) and \(\hat e\) are observed for indices up to \(T\) and recursively defined afterwards. The forecast on the differenced scale is \(\hat w_{T+h}=\hat u_{T+h}+\mu+x_{T+h}^\top\beta\), and the forecasts are then integrated back through every differencing stage:

    \[\hat y_{T+h}=\hat w_{T+h}+\hat y_{T+h-1} \quad\text{or}\quad \hat y_{T+h}=\hat w_{T+h}+\hat y_{T+h-m}, \qquad (11)\]

    depending on whether the stage was a regular or a seasonal difference. The code keeps all intermediate stages in stages_ for exactly this reason; undoing them in reverse order needs the parent series at each level.

    Psi-weights and the forecast variance #

    The psi-weights are the coefficients of the infinite MA representation of the integrated model. On the differenced scale they satisfy

    \[\psi_0=1,\qquad \psi_j=\sum_{l\ge1}a_l\,\psi_{j-l}+b_j . \qquad (12)\]

    Differencing multiplies the MA polynomial by \((1-B)^{-d}(1-B^m)^{-D}\), so the psi-weights of the observed series are obtained by cumulative summation \(d\) times and by summing every \(m\)-th term \(D\) times:

    \[\psi^{(d)}_j=\sum_{l=0}^{j}\psi^{(d-1)}_l,\qquad \psi^{(D)}_j=\sum_{k\ge0}\psi^{(D-1)}_{j-km}. \qquad (13)\]

    The \(h\)-step forecast error is \(\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j e_{T+h-j}\), hence

    \[\mathrm{Var}(\hat y_{T+h})=\sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2 . \qquad (14)\]

    For a random walk, (12) gives \(\psi_j=0\) for \(j\ge1\) and (13) turns that into \(\psi_j=1\) for all \(j\), so (14) is \(h\sigma^2\) and the band widens as \(\sqrt h\) — the demo's 4.148 to 9.274 over five steps. For the fitted ARIMA(1,1,1) the weights are \(1,1.877,2.434,2.788\), and the 3 000 simulated paths match the analytic standard deviations to 1.3%.

    Order selection #

    AICc compares models on the same differenced scale:

    \[\mathrm{AICc}=-2\log L+2k+\frac{2k(k+1)}{n-k-1}, \qquad (15)\]

    with \(k\) counting ARMA coefficients, regression coefficients, and \(\sigma^2\). Comparing across different \(d\) is a mistake — each difference removes an observation and changes the scale — so auto_arima fixes \(d\) first with the KPSS test:

    \[\eta=\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}S_t^2\Big/\hat\lambda^2,\qquad S_t=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\hat e_i, \qquad (16)\]

    where \(\hat e\) are the residuals from a regression on a constant and \(\hat\lambda^2\) is a Newey-West long-run variance. The null is level stationarity, so a value above 0.463 rejects at 5% and the series is differenced once more, up to twice. On the AR(2) draw the statistic is 0.232; on a random walk it is 1.44.

    Code walkthrough #

    The filter is the heart of the file: one loop, two dot products, and the lag arrays already expanded.

    def _filter(series: np.ndarray, a: np.ndarray, b: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Residuals of `u_t = sum a_i u_{t-i} + e_t + sum b_j e_{t-j}`."""
    n = series.size
    out = np.zeros(n)
    la, lb = a.size, b.size
    start = max(la, lb)
    for t in range(start, n):
        value = float(series[t])
        if la:
            value -= float(a @ series[t - la:t][::-1])
        if lb:
            value -= float(b @ out[t - lb:t][::-1])
        out[t] = value
    return out

    series[t - la:t][::-1] is the window \(u_{t-1},\dots,u_{t-la}\) in lag order, so the dot product pairs \(a_1\) with \(u_{t-1}\) without an index reversal elsewhere. The output is written in place, which is what lets the MA part read its own past residuals.

    The seasonal polynomials are built by convolution, not by hand:

    def _poly(regular, seasonal, m: int, sign: float) -> np.ndarray:
    """Dense coefficients of `(1 + sign*r(B))(1 + sign*S(B^m))`."""
    first = np.zeros(len(regular) + 1)
    first[0] = 1.0
    first[1:] = sign * np.asarray(regular, dtype=float)
    second = np.zeros(len(seasonal) * m + 1)
    second[0] = 1.0
    second[m::m] = sign * np.asarray(seasonal, dtype=float)
    return np.convolve(first, second)

    The slice assignment second[m::m] puts the seasonal coefficients at \(m,2m, \dots\) and np.convolve produces the cross terms of (3) automatically. The sign argument is what distinguishes the AR polynomial from the MA one; in the caller the AR result is negated once more, because (5) moves the AR terms to the left-hand side.

    The AR fast path is a single least-squares call, with the mean recovered afterwards:

            matrix = np.column_stack(blocks)
        coef, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(matrix, w[rows], rcond=None)
        a = coef[:length]
        constant = coef[length:]
        if design.shape[1] and self.include_mean:
            total = float(a.sum())
            mean = (constant[0] / (1.0 - total)
                    if abs(1.0 - total) > 1e-9 else 0.0)
            gamma = np.concatenate([[mean], constant[1:]])

    The guard on \(1-\sum a_l\) matters: a sum of AR coefficients exactly one is a unit root, and dividing by zero there would produce an infinite mean. It cannot happen for stationary data, but it can for a differenced series that is still non-stationary.

    The search path profiles the regression coefficients out of the objective, so the pattern search only ever moves the ARMA vector:

            def profile(values):
            a, b = split(values)
            start = max(a.size, b.size)
            filtered = _filter(w, a, b)
            if design.shape[1]:
                blocks = np.column_stack([_filter(design[:, j], a, b)
                                          for j in range(design.shape[1])])
                coef, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(blocks[start:], filtered[start:],
                                           rcond=None)
                residuals = filtered[start:] - blocks[start:] @ coef
            else:
                coef = np.zeros(0)
                residuals = filtered[start:]
            return float(residuals @ residuals), a, b, coef

    This is (8) written out: filter the response and every regressor with the same filter, then regress one on the others. For arimax that means the exog column is filtered alongside \(w\), which is exactly the GLS transformation that makes the regression coefficients consistent in the presence of AR errors.

    Forecasting keeps the observed residuals and extends both arrays by the horizon, so the same recursion serves the observed and future parts:

            for j in range(horizon):
            value = 0.0
            for i, coef in enumerate(self.a_, 1):
                value += coef * extended[n + j - i]
            for k, coef in enumerate(self.b_, 1):
                value += coef * innovations[n + j - k]
            extended[n + j] = value
        differenced = extended[n:] + future @ gamma
        return self._integrate(differenced)

    innovations beyond \(T\) stays zero, which is the conditional expectation of a future shock; extended beyond \(T\) is filled by the recursion itself. Note that the future design is multiplied by \(\gamma\) after the recursion, because the recursion is on \(u=w-Z\gamma\), not on \(w\).

    Undoing the differences walks the stages backwards:

            for _ in range(self.D_):
            parent = self.stages_[index - 1]
            n = parent.size
            extended = np.concatenate([parent, np.zeros(path.size)])
            for j in range(path.size):
                extended[n + j] = path[j] + extended[n + j - self.season_]
            path = extended[n:]
            index -= 1

    Appending zeros to the parent and filling forward is the inverse of differencing in four lines. The seasonal loop runs before the regular one because the stages were built in the other order; getting that order wrong produces a forecast that looks plausible but is integrated from the wrong level.

    The psi-weights are the same recursion as the forecast, with an indicator instead of an innovation:

            for j in range(1, horizon):
            value = float(self.b_[j - 1]) if j - 1 < self.b_.size else 0.0
            for i, coef in enumerate(self.a_, 1):
                if j - i >= 0:
                    value += coef * weights[j - i]
            weights[j] = value

    This is (12). The if j - i >= 0 guard is the difference between computing \(\psi_j\) and indexing before the start of the array; for \(j those terms are absent because \(\psi\) is zero at negative lags.

    The order search is two decisions, and the first is a test rather than a criterion:

    def _choose_d(y: np.ndarray, limit: int = 2) -> int:
    """Smallest number of differences that passes the KPSS test."""
    current = y
    for d in range(limit + 1):
        if current.size < 20:
            break
        if _kpss(current) <= 0.463:
            return d
        current = np.diff(current)
    return min(limit, y.size // 20)

    Differences are added until the statistic falls below the 5% critical value, with a hard cap at two and a size guard so a short series is not differenced into nothing. The AICc loop then only has to choose \(p\) and \(q\) at that fixed \(d\).

    Complexity #

    With \(n\) observations, \(L\) the largest expanded lag, \(p+P+q+Q\) the number of searched coefficients and \(h\) the horizon:

    • One filter: \(O(nL)\).
    • One AR fit: one lstsq on an \(n\times L\) matrix, \(O(nL^2)\).
    • One search: about eight step halvings times two directions times the number of coefficients, each evaluation costing one filter per design column.
    • auto_arima: nine fits at the chosen \(d\), plus the KPSS passes.
    • predict: \(O(hL)\); interval: adds \(O(hL)\) for the psi-weights.
    • Memory: \(O(n)\) for the series and its stages, \(O(L)\) for the coefficients.

    The demo's largest job is the 3 000-path simulation of an ARIMA(1,1,1), which runs the forecast recursion 36 000 times — still under a second, because the AR and MA vectors have one element each.

    Failure modes #

    Over-differencing. Each difference costs an observation, inflates the variance and pushes the MA polynomial towards a unit root at \(-1\), where the CSS surface is flat. Worse, AICc computed on the differenced series is not comparable across \(d\): in a control run outside the demo, an AICc search over \(d\) chose \((2,1,2)\) for a stationary AR(1) and beat the correct \((1,0,0)\) by more than 20 points. That is why auto_arima fixes \(d\) with KPSS instead of including it in the criterion.

    Roots on the unit circle. The search bounds every ARMA coefficient at \(\pm0.98\). A true MA root at \(-1\) (which is what a first difference of a stationary series produces) cannot be represented, so the fit parks a coefficient near the boundary and compensates with extra AR terms. If a fitted MA coefficient sits at the bound, the model is trying to difference again; inspect it rather than trusting the forecast.

    Spurious regression in arimax. If \(y\) and \(x\) are both trending and the errors are strongly autocorrelated, the profiled \(\beta\) can be large and significant with no causal content. The demo's \(\beta\) is 1.5854 against a true 1.5, which is honest sampling error at an effective sample of 75 — but it is also the size of error that a naive t-test would call a discovery. Difference or detrend both series before reading anything into \(\beta\).

    Seasonal lags eat the sample. With \(m=12\) and \(P=Q=1\) the largest lag is 12, so the first 12 residuals are discarded before estimation begins. On 60 points that is 20% of the data, and on two cycles it is most of the information. Require at least three cycles for a seasonal model, and prefer \(P=Q=1\) to larger orders.

    A forecast needs a forecast of \(x\). arimax requires future exog values, and the interval conditional on them ignores their uncertainty. A promotion forecast built on an assumed price path is only as good as that path; the ARIMA band does not include the risk that the assumption is wrong.

    Applications #

    ARIMA is the default model in macroeconomics and official statistics: monthly inflation, industrial production and GDP are still routinely forecast with SARIMA, and the Box-Jenkins workflow — identify, estimate, diagnose, forecast — is taught as the entry point to time-series modelling. Its appeal is that the components mean something: the AR coefficients describe persistence, the MA coefficients describe shock decay, and the differencing describes what kind of non-stationarity the series has.

    auto_arima is the standard baseline on Kaggle-style forecasting problems and in production demand pipelines, precisely because it is cheap and hard to beat on short, univariate series. arimax is the standard way to adjust a forecast for a known driver — price, weather, a holiday dummy — while keeping the error structure honest.

    It is a trap when the series has a structural break, when the seasonality is multiple and evolving (use a state-space or neural model), or when the driver is itself forecast. It is also a trap at long horizons: the psi-weights of an integrated model grow without bound, so a 24-step band on a random walk is almost useless, and that is a property of the model, not a bug.
