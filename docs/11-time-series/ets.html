    Exponential smoothing — level, trend and season in one recursion

    Intuition #

    A forecast is a summary that keeps correcting itself. Simple exponential smoothing holds one number, the level, and after each observation moves it a fraction of the way toward the error it just made:

    \[\ell_t=\ell_{t-1}+\alpha\,(y_t-\ell_{t-1}). \qquad (1)\]

    That is a weighted average with geometrically decaying weights, and \(\alpha\) is the only thing to choose. It is the cheapest adaptive forecaster in existence and the ancestor of everything in this file.

    Holt added a slope, so the summary becomes a line: one state for where the series is, one for where it is going. Winters added a season, so the summary becomes a line plus a repeating profile. Damping shrinks the slope so that a long-horizon forecast flattens instead of shooting off. These are the three switches of the ETS taxonomy — Error, Trend, Season — and every variant here is the same loop with some states present and some absent.

    Two things make this more than a bag of heuristics. First, the recursions are exactly the error-correction form of a linear state-space model, so the same recursion that produces point forecasts also produces an exact forecast variance by propagating a covariance matrix. The demo checks that band against 2 000 simulated paths and the two agree to 2.6% over eighteen steps. Second, the smoothing weights can be estimated by minimising the one-step squared error, which for additive errors is maximum likelihood, and the resulting likelihood supports AICc comparison across models with different numbers of states. On the demo's seasonal series the best seasonal model beats the best non-seasonal one by 177 AICc points.

    The interesting tension is that all of this is still local: one level, one slope, one profile. A series with a regime change will be chased slowly, and no amount of AICc will warn you.

    Variants #

    variant ETS states parameters demo MAE

    holt A,A,N level, trend \(\alpha,\beta\) 2.0781
    damped_trend A,Ad,N level, damped trend \(\alpha,\beta,\phi\) 1.9777
    holt_winters_add A,A,A + additive season \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) 0.9615
    holt_winters_mul A,A,M season × level \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) 1.0841
    ets_state_space A,Ad,A damped, seasonal \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\phi\) 0.8984
    auto_ets searched chosen by AICc chosen 0.9615

    simple_es is (1). On a seasonal series it does the only thing it can: the demo's optimiser drives \(\alpha\) to its 0.999 ceiling and the model degenerates to the naive forecast. That is not a bug, it is the method telling you it has no way to represent a period.

    holt adds a slope updated by its own error term. It is the right default for a series with a local linear trend and no season, and it is what drift becomes when the slope is allowed to adapt.

    damped_trend multiplies the slope by \(\phi\in(0,1)\) at every step, so the forecast is a geometric series that converges to a finite level. On the demo it edges out holt (1.9777 against 2.0781) with a fitted \(\phi\) at the 0.8 lower bound, which means the data wants very aggressive damping.

    holt_winters_add adds a seasonal profile whose entries are added to the line. This is the model to reach for when the seasonal swing is roughly constant in absolute size — temperature, staffing, scheduled maintenance.

    holt_winters_mul multiplies the line by the profile, so the swing grows with the level. On the demo's tripling series it cuts the in-sample SSE from 87.468 to 57.861 and the held-out MAE from 0.8698 to 0.7888.

    ets_state_space is the same additive-season model written as \(x_t=Fx_{t-1}+ge_t\) with a damped trend, which buys two things: the matrices are exposed, and interval uses the exact variance recursion instead of a heuristic. Its fitted \(\phi\) sits at 0.995, the top of the search range.

    auto_ets fits the nine admissible combinations and returns the one with the smallest AICc. On the additive series it chooses ETS(A,A,A) with the same parameters as holt_winters_add; on the multiplicative series it switches to ETS(A,A,M).

    Derivation #

    Setup #

    Write the level \(\ell_t\), the slope \(b_t\), and a profile \(s_t\) with period \(m\). The one-step forecast is built from the states as they stood before \(y_t\) was seen, and the error is

    \[e_t=y_t-\hat y_t . \qquad (2)\]

    Every update below is an error-correction step: the state moves in proportion to \(e_t\), and the forecast for \(t+1\) is recomputed from the moved states. This is the form in which the recursions are both easiest to implement and exactly equivalent to the state-space model of (9).

    Level only #

    With \(\ell_0=y_1\) and \(\hat y_t=\ell_{t-1}\),

    \[\ell_t=\ell_{t-1}+\alpha e_t,\qquad \hat y_{T+h}=\ell_T . \qquad (3)\]

    Unrolling the recursion gives the weighted-average form the demo asserts,

    \[\hat y_{T+1}=\alpha\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}(1-\alpha)^j y_{T-j} +(1-\alpha)^T y_1 , \qquad (4)\]

    which makes the memory explicit: the weight on an observation \(j\) steps old is \(\alpha(1-\alpha)^j\), so a half-life of \(\log(1/2)/\log(1-\alpha)\) observations.

    Trend, and damping #

    Holt's model adds a slope that is itself smoothed:

    \[\ell_t=\ell_{t-1}+\phi b_{t-1}+\alpha e_t,\qquad b_t=\phi b_{t-1}+\beta e_t,\qquad \hat y_t=\ell_{t-1}+\phi b_{t-1}. \qquad (5)\]

    With \(\phi=1\) this is Holt; with \(\phi<1\) the slope decays geometrically and the forecast is

    \[\hat y_{T+h}=\ell_T+b_T\sum_{j=1}^{h}\phi^j \;\longrightarrow\; \ell_T+\frac{\phi b_T}{1-\phi} \qquad (6)\]

    as \(h\to\infty\). The demo's fitted path converges to 54.619977, which is exactly \(\ell_T+0.9b_T/(1-0.9)\), and the gaps between successive forecasts shrink monotonically. Damping is the difference between a forecast that is still extrapolating at \(h=100\) and one that has settled.

    Seasonality #

    The additive profile is a fourth state, indexed modulo \(m\):

    \[\hat y_t=\ell_{t-1}+\phi b_{t-1}+s_{t-m},\qquad s_t=s_{t-m}+\gamma e_t . \qquad (7)\]

    The multiplicative version scales the line by the profile:

    \[\hat y_t=(\ell_{t-1}+\phi b_{t-1})\,s_{t-m},\qquad s_t=s_{t-m}+\gamma\,\frac{e_t}{\ell_{t-1}+\phi b_{t-1}} . \qquad (8)\]

    and its level and slope updates divide \(e_t\) by \(s_{t-m}\), because a relative error of \(\varepsilon_t=e_t/\hat y_t\) is what the multiplicative model assumes. Equations (7) and (8) are why the code guards season_now > 0 and base > 0: a zero or negative seasonal factor makes the multiplicative recursion meaningless, and during a parameter search it is reached routinely.

    The state-space form #

    Stack the states into \(x_t=(\ell_t,b_t,s_t,\dots,s_{t-m+1})^\top\) with the seasonal entry in use first. Then (5) and (7) are

    \[x_t=Fx_{t-1}+g e_t,\qquad \hat y_t=w^\top x_{t-1}, \qquad (9)\]

    with

    \[F=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \phi & 1 & 0 & \cdots\\ 0 & \phi & 0 & 0 & \cdots\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & \cdots\\ \vdots & & & \ddots & \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \end{pmatrix},\quad w=\begin{pmatrix}1\\ \phi\\ 1\\ 0\\ \vdots\end{pmatrix},\quad g=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\ \beta\\ 0\\ \vdots\\ \gamma\end{pmatrix}. \qquad (10)\]

    The first row of \(F\) rebuilds the forecast from level, damped slope and the seasonal entry; the last row of the seasonal block is zero because the new seasonal value arrives through \(g e_t\). The shift rows rotate the profile, so the entry used at \(t+1\) moves to the front. For a non-seasonal model the seasonal rows and columns are simply absent, and \(F\) is \(1\times1\) or \(2\times2\).

    Forecast variance #

    Because (9) is linear in the state, the forecast covariance propagates exactly. Let \(P_0=0\), conditional on the estimated states, and iterate

    \[\mathrm{Var}(\hat y_{T+h})=w^\top P_{h-1}w+\sigma^2,\qquad P_h=FP_{h-1}F^\top+\sigma^2 gg^\top . \qquad (11)\]

    The trailing \(\sigma^2\) is the innovation in \(y_{T+h}\) itself; the covariance term carries the uncertainty accumulated in the states. For simple exponential smoothing \(F=1\), \(w=1\), \(g=\alpha\), so \(P_h=h\alpha^2\sigma^2\) and

    \[\mathrm{Var}(\hat y_{T+h})=\sigma^2\bigl(1+(h-1)\alpha^2\bigr), \qquad (12)\]

    which the demo checks against the band to machine precision: at \(h=6\) with \(\alpha=0.4\) the standard deviation is 3.3064. Simulation of the full state-space model is the independent check, and it agrees to 2.6%.

    Estimation and selection #

    With additive errors and \(\sigma^2\) concentrated out, minus twice the Gaussian log-likelihood is

    \[-2\log L=n_{\rm eff}\Bigl(\log\frac{2\pi\,\mathrm{SSE}}{n_{\rm eff}}+1\Bigr), \qquad (13)\]

    a monotone function of the sum of squared one-step errors, so minimising SSE is maximum likelihood. For multiplicative seasonality the likelihood is written in the relative errors with the Jacobian \(-\sum_t\log\hat y_t\), which is why sigma2_ is a relative variance for those models. The information criteria are

    \[\mathrm{AIC}=-2\log L+2k,\qquad \mathrm{AICc}=\mathrm{AIC}+\frac{2k(k+1)}{n_{\rm eff}-k-1}, \qquad (14)\]

    with \(k\) counting the smoothing parameters, \(\sigma^2\), and the initial states. The correction matters here: a seasonal model carries \(m\) extra states, and at \(n_{\rm eff}=108\) and \(m=12\) the AICc penalty for those states is tens of points, not a rounding error.

    Initialisation #

    The first season sets the level and the profile, and the first two seasons set the slope:

    \[\ell_0=\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^{m}y_j,\qquad s_{1-j}=y_j-\ell_0,\qquad b_0=\frac{\bar y_{m+1:2m}-\bar y_{1:m}}{m}. \qquad (15)\]

    With a multiplicative profile the subtraction is a division. The first \(m\) residuals are near zero by construction, so the fit drops them from the SSE and from \(n_{\rm eff}\); that is the start_ attribute, and it is why a seasonal fit on 120 points reports 108 usable residuals.

    Code walkthrough #

    Everything happens in one loop over the series, with the seasonal entry rotated so that seasons[0] is always the value for the current step:

            for t in range(y.size):
            season_now = float(seasons[0]) if with_season else 0.0
            base = level + phi * slope
            if season == "A":
                forecast = base + season_now
            elif season == "M":
                if base <= 1e-9 or season_now <= 1e-9:
                    return None
                forecast = base * season_now
            else:
                forecast = base

    base is computed once and reused by both the forecast and the multiplicative updates, which is what keeps (8) consistent. The return None is not an error path for the user: it is how an invalid parameter combination is signalled to the optimiser, which turns it into an infinite loss.

    The correction step is the whole of (5) and (8) in four lines:

                error = float(y[t]) - forecast
            level = base + alpha * (error if season != "M"
                                    else error / season_now)
            slope = phi * slope + (beta * (error if season != "M"
                                           else error / season_now))

    Reading the two conditional expressions is the fastest way to see why the multiplicative model needs the seasonal factor twice: the level error is relative to \(s_{t-m}\), and the slope error is too. For an additive model the same lines are the textbook Holt updates.

    The profile is rotated with a slice and a concatenation rather than with an index variable:

                if season == "A":
                seasons = np.concatenate([seasons[1:], [season_now + gamma * error]])
            elif season == "M":
                seasons = np.concatenate([seasons[1:],
                                          [season_now + gamma * error / base]])

    After this line seasons[0] is the value for \(t+1\), so the loop never has to know where in the cycle it is. The cost is a copy of \(m\) floats per step, which is irrelevant next to the clarity.

    The parameters are found by a coordinate pattern search: try a step up and down each free parameter, keep any improvement, and halve all step sizes when a full sweep finds nothing.

            for _ in range(90):
            improved = False
            for name in free:
                low, high = LIMITS[name]
                for delta in (steps[name], -steps[name]):
                    trial = dict(values)
                    trial[name] = float(np.clip(values[name] + delta,
                                                low, high))
                    if trial[name] == values[name]:
                        continue
                    score = loss(trial)
                    if score < best - 1e-12:
                        values, best, improved = trial, score, True

    There is no gradient because propagating one through the recursion gives an expression that changes shape with the model and is easy to get subtly wrong, and because \(lpha\) and \(eta\) trade off along a flat ridge where a gradient's step size is hard to tune. Pattern search is slower but far harder to derail. Two limits are clipped: \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) to \([0.01,0.999]\) and \(\phi\) to \([0.80,0.995]\), the range in which a damped trend is identified rather than merely fitted.

    Forecasting damps the slope, not just the first step:

            for h in range(horizon):
            base = level + phi * slope
            if self.season_ == "A":
                out[h] = base + seasons[h % self.season_period_]
            elif self.season_ == "M":
                out[h] = base * seasons[h % self.season_period_]
            else:
                out[h] = base
            level = base
            slope = phi * slope

    The slope = phi * slope line is the one that is easy to forget, and forgetting it turns (6) into an undamped line with a \(\phi\)-scaled slope that diverges. The damped-limit assertion in the demo exists precisely to catch that bug.

    state_space builds the matrices of (10) from the fitted states, which is used by interval and by the demo's simulation:

            if self.season_ == "A":
            matrix[0, 2] = 1.0
            for j in range(m - 1):
                matrix[2 + j, 3 + j] = 1.0
            w[2] = 1.0
            g[-1] = self.params_["gamma"]

    The shift is matrix[2 + j, 3 + j] = 1.0, so row 2 copies column 3, row 3 copies column 4, and so on; the last seasonal row is left at zero because its new value comes from g. Only additive seasonality has this form, so the method raises for multiplicative models rather than inventing a matrix.

    The variance recursion is six lines and no simulation:

                matrix, w, g, _ = self.state_space()
            covariance = np.zeros_like(matrix)
            variance = np.empty(horizon)
            for h in range(horizon):
                variance[h] = float(w @ covariance @ w) + self.sigma2_
                covariance = (matrix @ covariance @ matrix.T
                              + self.sigma2_ * np.outer(g, g))

    covariance starts at zero because the states are treated as known; that understates the band slightly, since it ignores the error in estimating \(\ell_T\) and \(b_T\), but it is the standard conditional ETS variance and it is what the Monte Carlo check validates.

    Model selection is a loop over candidates with AICc as the comparator:

            best, table = None, []
        for trend, season in candidates:
            fitted = self._fit_spec(y, trend, season)
            table.append((f"ETS(A,{trend},{season})", fitted["aicc"]))
            if best is None or fitted["aicc"] < best["aicc"]:
                best = fitted

    table is kept so that the chosen model can be interrogated: the demo prints all nine AICc values and the gap between the first and second is 3.4 points, while the gap to the best non-seasonal model is 177.

    Complexity #

    With \(n\) observations, \(m\) the period, \(p\) free parameters and \(h\) the horizon:

    • One pass of the recursion: \(O(n)\) time — the seasonal rotation copies \(m\) values per step, so the constant is \(O(m)\) — and \(O(n)\) memory for the fitted values and residuals.
    • One fit: \(O(n \cdot p \cdot 2 \cdot R)\) where \(R\approx 8\) is the number of step halvings; the demo's nine-candidate auto_ets is about seventy thousand recursion steps.
    • predict: \(O(h)\).
    • interval: \(O(h\,d^3)\) for the \(d\times d\) covariance products, with \(d=1+m\) at most 14. Negligible.
    • The demo's simulation: 2 000 paths of 18 steps, 36 000 small matrix products, is the most expensive thing in the file.

    This is \(O(n)\) against \(O(n^3)\) for a Gaussian process and \(O(np)\) per boosting round, which is why exponential smoothing survived the neural era: it is the cheapest model that represents a trend and a season at all.

    Failure modes #

    A weight stuck at its boundary is a diagnosis, not a nuisance. simple_es ends at \(\alpha=0.999\) on the seasonal series because no value of \(\alpha\) can represent a period-12 pattern; the best it can do is track the last observation. A grid over 200 values confirms 0.995 as the true optimum, so the boundary is real. When a weight hits the ceiling, the model is misspecified — add the missing state rather than narrowing the bound.

    AICc and a short holdout can disagree. ets_state_space has the best 12-point MAE (0.8984 against 0.9615) but a worse AICc (325.25 against 321.87), because the extra damping parameter does not pay for itself in-sample. With twelve held-out points the MAE difference is one noise draw; AICc uses 108 residuals. Prefer the criterion that uses more data, and treat a one-cycle holdout as a sanity check, not a ranking.

    A trend model on a series without a trend. Holt and the damped variant will extrapolate whatever slope the last few points imply, and damping only shrinks it. On a mean-reverting series a differencing or a mean forecast is safer; the diagnostic is a residual autocorrelation that stays positive at long lags.

    Multiplicative seasonality near zero. Equations (8) divide by the level and the seasonal factor, so a series that touches zero makes the recursion explode. The code refuses non-positive \(y\) up front, but a series that is merely small is still numerically fragile: relative errors become enormous and the SSE surface is dominated by the smallest values. Use the additive model unless the swing is demonstrably proportional to the level.

    Discarding the first season. With \(m=12\) and \(n=120\), 10% of the sample is spent on initialisation. On a series of two cycles there are only twelve usable residuals, \(k\) can approach \(n_{\rm eff}\), and AICc's denominator \(n_{\rm eff}-k-1\) becomes small enough to dominate the ranking. Require at least three cycles before trusting auto_ets.

    The wrong period. A seasonal model with \(m\) off by one forecasts the wrong phase every cycle. Unlike the baselines in baselines.py, ETS does not warn: the SSE falls, because a misaligned profile still absorbs variance. Always compare against the non-seasonal model on held-out data, and inspect the fitted profile for a plausible shape.

    Applications #

    Exponential smoothing is the production forecaster for demand planning, call centre staffing, energy load and inventory replenishment, and it has been for sixty years. The reasons are practical: the states are interpretable (a planner can read the current level and the seasonal index), the model is cheap enough to refit every night for millions of SKUs, and the intervals from (11) are honest enough for safety stock.

    The ETS framework also gives a principled way to answer "which model?" without a validation set, which matters when each series is short. auto_ets is the workhorse behind the forecast package's ets(), and the AICc table it produces is a useful artefact in its own right: it shows how much of the signal each state is buying.

    Hierarchical forecasting uses the additive decomposition directly: forecasts at the region, category and SKU level must add up, and the level/trend/season states give a natural space in which to reconcile them.

    It is a trap when the series is driven by known covariates (price, promotion, weather), where a regression with ARIMA errors or a gradient-boosted model will win, and when the future is a regime change rather than a continuation. It is also a trap for long horizons with a seasonal model: the profile repeats forever, and after two or three cycles the band is wide enough that the point forecast is not the interesting part.
