    VAR — one autoregression for the whole system

    Intuition #

    A VAR is what you get when you stop asking "how does \(y\) depend on its own past" and start asking "how does each variable depend on everyone's past". Every equation has the same right-hand side — a constant and \(p\) lags of all \(k\) series — so the system is just \(k\) regressions stacked side by side. That symmetry is the whole reason the model is easy to estimate: equation-by-equation least squares is not an approximation, it is exactly the joint MLE.

    The interesting content is in what the fitted matrices let you ask:

    • Stability. Stack the \(p\) coefficient matrices into one companion matrix. If every eigenvalue is inside the unit circle, shocks die out; if not, the fitted system implies an explosive path and forecasts are meaningless beyond a few steps.
    • Granger causality. Does adding lags of \(x\) improve the forecast of \(y\)? That is an \(F\)-test on a block of coefficients. It is a statement about predictability inside this sample and this lag order, not about intervention.
    • Impulse responses. The MA representation turns the fitted recursion into a sequence of matrices \(\Psi_h\): what a one-unit shock at time \(t\) does to the system at \(t+h\). Orthogonalising with a Cholesky factor makes the shocks uncorrelated, at the cost of imposing a causal ordering.
    • Forecast error variance decomposition. At each horizon, split each variable's forecast uncertainty into shares attributable to each orthogonal shock. It answers "what moves \(y\)" with the same ordering caveat.

    The variants here are the standard extensions: exogenous regressors (varx), the three diagnostics as first-class variants (granger, irf, fevd), and a ridge-penalised fit for systems where \(k^2p\) is large relative to the sample.

    Variants #

    variant adds demo result
    var the plain reduced-form system \(A\) recovered, stable
    varx contemporaneous regressors \(x_t\) \(B=\mathrm{diag}(0.78,-0.54)\)
    granger \(F\)-tests for block exclusion \(p=2.7\times10^{-22}\)
    irf orthogonalised responses \(\Psi_1=\hat A\)
    fevd variance shares \([0.80,0.20]\)
    var_ridge L2 penalty on coefficients norm 0.494 vs 0.870

    var is the base model. On 400 points of a bivariate VAR(1) with \(A=[[0.6,0],[0.35,0.45]]\) it estimates \([[0.6264,0.0343],[0.4187,0.4336]]\), with companion eigenvalues \(0.6838\) and \(0.3761\) — comfortably stable. The demo also fits a VAR(2) and checks that the one-step forecast equals \(c+A_1y_T+A_2y_{T-1}\).

    varx appends \(x_t\) to every equation. This is the multivariate analogue of ARIMAX: the coefficients on \(x\) are estimated jointly with the autoregressive part, so a persistent \(y\) does not masquerade as an effect of \(x\). On the demo's \(y_t=Ay_{t-1}+Bx_t+u_t\) with \(B=\mathrm{diag}(0.8,-0.5)\), the fit recovers \([[0.7802,-0.0634],[0.0391,-0.5427]]\). It is still a regression, not an identification strategy: if \(x_t\) responds to \(y_t\) within the same period, the coefficient is not causal.

    granger computes the \(F\)-statistic for each ordered pair. In the demo, \(y_0\) strongly predicts \(y_1\) (\(F=106.64\), \(p=2.7\times10^{-22}\)) while \(y_1\) does not predict \(y_0\) (\(F=0.74\), \(p=0.390\)), exactly as the true zero in \(A_{01}\) implies. granger_pvalues_ is a \(k\times k\) matrix with the effect in the row and the cause in the column, and \(1\) on the diagonal because self-causation is not tested.

    irf stores the orthogonalised responses at the requested horizon. For a VAR(1) the reduced-form weights are powers of \(\hat A\): the demo checks \(\Psi_1=\hat A\) and \(\Psi_2=\hat A^2\) to machine precision, and checks that the \(h=0\) response times its transpose reproduces \(\hat\Sigma\).

    fevd turns those responses into variance shares. It is the diagnostic that makes the ordering assumption visible: on data with correlated shocks, the second variable's variance is 80% attributed to the first shock, because the Cholesky factor lets shock one move both variables at impact and shock two move only the second.

    var_ridge replaces OLS with a ridge solve, leaving the intercept unpenalised. The demo's coefficient norm drops from 0.8700 to 0.4942 at \(\lambda=1\), and ridge=0 reproduces OLS exactly. The penalty is scaled by the trace of the Gram matrix so that \(\lambda\) means something across data sets.

    Derivation #

    The model and its stacked form #

    With \(k\) variables, \(p\) lags and \(n\) observations,

    \[Y_t=c+\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_iY_{t-i}+u_t,\qquad u_t\sim\mathcal N(0,\Sigma). \qquad (1)\]

    Collect the regressors for row \(t\) into \(z_t=(1,Y_{t-1}^\top,\dots,Y_{t-p}^\top)^\top\in\mathbb R^{d}\) with \(d=1+kp\), and stack rows \(t=p+1,\dots,n\) into \(Z\in\mathbb R^{n_0\times d}\) and \(Y\in\mathbb R^{n_0\times k}\). Then (1) is the matrix regression

    \[Y=ZB+U,\qquad \hat B=(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top Y . \qquad (2)\]

    Because every equation shares the same \(Z\), (2) is numerically identical to \(k\) separate least-squares fits; the code uses one lstsq, and the demo verifies the equality by refitting each equation by hand. Writing \(B\) as \([c^\top;B_1;\dots;B_p]\) and transposing the lag block gives coef_[i] = \(A_{i+1}\). The residual covariance is \(\hat\Sigma=\hat U^\top\hat U/(n_0-d)\).

    Stability #

    Define the state \(\xi_t=(Y_t^\top,\dots,Y_{t-p+1}^\top)^\top\) and the companion matrix

    \[F=\begin{bmatrix} A_1&A_2&\cdots&A_{p-1}&A_p\\ I&0&\cdots&0&0\\ 0&I&\cdots&0&0\\ \vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\ 0&0&\cdots&I&0 \end{bmatrix}. \qquad (3)\]

    Then \(\xi_t=F\xi_{t-1}+v_t\), and the system is stable exactly when \(\rho(F)=\max_j\lvert\lambda_j(F)\rvert<1\). For \(p=1\), \(F=A_1\). The demo prints \(\rho\) for both fitted systems: \(0.6838\) for the VAR(1) and \(0.8035\) for the VAR(2), whose companion has four eigenvalues because the state is four dimensional.

    Impulse responses #

    Repeated substitution of (1) gives the MA representation

    \[Y_t=\mu+\sum_{h\ge0}\Psi_h u_{t-h},\qquad \Psi_0=I,\quad \Psi_h=\sum_{i=1}^{\min(p,h)}A_i\Psi_{h-i}. \qquad (4)\]

    The reduced-form shocks are correlated, so a unit shock to \(u_{1t}\) also moves \(u_{2t}\) on impact. The Cholesky factor \(P\) with \(PP^\top=\Sigma\) (lower triangular) defines orthogonal shocks \(\varepsilon_t=P^{-1}u_t\), and

    \[\Theta_h=\Psi_hP \qquad (5)\]

    is the response to a one-standard-deviation shock in \(\varepsilon_j\) alone. The triangularity of \(P\) is what imposes the ordering: \(\varepsilon_1\) affects every variable at impact, \(\varepsilon_2\) affects variables \(2,\dots,k\), and so on. A different ordering produces a different \(\Theta_0\) and different long-run shares; the data do not choose between them.

    Forecast error variance decomposition #

    The \(H\)-step forecast error is \(\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\Theta_h\varepsilon_{t+H-h}\), so the contribution of shock \(j\) to variable \(i\) is the sum of squares of the \((i,j)\) entries:

    \[\mathrm{FEVD}_{ij}(H)= \frac{\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\Theta_{h,ij}^2} {\sum_{j'=1}^{k}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\Theta_{h,ij'}^2}. \qquad (6)\]

    Each row sums to one. At \(H=1\) the shares are \(\Theta_0^2\) normalised, which is not the identity when \(\Sigma\) is non-diagonal; as \(H\) grows they approach the long-run shares implied by the MA weights. The demo's \([0.8002,0.1998]\) for the second variable is that limit, not a property of the data alone.

    Granger causality #

    For the equation of variable \(e\), the unrestricted residual sum of squares is \(SSE_u\). The restricted model drops the \(p\) lag blocks of variable \(c\) and refits, giving \(SSE_r\). Under the null that those blocks are zero,

    \[F=\frac{(SSE_r-SSE_u)/p}{SSE_u/\nu},\qquad \nu=n_0-d, \qquad (7)\]

    and \(F\) has an \(F(p,\nu)\) distribution. The code builds the restricted design by deleting the relevant columns from the already-stacked design_, so exogenous columns and all other lags stay in both models. Rejecting (7) says the lags help prediction; it does not say that changing \(c\) would change \(e\).

    Ridge #

    For large \(k\) and \(p\), \(d=1+kp\) can approach \(n_0\) and OLS becomes unstable. var_ridge minimises

    \[\lVert Y-ZB\rVert_F^2+\lambda\tau\lVert B_{[1:]}\rVert_F^2,\qquad \tau=\frac{\mathrm{tr}(Z^\top Z)}{d}, \qquad (8)\]

    where \(B_{[1:]}\) excludes the intercept row. The normal equations are \((Z^\top Z+\lambda\tau I)B=Z^\top Y\) with the \((1,1)\) penalty entry zeroed, which is the shrink[0, 0] = 0.0 line in the code. Scaling by \(\tau\) makes \(\lambda\) dimensionless: \(\lambda=1\) shrinks towards zero by an amount comparable to the average curvature of the loss.

    Exogenous regressors #

    varx extends (1) to

    \[Y_t=c+\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_iY_{t-i}+Bx_t+u_t, \qquad (9)\]

    with \(x_t\in\mathbb R^m\) and \(B\in\mathbb R^{m\times k}\). Estimation is unchanged — \(x_t\) is simply more columns of \(Z\) — and forecasting requires a future path for \(x\). The demo checks the first forecast against the manual expression \(c+\hat A y_T+\hat Bx_{T+1}\).

    Likelihood and information criteria #

    With \(\hat\Sigma_{MLE}=\hat U^\top\hat U/n_0\), the Gaussian log-likelihood is

    \[\ell=-\frac{n_0}{2}\Bigl(k\log2\pi+\log\lvert\hat\Sigma_{MLE}\rvert+k\Bigr), \qquad (10)\]

    and \(\mathrm{AIC}=-2\ell+2k d\), \(\mathrm{BIC}=-2\ell+kd\log n_0\). The factor \(k\) multiplies the parameter count because each of the \(d\) regressors has one coefficient per equation. The demo's VAR(2) has \(\mathrm{AIC}=2765.92\); the number is comparable across lag orders of the same variables, which is how lag length is normally chosen.

    Code walkthrough #

    Everything begins by stacking the lag blocks. Adding the intercept first and each lag in order means the coefficient slice for lag \(i\) is contiguous:

            rows = np.arange(p, n)
        blocks = [np.ones((rows.size, 1))]
        for lag in range(1, p + 1):
            blocks.append(Y[rows - lag])
        if exog is not None:
            blocks.append(exog[rows])
        design = np.column_stack(blocks)
        target = Y[rows]
        penalty = 0.0 if self.variant != "var_ridge" else self.ridge
        if penalty > 0.0:
            gram = design.T @ design
            scale = np.trace(gram) / gram.shape[0]
            shrink = np.eye(gram.shape[0]) * penalty * scale
            shrink[0, 0] = 0.0
            beta = np.linalg.solve(gram + shrink, design.T @ target)
        else:
            beta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(design, target, rcond=None)

    Y[rows - lag] is the lagged matrix aligned with target, so no shifting is needed later. The ridge branch solves the normal equations directly instead of lstsq, because the penalty is a modification of the normal equations rather than of the design; shrink[0, 0] = 0.0 keeps the intercept free. With penalty == 0 the two branches coincide, which the demo checks.

    The flat coefficient matrix is then folded back into a stack of \(k\times k\) matrices, transposing because beta stores one column per equation:

            self.coef_ = np.empty((p, k, k))
        for lag in range(p):
            self.coef_[lag] = beta[1 + lag * k:1 + (lag + 1) * k].T

    The transpose is the step that is easy to get wrong: row \(r\) of beta holds regressor \(r\)'s coefficients for all equations, whereas coef_[lag][a, b] must mean "effect of variable \(b\) at lag \(lag+1\) on variable \(a\)".

    The companion matrix is assembled from blocks, with identity blocks below the diagonal:

        def _companion(self) -> np.ndarray:
        k, p = self.k_, self.p_
        top = np.column_stack([self.coef_[lag] for lag in range(p)])
        if p == 1:
            return top
        lower = np.zeros((k * (p - 1), k * p))
        for lag in range(p - 1):
            lower[lag * k:(lag + 1) * k, lag * k:(lag + 1) * k] = np.eye(k)
        return np.vstack([top, lower])

    This is (3) written block by block. The p == 1 shortcut avoids allocating a \(k(p-1)\times kp\) zero block with zero rows for the most common case.

    Forecasting shifts a rolling window of the last \(p\) vectors. The stored history is reversed, so lag \(1\) is entry \(0\):

            history = [self.history_[lag - 1] for lag in range(1, self.p_ + 1)]
        out = np.empty((horizon, self.k_))
        for h in range(horizon):
            value = self.intercept_.copy()
            for lag, past in enumerate(history):
                value = value + self.coef_[lag] @ past
            if self.exog_coef_ is not None:
                value = value + self.exog_coef_.T @ exog[h]
            out[h] = value
            history = [value] + history[:-1]

    history = [value] + history[:-1] slides the window without copying the whole path, and the exog term is added at each step from the supplied future values. The coefficient @ past orientation matches (1): the coefficient matrix acts on a column vector.

    The MA weights are the same recursion with an identity seed:

            k = self.k_
        weights = np.zeros((horizon, k, k))
        weights[0] = np.eye(k)
        for h in range(1, horizon):
            value = np.zeros((k, k))
            for lag in range(1, min(self.p_, h) + 1):
                value += self.coef_[lag - 1] @ weights[h - lag]
            weights[h] = value
        return weights

    This is (4). The min(self.p_, h) bound is what stops the recursion from reaching for lags that do not exist yet; for \(h the missing terms are zero by definition.

    Orthogonalisation is one matrix factor and one multiplication:

        def irf(self, horizon: int) -> np.ndarray:
        """Orthogonalised impulse responses, shape `(horizon, k, k)`."""
        self._check_fitted()
        weights = self.ma_coefficients(horizon)
        shock = np.linalg.cholesky(self.sigma_)
        return weights @ shock

    np.linalg.cholesky returns the lower-triangular \(P\) of (5), so weights @ shock right-multiplies every horizon's \(\Psi_h\) by \(P\). The first column of the result is the response to a shock in the first variable, which is why variable order matters.

    The variance decomposition is a cumulative sum of squares followed by a row-normalisation:

            responses = self.irf(horizon)
        cumulative = np.cumsum(responses ** 2, axis=0)
        total = cumulative.sum(axis=2, keepdims=True)
        return cumulative / total

    axis=0 accumulates over horizons, axis=2 sums over shocks, and keepdims makes the division broadcast against the \((k,k)\) share matrix at every horizon. This is (6) with the \(H\)-th entry containing the first \(H\) terms.

    Granger's restricted fit reuses the full design and deletes columns:

            dropped = [1 + (lag - 1) * k + cause for lag in range(1, p + 1)]
        keep = [c for c in range(self.design_.shape[1]) if c not in dropped]
        target = self.fitted_ + self.residuals_
        subset = self.design_[:, keep]
        restricted, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(subset, target[:, effect], rcond=None)
        residual = target[:, effect] - subset @ restricted
        sse_r = float(residual @ residual)

    The column formula follows from the layout: column \(0\) is the intercept, then lag \(1\)'s \(k\) variables, and variable cause sits at offset cause inside each block. target = fitted_ + residuals_ reconstructs \(Y\) on the estimation rows without storing it separately, and the unrestricted SSE comes from the residuals already in the model. Deleting columns rather than rebuilding the design is what keeps exogenous regressors in the restricted model automatically.

    Complexity #

    With \(n\) observations, \(k\) variables, \(p\) lags, \(m\) exogenous columns and horizon \(H\):

    • Design: \(O(n(kp+m))\) memory and time to build.
    • Fit: one least squares on an \(n\times d\) design with \(k\) responses, \(O(nd^2+nkd)\); the ridge branch solves a \(d\times d\) system, \(O(d^3)\).
    • Companion eigenvalues: \(O((kp)^3)\).
    • Forecast: \(O(Hk^2p)\) for \(k\) equations, since each step multiplies \(p\) matrices by vectors.
    • MA weights and IRF: \(O(Hkp\,k^2)=O(Hpk^3)\) in the worst case, because every step multiplies \(k\times k\) matrices.
    • Granger test: one restricted least squares per ordered pair, so \(O(k^2nd^2)\) if all pairs are computed.
    • FEVD: \(O(Hk^3)\) after the IRF.

    The demo's largest job is the 400-point bivariate VAR(2) fit, which is three least-squares calls on a \(498\times5\) design; the \(F\)-tests dominate nothing. Memory is \(O(nd)\) for the design, which is the price of keeping it for the diagnostics.

    Failure modes #

    Granger causality is not causation. The test asks whether lags of \(x\) improve the forecast of \(y\) given lags of \(y\) and the other variables. A common driver, a policy reaction function, or an anticipation effect can all produce a significant \(F\) with no intervention behind it. The demo's null case is clean because the data were simulated with an exact zero; real data rarely are.

    The Cholesky ordering is an assumption. fevd and irf depend on the column order of \(Y\), because \(P\) is triangular. On the demo's data, reversing the columns moves the second variable's own share from 0.20 to 0.33. The demo's \([0.8002,0.1998]\) is a statement about the ordering as much as about the dynamics, which is why it prints that caveat next to the number. Structural VARs replace the Cholesky factor with an identified impact matrix, and that is a different modelling exercise.

    Spurious stability. A fitted companion matrix can look stable while one eigenvalue is \(0.99\), which produces forecasts that look fine for five steps and then explode. Check \(\rho(F)\) against the horizon you actually need, not just against one. A near-unit root also makes the Granger tests liberal, because the asymptotic \(F\) approximation assumes stationarity.

    Lag length eats the sample. A VAR(\(p\)) with \(k\) variables spends \(1+kp\) degrees of freedom per equation. With \(k=5\) and \(p=4\) that is 21 parameters per equation, so a 100-point series leaves 79 residual degrees of freedom and very wide confidence bands. Ridge helps the variance but biases every coefficient towards zero, including the ones that are real; the demo's norm drop from 0.870 to 0.494 is a warning, not a recommendation.

    Exogenous forecasts are not free. varx conditions on a future path for \(x\), and its intervals ignore the uncertainty in that path. A scenario forecast built on an assumed policy rate is a conditional statement, not a predictive distribution.

    Correlated residuals are not a modelling error. They are the normal case, and they are exactly why the reduced-form IRF needs orthogonalisation. The mistake is to read the orthogonalised result as if the data had identified the ordering.

    Applications #

    VARs are the standard workhorse of empirical macroeconomics. Sims (1980) argued that a small system of macroeconomic aggregates, estimated with few restrictions, forecasts better and says more than the large structural models then in use, and the VAR-plus-IRF workflow has been the default ever since. Central banks use them to trace the response of output and inflation to a policy shock, and the variance decomposition is a standard way to ask how much of a variable's movement is attributable to each shock.

    Granger tests are used well beyond economics: in neuroscience, lagged predictability between brain regions is a common connectivity screen; in genomics, it is applied to expression time series; in industrial forecasting, it is a cheap way to decide whether a downstream sensor belongs in a model. The ridge variant matters when the system is wide — dozens of series, a few hundred observations — which is now common in macro nowcasting and in sensor networks.

    The model is the wrong tool when the relationships are nonlinear, when the series have structural breaks, or when the question is explicitly about a counterfactual intervention. In those cases a VAR is a baseline and a diagnostic, not the final answer.
