    Croston — forecasting demand that mostly is not there

    Intuition #

    Most forecasting methods assume a value arrives every period. Intermittent demand breaks that assumption: a spare part sells on 8% of days, a repair kit is ordered a few times a year, a call centre receives nothing for hours and then three calls at once. Applying exponential smoothing directly to such a series gives a forecast that is too high on most days and too low on the days that matter, and the errors are dominated by the zeros.

    Croston's insight was to stop forecasting the series and start forecasting its two ingredients. Demand has a size when it occurs and an interval between occurrences. Smooth each one separately, then forecast the rate as size divided by interval. A long gap lowers the rate through the interval estimate rather than through a zero that drags an ordinary smoother down.

    The variants fix different weaknesses of that recipe:

    • croston is the original: smooth sizes and inter-arrival gaps, forecast size over interval.
    • sba applies the Syntetos–Boylan correction, multiplying the rate by \(1-\alpha/2\) to remove Croston's small positive bias.
    • tsb smooths the probability of a demand every period and the size only at arrivals. Because the probability decays during a long gap, TSB can forecast obsolescence; Croston cannot.
    • optimized_croston fits the smoothing parameter by minimising the one-step sum of squares instead of using a fixed value.

    The demo measures all four on simulated intermittent demand: a 600-period series with 115 demands, a regime change in the demand probability, and a Poisson interval whose coverage is checked by simulation.

    variant state demo result

    sba Croston with \(1-\alpha/2\) rate 0.9061, distance 0.0156
    tsb probability and size MAE 0.5219 vs 0.6603

    croston sees 115 demands in 600 periods, with an average size of \(4.6951\) and an average smoothed interval of \(4.9228\). The implied rate is \(0.9537\) against an empirical mean of \(0.9217\) and a true rate of \(1.00\). The estimate is noisy because the interval state reacts slowly to a long gap at the end of the sample; with \(\alpha=0.1\) the last gap carries a lot of weight.

    sba multiplies the rate by \(1-\alpha/2=0.95\), giving \(0.9061\). Its distance to the empirical mean is \(0.0156\) against \(0.0321\) for plain Croston. That is the bias correction working on this sample, but it is not a guarantee: the correction is derived for a stationary process with a known \(\alpha\), and a single series can go either way.

    tsb is fitted to a series whose demand probability drops from 35% to 10% halfway through. Its probability state ends at \(0.0172\) after a long stretch without a demand, so its terminal rate (\(0.0678\)) is far below the trailing 100-period mean (\(0.2600\)). The terminal rate is not the right comparison: the one-step MAE over the last 100 periods is \(0.5219\) against \(0.6603\) for Croston. TSB tracks the new regime faster and also decays toward zero when demand stops altogether.

    optimized_croston scans \(\alpha\) on a grid and chooses \(0.32\), lowering the one-step SSE from \(1176.96\) at \(\alpha=0.1\) to \(1042.56\). The rate moves from \(0.4177\) to \(0.3819\), closer to the post-change local mean. The fitted \(\alpha\) is a one-step-optimal value; it need not be optimal for a 12-step forecast.

    Derivation #

    The demand process #

    Write the series as a marked point process:

    \[Y_t=Z_t\,\mathbf 1[A_t], \qquad (1)\]

    where \(A_t\) is the event that a demand arrives at \(t\) and \(Z_t>0\) is its size. The forecast target is the rate \(\lambda=\mathbb E[Y_t]=\Pr(A_t)\,\mathbb E[Z_t\mid A_t]\). The two factors are estimated separately because they carry different information: sizes are observed only on arrival, and the interval is observed only as elapsed time.

    Croston's recursions #

    Let \(z_t\) be the smoothed size and \(p_t\) the smoothed interval. At an arrival,

    \[z_t=\alpha Y_t+(1-\alpha)z_{t-1}, \qquad (2)\]

    and the interval is updated with the gap \(q_t\) since the previous arrival:

    \[p_t=\alpha q_t+(1-\alpha)p_{t-1}. \qquad (3)\]

    On a zero period nothing is updated except the gap counter \(q_t\leftarrow q_t+1\); the size and interval stay where they were. After the last observation the forecast is flat:

    \[\hat Y_{T+h}=\frac{z_T}{p_T},\qquad h=1,\dots,H. \qquad (4)\]

    The flat forecast is deliberate. Croston models a rate, not a path; there is no trend or seasonal term to extrapolate, and allowing the rate to drift would invent structure the data do not support.

    The bias and the SBA correction #

    The ratio \(z/p\) is a biased estimator of \(z/p\)'s population counterpart. For independent size and interval with means \(\mu_z,\mu_p\) and interval coefficient of variation \(\mathrm{CV}_p\),

    \[\mathbb E\!\left[\frac{z}{p}\right] \approx\frac{\mu_z}{\mu_p} \bigl(1+\mathrm{CV}_p^2\bigr), \qquad (5)\]

    so the estimate is too high, and the bias grows with the variability of the intervals. Syntetos and Boylan proposed the correction

    \[\hat Y^{\mathrm{SBA}}_{T+h} =\Bigl(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Bigr)\frac{z_T}{p_T}, \qquad (6)\]

    which is the leading term of the inverse for a smoothing parameter \(\alpha\). It is an approximation, not an exact unbiasedness result: it assumes stationarity and a particular relationship between \(\alpha\) and the interval variability.

    TSB #

    Teunter, Syntetos and Babai replace the interval state with a probability that is updated every period. Let \(\pi_t\) be the probability of a demand at \(t\) and \(z_t\) the size:

    \[\pi_t=\alpha\,\mathbf 1[Y_t>0]+(1-\alpha)\pi_{t-1}, \qquad (7)\]
    \[z_t= \begin{cases} \beta Y_t+(1-\beta)z_{t-1}, & Y_t>0,\\ z_{t-1}, & Y_t=0, \end{cases} \qquad (8)\]

    and the forecast is

    \[\hat Y_{T+h}=\pi_T\,z_T . \qquad (9)\]

    The difference from Croston is visible in (7)–(8): the probability is smoothed on every observation, so a run of zeros drives \(\pi\) toward zero, while the size is only revised when a demand arrives. That is what makes TSB able to forecast obsolescence. The price is that the probability reacts to every zero, so a single unusual gap can push the forecast down quickly.

    Fitting alpha #

    For optimized_croston, \(\alpha\) is chosen by minimising the one-step sum of squares:

    \[\hat\alpha=\arg\min_{\alpha\in(0,1]} \sum_{t=\text{burn}+1}^{n} \bigl(Y_t-\hat Y_t(\alpha)\bigr)^2, \qquad (10)\]

    where \(\hat Y_t(\alpha)\) is the forecast made before \(Y_t\) is observed. The first few residuals are excluded because the state is initialised from the first demand and is not meaningful before it. The search is a grid of 49 values from \(0.02\) to \(0.98\); the objective is not smooth in \(\alpha\) because the gap counter is integer-valued, so a grid is more reliable than a gradient.

    Poisson intervals #

    Conditional on the rate \(\lambda\), the number of demands in \(h\) periods is

    \[S_h\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(h\lambda). \qquad (11)\]

    The interval reports the smallest \(k\) with \(\Pr(S_h\le k)\ge q\), and the analogous upper quantile, where \(q=(1-\text{level})/2\). This treats demand occurrences as Poisson and ignores the variability of the sizes: a lumpy series with occasional very large orders has a much wider total-demand distribution than (11) implies. Use the interval as a count model, not as a full predictive distribution for revenue.

    Code walkthrough #

    The Croston engine keeps three pieces of state: the smoothed size, the smoothed interval, and the gap since the last demand. The forecast is written before the update, so fitted_[t] is a genuine one-step-ahead prediction:

        for t in range(first + 1, n):
        fitted[t] = size / interval
        if y[t] > 0.0:
            size = alpha * y[t] + (1.0 - alpha) * size
            interval = alpha * gap + (1.0 - alpha) * interval
            gap = 1.0
        else:
            gap += 1.0

    The initialisation sets size to the first positive demand and interval to one. That is arbitrary but harmless for the fit, because the first first + 1 residuals are excluded from the sum of squares. Using a future mean of the positive demands would leak information into the forecast and flatter the in-sample error.

    TSB shares the skeleton but updates the probability on every period. Only the size is updated at arrivals, which is what lets the probability decay during a gap:

        for t in range(first + 1, n):
        fitted[t] = probability * size
        if y[t] > 0.0:
            probability = alpha + (1.0 - alpha) * probability
            size = beta * y[t] + (1.0 - beta) * size
        else:
            probability = (1.0 - alpha) * probability

    The initial probability of \(0.5\) is a weak prior, not an estimate. With 600 observations its influence is negligible, but on a short series it matters; in a production system it should be set from the known demand frequency or estimated from a longer history.

    The optimised variant scans alpha and keeps the best sum of squares. The candidate list is stored so that the demo, or a user, can see how flat the objective is:

                candidates = []
            best = None
            for alpha in np.arange(0.02, 0.99, 0.02):
                result = _croston(y, float(alpha))
                candidates.append((float(alpha), result["sse"]))
                if best is None or result["sse"] < best["sse"]:
                    best = result
                    best_alpha = float(alpha)

    The SBA branch is the same engine with a correction factor. Keeping the uncorrected size_ and interval_ on the object means the components can be inspected even when the reported rate is corrected:

            else:
            correction = 1.0 if self.variant == "croston" else 1.0 - 0.5 * self.alpha
            best = _croston(y, self.alpha, correction)
            self.alpha_ = self.alpha
            self.candidates_ = [(self.alpha, best["sse"])]

    The correction multiplies only the final rate, not the size or interval states; applying it inside the recursion would double-correct every update.

    Prediction is intentionally trivial. The method forecasts a rate, and the rate does not depend on the horizon:

        def predict(self, horizon: int = 12) -> np.ndarray:
        """Return the constant per-period rate for each step."""
        if not hasattr(self, "rate_"):
            raise RuntimeError("fit the model before predicting")
        if not isinstance(horizon, (int, np.integer)) or horizon < 1:
            raise ValueError("horizon must be a positive integer")
        return np.full(int(horizon), self.rate_)

    Returning an array rather than a scalar keeps the interface uniform with the other forecasters in this chapter, and makes it explicit that every horizon gets the same number. A user who wants a cumulative forecast should sum the array, not expect the method to grow.

    The Poisson quantile is computed by summing probabilities until the target is reached. The loop is bounded so that a pathological mean cannot hang the program:

    def _poisson_quantile(mean: float, probability: float) -> int:
    """Smallest k with P(Poisson(mean) <= k) >= probability."""
    if mean <= 0.0:
        return 0
    term = float(np.exp(-mean))
    total = term
    value = 0
    while total < probability and value < 100000:
        value += 1
        term *= mean / value
        total += term
    return value

    The recurrence term *= mean / value computes each Poisson probability from the previous one, which avoids a factorial that would overflow for even moderate counts. The cap of 100000 is far above any realistic demand count and exists only to make the termination property obvious.

    The interval applies the quantile to the cumulative mean at each horizon. Because the mean grows with \(h\), both bounds are non-decreasing:

            tail = 0.5 * (1.0 - level)
        means = self.rate_ * np.arange(1, int(horizon) + 1)
        lower = np.array([_poisson_quantile(mean, tail) for mean in means])
        upper = np.array([_poisson_quantile(mean, 1.0 - tail) for mean in means])
        return lower, upper

    At \(h=1\) with a rate near one, the 90% interval is \([0,3]\), which covers more than 90% because the Poisson is discrete and skewed. That conservatism is visible in the demo: the simulated coverage starts at \(0.9835\) and settles near \(0.9235\) by \(h=12\).

    Complexity #

    With \(n\) observations, \(G\) alpha candidates and \(H\) forecast steps:

    • One Croston or TSB pass: \(O(n)\) time and \(O(n)\) memory for the fitted values and residuals.
    • Optimised fit: \(O(Gn)\) with \(G=49\) in the demo; a grid search over two parameters would be \(O(G^2n)\), which is why TSB fixes beta to alpha when it is not supplied.
    • Prediction: \(O(H)\) to build the constant array, or \(O(1)\) for a scalar.
    • Interval: \(O(H\bar k)\) for the cumulative sums, where \(\bar k\) is the largest quantile; at a rate near one that is a handful of terms per step.
    • Memory: \(O(n)\) for fitted_ and residuals_; none of the states grow with \(n\).

    The demo's four fits on 600 points run in a few hundredths of a second. The method is cheap enough to refit per SKU in a nightly batch, which is how it is usually deployed.

    Failure modes #

    A zero is data, not a missing value. Croston and TSB interpret every zero as "no demand this period". If a zero actually means "the product was out of stock" or "the feed was down", the model learns a demand rate that is too low and the bias propagates into safety stock. Distinguish censored periods from genuine zeros before fitting, and treat stockouts as missing observations.

    Croston is slow to react to a change in frequency. The interval state is only updated at arrivals, so it takes several arrivals to reflect a new regime. In the demo's regime change, Croston's terminal rate is \(0.4177\) against a trailing mean of \(0.2600\). TSB reacts faster because it updates the probability every period, which is why TSB is preferred when demand may be disappearing.

    TSB overreacts to a long gap. The mirror image of the previous point: a run of zeros multiplies the probability by \((1-\alpha)\) each period, so the terminal rate can be near zero even when the long-run rate is not. In the demo, TSB ends at \(0.0678\) while the last 100 periods average \(0.2600\). Judge the method by one-step accuracy over the recent window, not by the terminal number alone.

    The SBA correction assumes stationarity. The factor \(1-\alpha/2\) is derived for a stable demand process with a fixed smoothing parameter. Under a regime change, or when \(\alpha\) was chosen for a different regime, it can move the forecast away from the truth. It is a small correction, not a substitute for modelling the change.

    Optimised alpha overfits one-step errors. The grid chooses the value that minimises the sum of squared one-step errors. That is not the same as the value that minimises the error of a 12-step cumulative forecast, and on a short series the selected \(\alpha\) can be unstable. Validate on a rolling origin, and prefer a value from a neighbourhood of the optimum rather than a sharp minimum.

    The Poisson interval ignores size variability. Equation (11) models the number of arrivals, not the total size. A series with occasional orders of hundreds of units has a heavy-tailed total-demand distribution that a Poisson count interval understates badly. For inventory, bootstrap the sizes or use a compound Poisson model; the count interval is only a lower bound on the uncertainty.

    The flat forecast is an assumption. Croston and TSB produce the same rate for every horizon. If the demand rate is trending, or has a seasonal pattern (e.g. heating spares), the flat forecast is biased at long horizons. Deseasonalise or detrend first, forecast the adjusted rate, and add the structure back.

    Applications #

    Croston's method is standard in spare-parts and slow-moving inventory management. Airlines, utilities and heavy-equipment manufacturers use it to forecast the demand rate for components that fail rarely but expensively, and to set reorder points from the resulting rate and interval. The SBA variant is the default in several commercial inventory packages because the positive bias of plain Croston translates directly into excess safety stock.

    TSB is used where obsolescence matters: end-of-life electronics, fashion accessories, and any SKU whose demand can stop permanently. Its ability to drive the probability toward zero is the reason it is preferred to Croston for product-life-cycle forecasting. The same machinery applies to call arrivals in a service centre, insurance claims with a low frequency and a heavy size tail, and failure counts in reliability engineering.

    The method is the wrong tool when demand is dense enough for ordinary exponential smoothing, when the sizes carry a trend, or when the arrivals are strongly seasonal. It is also not a model of the size distribution: for revenue forecasting, the size tail matters and a compound model is needed. Use Croston as the rate forecast and model the size distribution separately.
