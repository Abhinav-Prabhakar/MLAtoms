    11 · Time series/tier 2/5 variants/source

    Spectral analysis — five ways to ask what a signal is made of

    Intuition #

    A time series is a sum of oscillations. Spectral analysis turns that sentence into arithmetic: project the signal onto sinusoids of every frequency and see how much energy each one carries. The projection is the discrete Fourier transform, and its squared magnitude is the periodogram.

    The periodogram is the right idea with a practical defect. Each frequency bin is a single complex number, so its squared magnitude is an exponentially distributed random variable: noisy, and no less noisy as the series grows longer. Adding data adds bins, not precision. Every other variant in this file is a way of buying variance reduction without giving up too much frequency resolution:

    • periodogram is the raw estimate. Best resolution, worst variance, and heavy leakage from strong low-frequency components.
    • welch cuts the series into overlapping segments, computes a periodogram of each, and averages. Variance falls roughly as \(1/K\); resolution falls to \(f_s/L\) for segment length \(L\).
    • multitaper keeps the full length but multiplies it by several orthogonal tapers and averages the results. It reduces leakage rather than resolution, which is why it finds a weak tone next to a strong one.
    • lomb_scargle fits sinusoids directly at the observation times, so it works when samples are unevenly spaced and the FFT grid does not exist.
    • stft_features keeps time as well as frequency: it transforms short frames and summarises each frame by centroid, bandwidth, rolloff and flatness, which is what a downstream model actually consumes.

    The demo checks the two properties that separate a working implementation from a plausible-looking one: the integral of the spectrum must recover the signal variance, and the peak must land within a bin or two of the planted frequency. It then measures the leakage that the multitaper is supposed to fix.

    periodogram on 1 024 samples with tones at \(0.10\) and \(0.23\) cycles per sample puts its maximum at \(0.0996\), one bin below the truth, with resolution \(0.00098\). The integral of the one-sided density is \(2.4251\) against a signal variance of \(2.3556\) — a 2.9% overshoot from the finite-sample noise and the DC/Nyquist half-bins. That is the estimator working correctly, and also the estimator being noisy.

    welch uses 31 half-overlapping segments of 512 samples from an 8 192-point series. Averaging cuts the bin-to-bin scatter enough that the peak is clean at \(0.0996\), and the integral \(2.3773\) against \(2.3708\) is within 0.3%. The price is resolution: \(0.00195\), twice the periodogram's, so two tones closer than about \(0.002\) cycles per sample would merge into one hump.

    multitaper builds seven discrete prolate spheroidal sequences at time-bandwidth product \(NW=4\). The first taper concentrates \(1.000000\) of its power inside \(|f|, and the tapers are orthonormal to machine precision. On a signal with a tone ten times stronger at \(0.01\), the power at \(0.2\) over the local floor is \(838.0\) for the periodogram and \(79.2\) for the multitaper: the leakage from the strong tone has been suppressed by a factor of ten.

    lomb_scargle receives 300 samples at irregular times over 60 seconds and finds the planted \(1.2\) Hz tone at \(1.1998\), with a grid spacing of \(0.00210\) and 1 906 candidate frequencies. The peak height \(0.8551\) is not one because the normalisation is against total variance, part of which is noise; the location, not the height, is the estimate of interest.

    stft_features tracks a chirp that sweeps from 2 Hz to 7 Hz. With 128-sample frames at a 50 Hz sampling rate it produces a \(12\times65\) spectrogram, a centroid that rises from \(2.640\) to \(6.160\) Hz, and an 85% rolloff that rises from \(2.734\) to \(6.250\) Hz. The mean flatness prints as \(0.00000\): a clean chirp is tonal, so its geometric mean is many orders of magnitude below its arithmetic mean.

    Derivation #

    The discrete Fourier transform #

    For samples \(x_0,\dots,x_{n-1}\) and a window \(w_t\), the windowed transform is

    \[X_k=\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}w_t\,x_t\,e^{-i2\pi kt/n}, \qquad k=0,\dots,n-1 . \qquad (1)\]

    The frequency attached to bin \(k\) is \(f_k=kf_s/n\), so neighbouring bins are \(\Delta f=f_s/n\) apart and the highest representable frequency is \(f_s/2\). The transform is a change of basis, not a model: it loses nothing and assumes nothing beyond finite sampling.

    The periodogram #

    The periodogram estimates the spectral density by

    \[\hat S(f_k)=\frac{\lvert X_k\rvert^2}{f_s\sum_{t}w_t^2}, \qquad (2)\]

    with the non-DC, non-Nyquist bins doubled to fold negative frequencies onto the one-sided axis. The denominator is the window energy, which is \(n\) for a rectangular window and roughly \(3n/8\) for a Hann window. That normalisation is what makes the integral recover the variance:

    \[\int_0^{f_s/2}\hat S(f)\,df\;\approx\;\frac1n\sum_t (x_t-\bar x)^2 . \qquad (3)\]

    The demo checks (3) numerically. It is the first thing to test, because a factor-of-two error in the one-sided folding is invisible in a plot and wrong by 3 dB in every downstream number.

    The defect is in the sampling distribution. For a Gaussian process,

    \[\mathrm{Var}\bigl(\hat S(f)\bigr)\approx S(f)^2 \qquad (4)\]

    at every frequency, independent of \(n\). The estimate is asymptotically unbiased but not consistent: more data gives a finer grid of equally noisy points. Smoothing over neighbouring bins, averaging over segments or averaging over tapers are the three ways to reduce (4).

    A finite record also convolves the true spectrum with the window's spectral window. A rectangular window has sidelobes that decay slowly, so a tone at \(0.01\) with amplitude ten leaks across the whole band; a Hann window trades a wider main lobe for sidelobes below \(-31\) dB. This is the leakage the demo measures.

    Welch averaging #

    Split the series into \(K\) segments of length \(L\), overlapping by \(L/2\), and average their periodograms:

    \[\hat S_W(f)=\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat S_k(f). \qquad (5)\]

    If the segments were independent, (4) would give \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat S_W)\approx S^2/K\). Overlapping segments are correlated, so the gain is smaller than \(1/K\); half overlap is the usual compromise because it doubles the segment count for a modest correlation penalty. Resolution is now \(f_s/L\), not \(f_s/n\): averaging buys variance with bias, and the bias is a blurred peak.

    Multitaper estimation #

    Instead of one window, use \(K\) orthogonal windows \(v_t^{(j)}\) and average their periodograms. The tapers are chosen to maximise the fraction of their energy inside a bandwidth \([-W,W]\):

    \[\lambda=\frac{\int_{-W}^{W}\lvert V(f)\rvert^2\,df} {\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\lvert V(f)\rvert^2\,df}. \qquad (6)\]

    The maximiser solves a symmetric tridiagonal eigenproblem. With \(W=NW/n\) and \(t=0,\dots,n-1\),

    \[A_{tt}=\Bigl(\frac{n-1}{2}-t\Bigr)^2\cos(2\pi W),\qquad A_{t,t+1}=A_{t+1,t}=\frac{t(n-t)}{2}, \qquad (7)\]

    and the leading \(2NW-1\) eigenvectors are the discrete prolate spheroidal sequences. Each has most of its energy in the band, so each produces a periodogram with small leakage, and the estimates are approximately independent because the tapers are orthogonal. The multitaper estimate is

    \[\hat S_M(f)=\frac1K\sum_{j=1}^{K} \frac{\lvert\sum_t v_t^{(j)}x_t e^{-i2\pi ft}\rvert^2} {f_s\sum_t (v_t^{(j)})^2}. \qquad (8)\]

    The demo checks both halves of the claim: the tapers are orthonormal, the first one concentrates essentially all its power in the band, and the leakage floor next to a strong tone is an order of magnitude lower than the periodogram's.

    Lomb-Scargle #

    When the observation times \(t_i\) are irregular, there is no uniform grid and no FFT. Fit a sinusoid at frequency \(f\) by least squares instead, allowing a phase shift \(\tau\) chosen so the sine and cosine bases are orthogonal:

    \[\hat x(t)=a\cos\bigl(2\pi f(t-\tau)\bigr)+b\sin\bigl(2\pi f(t-\tau)\bigr), \qquad \tan(4\pi f\tau)=\frac{\sum_i\sin(4\pi ft_i)}{\sum_i\cos(4\pi ft_i)} . \qquad (9)\]

    For fixed \(f\) the two coefficients separate, and the normalised power is

    \[P(f)=\frac12\left[ \frac{\bigl(\sum_i x_i\cos 2\pi f(t_i-\tau)\bigr)^2} {\sum_i\cos^2 2\pi f(t_i-\tau)} + \frac{\bigl(\sum_i x_i\sin 2\pi f(t_i-\tau)\bigr)^2} {\sum_i\sin^2 2\pi f(t_i-\tau)} \right]\Big/\Bigl(\tfrac12 n\hat\sigma^2\Bigr). \qquad (10)\]

    The code evaluates (10) on a grid with spacing \(1/(\text{oversample}\cdot \text{span})\), which is the natural resolution of a record of that length. The default upper limit is the Nyquist frequency implied by the median spacing; for irregular sampling that is a guide, not a hard boundary, and aliases can appear above it.

    Short-time features #

    The STFT applies (1) to short frames:

    \[X_{m,k}=\sum_{t=0}^{L-1}w_t\,x_{mH+t}\,e^{-i2\pi kt/L}, \qquad P_{m,k}=\frac{\lvert X_{m,k}\rvert^2}{f_s\sum_t w_t^2}, \qquad (11)\]

    where \(H\) is the hop. Frame length is the time-frequency trade-off in its purest form: a long frame resolves close frequencies but smears events in time, a short frame does the reverse. The demo uses 128 samples at 50 Hz — 2.56 s per frame — and a 32-sample hop.

    Each frame is then reduced to four numbers. The spectral centroid is the power-weighted mean frequency,

    \[c_m=\frac{\sum_k f_k P_{m,k}}{\sum_k P_{m,k}}, \qquad (12)\]

    and the bandwidth is the matching standard deviation,

    \[b_m=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_k (f_k-c_m)^2P_{m,k}}{\sum_k P_{m,k}}} . \qquad (13)\]

    The rolloff is the smallest \(f_k\) whose cumulative power reaches 85% of the frame total, a robust alternative to the maximum. Flatness is the ratio of the geometric to the arithmetic mean,

    \[\phi_m=\frac{\exp\bigl(\frac1K\sum_k\log(P_{m,k}+\epsilon)\bigr)} {\frac1K\sum_k P_{m,k}}, \qquad (14)\]

    which is near one for white noise and near zero for a tone. The \(\epsilon\) guards the logarithm of an empty bin; without it a silent frame produces \(-\infty\) and the whole feature vector becomes unusable.

    Code walkthrough #

    All five variants share one normalisation. The window function is where the leakage trade-off is made, and it is deliberately a small table rather than a general window factory:

        grid = np.arange(n)
    if name in ("boxcar", "rectangular", "none"):
        return np.ones(n)
    if name == "hann":
        return 0.5 - 0.5 * np.cos(2.0 * np.pi * grid / n)
    if name == "hamming":
        return 0.54 - 0.46 * np.cos(2.0 * np.pi * grid / n)
    if name == "blackman":
        return (0.42 - 0.5 * np.cos(2.0 * np.pi * grid / n)
                + 0.08 * np.cos(4.0 * np.pi * grid / n))

    The three cosine windows differ only in how much weight they put on the ends. Hann reaches zero at both ends, which removes the discontinuity that causes leakage; Blackman adds a second harmonic of the cosine to push the sidelobes lower still, at the cost of a wider main lobe.

    The shared density calculation implements (2), including the one-sided doubling. The nfft % 2 branch is the detail that is easy to get wrong: for an even transform length the Nyquist bin is its own mirror image and must not be doubled, while for an odd length every bin above DC has a partner:

        x = x - x.mean()
    window = _window(name, x.size)
    transform = np.fft.rfft(x * window, n=nfft)
    power = np.abs(transform) ** 2 / (fs * float(window @ window))
    if nfft % 2 == 0:
        power[1:-1] *= 2.0
    else:
        power[1:] *= 2.0
    return np.fft.rfftfreq(nfft, 1.0 / fs), power

    The mean is removed before the transform, so the DC bin carries only the residual and the integral in (3) is the variance rather than the second moment. Note that nfft may exceed the segment length: zero padding interpolates the spectrum onto a finer grid, but it does not add resolution.

    The DPSS construction is (7) written as three diagonals. Building the full matrix is wasteful for large \(n\), but it keeps the correspondence to the eigenproblem explicit, and the taper count is what the demo inspects:

        half = 0.5 * (n - 1)
    index = np.arange(n, dtype=float)
    diagonal = (half - index) ** 2 * np.cos(2.0 * np.pi * nw / n)
    off = index[1:] * (n - index[1:]) / 2.0
    matrix = np.diag(diagonal) + np.diag(off, 1) + np.diag(off, -1)
    values, vectors = np.linalg.eigh(matrix)
    order = np.argsort(values)[::-1]
    count = max(1, int(np.floor(2.0 * nw - 1.0)))

    nw is the time-bandwidth product \(NW\), so the bandwidth is \(NW/n\) and the number of usable tapers is \(2NW-1\). The eigenvectors are sorted descending because eigh returns ascending eigenvalues and the leading taper is the one with the largest concentration. The sign flip makes the largest coefficient positive, which matters only for reproducibility of plots.

    Welch is a loop over a view, not a copy. sliding_window_view creates the frames by stride manipulation, and the [::hop] slice selects the overlapping ones:

            frames = _frames(x, size, size - overlap)
        length = size if nfft is None else int(nfft)
        if length < size:
            raise ValueError("nfft must be at least nperseg")
        stack = np.empty((frames.shape[0], length // 2 + 1))
        for row, frame in enumerate(frames):
            frequencies, stack[row] = _density(frame, fs, window, length)
        return Spectrum(frequencies, stack.mean(axis=0), variant, fs, fs / size,
                        segments=int(frames.shape[0]))

    The reported resolution is fs / size, the segment length, not the full record length. That is the honest number: Welch has thrown away the extra resolution in exchange for the variance reduction.

    The multitaper loop is the same averaging idea with tapers instead of segments. Each taper gets its own normalisation by its energy, because the tapers have different shapes even though they are all unit norm:

            stack = np.empty((sequences.shape[0], length // 2 + 1))
        for row, taper in enumerate(sequences):
            transform = np.fft.rfft((x - x.mean()) * taper, n=length)
            power = np.abs(transform) ** 2 / (fs * float(taper @ taper))
            if length % 2 == 0:
                power[1:-1] *= 2.0
            else:
                power[1:] *= 2.0
            stack[row] = power

    Because the tapers are orthogonal, the \(K\) estimates are approximately uncorrelated, which is what makes the average worth doing. The mean is removed once, outside the loop, so every taper sees the same centred signal.

    Lomb-Scargle is the only variant that is a direct transcription of a least-squares problem. The phase is computed from the data at each frequency, then the cosine and sine projections are normalised separately:

        for row, frequency in enumerate(frequencies):
        angle = 2.0 * np.pi * frequency * times
        phase = np.arctan2(np.sin(angle).sum(), np.cos(angle).sum()) / (
            2.0 * np.pi * frequency)
        cosine = np.cos(2.0 * np.pi * frequency * (times - phase))
        sine = np.sin(2.0 * np.pi * frequency * (times - phase))
        left = float((centred @ cosine) ** 2 / (cosine @ cosine))
        right = float((centred @ sine) ** 2 / (sine @ sine))
        power[row] = 0.5 * (left + right) / (0.5 * x.size * variance)

    arctan2 gives the phase in \((-1/2,1/2]\) cycles, and dividing by the frequency converts it to a time shift. Computing the phase this way is equivalent to the closed form in (9) and avoids a separate arctangent identity. The final division by \(n\hat\sigma^2/2\) is the normalisation that makes a pure sinusoid of amplitude \(A\) peak near \(A^2/(2\hat\sigma^2)\).

    The STFT features are vectorised across frames. Each row of block is one frame's power spectrum, so the centroid and bandwidth are matrix-vector products against the frequency axis:

            total = block.sum(axis=1)
        total = np.where(total > 0.0, total, 1.0)
        centroid = block @ frequencies / total
        bandwidth = np.sqrt(
            np.maximum(block @ (frequencies ** 2) / total - centroid ** 2, 0.0))
        cumulative = np.cumsum(block, axis=1)

    The maximum(..., 0.0) is not decoration: the variance formula subtracts a squared mean from a mean square, and floating-point cancellation can make the difference a tiny negative number, whose square root would be nan.

    Rolloff and flatness finish the feature dictionary. Rolloff searches the cumulative sum; flatness is the exponential of the mean log, with an epsilon that keeps silent bins finite:

            rolloff = np.empty(block.shape[0])
        for row in range(block.shape[0]):
            threshold = 0.85 * cumulative[row, -1]
            rolloff[row] = frequencies[int(np.searchsorted(cumulative[row],
                                                           threshold))]
        epsilon = 1e-18
        flatness = np.exp(np.mean(np.log(block + epsilon), axis=1)
                          ) / np.maximum(block.mean(axis=1), epsilon)

    searchsorted returns the first index whose cumulative value reaches the threshold, which is exactly the definition of the rolloff. The frame time stored alongside is the centre, (row * hop + size / 2) / fs, so that a feature at time \(t\) refers to the middle of the window that produced it rather than its start.

    Complexity #

    With \(n\) samples, \(K\) segments or tapers of length \(L\), \(F\) candidate frequencies and \(M\) frames:

    • One FFT: \(O(L\log L)\) time and \(O(L)\) memory.
    • Periodogram: one FFT of length \(n\), so \(O(n\log n)\).
    • Welch: \(K\) FFTs of length \(L\), so \(O(KL\log L)\); memory \(O(KL)\) for the stacked spectra if they are kept, \(O(L)\) if averaged streaming.
    • Multitaper: \(K\) FFTs plus the DPSS eigenproblem. The code calls eigh on a dense \(n\times n\) matrix, so the construction costs \(O(n^3)\) and dominates for large \(n\); a production implementation exploits the tridiagonal structure for \(O(n^2)\) or uses a specialized routine.
    • Lomb-Scargle: \(O(nF)\) with no FFT, where \(F\approx\text{oversample}\cdot\text{span}\cdot f_{\max}\).
    • STFT: \(O(M L\log L)\) for the transforms plus \(O(ML)\) for the features.
    • Peak search and integration: \(O(n)\) extra.

    The demo's slowest call is the 512-point multitaper fit, at about 0.03 s; the 8 192-point Welch estimate and the 1 024-point periodogram are effectively instantaneous by comparison. The Lomb-Scargle loop over 1 906 frequencies on 300 points is the other visible cost, and it is linear in both.

    Failure modes #

    The periodogram is not a consistent estimator. Its variance does not shrink with \(n\); it only looks smoother because the bins get closer together. A plot of a periodogram of a long white-noise series is a forest of spikes whose height is meaningless. Smooth it, average segments, or use tapers before reading a peak height.

    Leakage masquerades as signal. A strong trend or a large low-frequency component spreads power across every bin. In the demo the periodogram's floor next to a strong tone is ten times the multitaper's. Detrend before transforming, and use a window with low sidelobes. A peak that moves when you change the window is leakage, not a resonance.

    Welch resolution is set by the segment, not the record. Users often expect more data to resolve closer frequencies; with Welch it does not. If two tones are closer than \(f_s/L\), choose a longer segment and accept more variance, or switch to a multitaper estimate with a smaller bandwidth.

    The time-bandwidth product is a real trade-off. Increasing \(NW\) gives more tapers, hence less variance, but each taper occupies a wider band, so nearby features blur. The demo's \(NW=4\) yields seven tapers and a main lobe about \(8/n\) wide. There is no setting that gives both a narrow lobe and many independent estimates.

    Irregular sampling has aliases too. Lomb-Scargle handles gaps but not aliasing: a sampling pattern with a strong periodicity, such as nightly observations, imprints that periodicity on the spectrum. The default frequency limit is the Nyquist rate of the median spacing, which is a heuristic. Check that a peak survives a random subsample and a different frequency grid before believing it.

    Normalisations differ between implementations. The Lomb-Scargle power in (10) is dimensionless and bounded by roughly the squared amplitude over the variance; other packages return power spectral density, or an unnormalised periodogram. Peak heights from two libraries are not comparable, and a "significant" peak under one normalisation may be ordinary under another.

    Frame length hides events. A 128-sample STFT frame at 50 Hz spans 2.56 s. A 100 ms transient is averaged into a frame and its spectral signature is diluted. Shorten the frame for transients, lengthen it for pitch, and always report the hop and frame length with the features, because a centroid computed at a different frame size is a different number.

    Flatness needs a floor. Without the epsilon, a frame with an exact zero bin gives \(\log 0=-\infty\) and the geometric mean collapses to zero even when the rest of the frame is loud. The demo's chirp is genuinely tonal, so its flatness is below the printed precision; do not read that as a bug.

    Applications #

    Spectral analysis is the default first look at any sensor signal. In audio it separates pitch from timbre, and the STFT features are the input to speech recognisers, music taggers and anomaly detectors. In electroencephalography, Welch averaging over two-second epochs is the standard way to estimate band power in the delta, theta, alpha and beta ranges, and the multitaper is preferred when the recording is short and leakage from the strong alpha rhythm would contaminate the higher bands.

    Astronomy is the home of Lomb-Scargle: light curves are sampled when the telescope is open and the weather allows, so the time grid is irregular by construction. The same estimator appears in paleoclimate work on varved sediments and in genomics on unevenly spaced methylation measurements. In vibration monitoring, the multitaper is used to detect a failing bearing tone against the broad noise of a machine, and the STFT tracks how that tone drifts as the fault develops.

    The method is the wrong tool when the signal is non-stationary on the scale of the record and the question is about time localisation: a single periodogram averages the whole record, and the STFT only localises to a frame. For time-varying spectra with better resolution, wavelets or the empirical mode decomposition are the natural next step. It is also the wrong tool for very short records, where the frequency grid is coarser than the features of interest; there, a parametric model such as an AR process estimated in the time domain gives sharper peaks from the same data.
