    DLinear — decomposition and a linear map, and why that is hard to beat

    Intuition #

    In 2023 a paper asked whether Transformers are actually necessary for long-horizon forecasting. Its answer was a one-layer linear map applied to a decomposed look-back window, and that map matched or beat the Transformer models on the standard benchmarks. The result is uncomfortable but simple: when the data are a smooth trend plus a repeating seasonal shape, a linear function of the recent past is already a good forecast, and most of the modelling effort in a deep architecture is spent relearning that structure.

    DLinear writes the assumption down explicitly. Take the last \(L\) observations, split them into a moving-average trend and a residual seasonal part, and learn two linear maps from those \(L\) values to the next \(H\). The sum is the forecast. The model has \(2LH\) weights — a few thousand for typical settings — and fitting it is one ridge regression, not a training loop.

    The variants isolate the two ideas inside that recipe:

    • dlinear is the full model: decomposition plus a jointly fitted linear head on the concatenated trend and seasonal features.
    • nlinear removes the decomposition and instead subtracts the last observation from every input and from the target. The forecast is invariant to an additive level shift, which is its whole purpose.
    • moving_avg_decomp keeps the decomposition but replaces the learned trend head with an explicit least-squares line through the trend, so the trend forecast is extrapolation rather than regression.
    • channel_independent fits one pair of heads per channel instead of sharing a single map across channels, which is what the original paper does.

    The demo checks accuracy against naive and seasonal-naive baselines, verifies the decomposition identity, measures the shift-equivariance that separates nlinear from dlinear, and compares pooled against per-channel heads on a two-channel series whose scales differ by an order of magnitude.

    Variants #

    variant forecast demo result
    dlinear linear map on trend and seasonal MAE 0.2545 against 1.7795
    nlinear linear map on last-value differences shift error 1.1e-13
    moving_avg_decomp line extrapolation and seasonal map MAE 0.1989 vs 1.1324
    channel_independent one map per channel MAE 0.1468 against 0.1518

    dlinear fits 541 windows from a 600-point training segment of a series with a linear trend, two harmonics and noise. The in-sample MSE is \(0.0910\), and rolling one-step-ahead blocks give a mean absolute error of \(0.2545\) against \(1.7795\) for the naive forecast and \(0.5632\) for seasonal naive with the correct period of 24. The gain over seasonal naive is the interesting one: the linear map is not just repeating yesterday, it is learning how the seasonal shape and the trend combine over the 48-step look-back.

    nlinear is trained on a stationary series and then asked to forecast after the level jumps by \(+1000\). Its forecast is exactly equivariant: shifting the history by \(c\) shifts every forecast by \(c\), with a numerical error of \(1.14\times10^{-13}\). dlinear is not equivariant; the same shift changes its forecast by \(1.3626\) too little, because its intercept and weights were fitted to the old level. One full window after the break the rolling MAE is \(0.1847\) for nlinear and \(0.9947\) for dlinear. The difference is real but modest on this synthetic series — a linear model partially follows a level shift — so the equivariance check, not the MAE, is the honest demonstration of the mechanism.

    moving_avg_decomp handles a trend whose slope changes halfway through the record. The trend component is smoothed with a 25-point kernel, a line is fitted to the last 60 trend values and extrapolated 12 steps, and a ridge head learns the residual. Its rolling MAE is \(0.1989\) against \(1.1324\) for naive. The explicit line is more interpretable than a learned trend head and is the right choice when the trend is locally linear by assumption.

    channel_independent is fitted to two channels with very different scales and periods: one has amplitude 4 and period 12, the other amplitude 0.4 and period 7. Separate heads reach a two-channel MAE of \(0.1468\) against \(0.1518\) for a single shared head. The improvement is only 3%, which is itself a useful result: on short windows with standardised features, pooling is a mild regulariser rather than a disaster. On channels with genuinely different dynamics the gap widens.

    Derivation #

    The windowed forecasting problem #

    Fix a look-back \(L\) and a horizon \(H\). For every origin \(t\) with enough history and future, form the pair

    \[x_{t-L+1:t}\in\mathbb R^{L},\qquad x_{t+1:t+H}\in\mathbb R^{H}, \qquad (1)\]

    and choose a forecast function \(f\) minimising squared error over the training origins:

    \[\min_{f}\sum_{t=L}^{n-H} \bigl\lVert f(x_{t-L+1:t})-x_{t+1:t+H}\bigr\rVert_2^2 . \qquad (2)\]

    The whole family differs only in how \(f\) is parameterised. DLinear restricts \(f\) to be affine after a fixed decomposition; NLinear applies an affine map after a fixed normalisation.

    Moving-average decomposition #

    For an odd kernel \(k=2p+1\), the trend is the centred average

    \[\tau_t=\frac1k\sum_{j=-p}^{p}x_{t+j}, \qquad (3)\]

    with the series extended by edge replication at both ends so that every observation has a full window. The seasonal residual is whatever is left:

    \[\sigma_t=x_t-\tau_t . \qquad (4)\]

    The identity (4) is exact by definition, which is why the demo can assert it to machine precision. The modelling assumption is not the identity but the claim that \(\tau\) is smooth and \(\sigma\) is periodic; a kernel much shorter than the period leaves seasonal variation in the trend, and a kernel much longer than the window flattens genuine changes.

    DLinear #

    Apply (3)–(4) to the look-back window, giving vectors \(\tau\) and \(\sigma\) of length \(L\). The forecast is

    \[\hat x_{t+1:t+H}=W_\tau\tau+W_\sigma\sigma+b, \qquad (5)\]

    with \(W_\tau,W_\sigma\in\mathbb R^{H\times L}\) and \(b\in\mathbb R^{H}\). Equation (5) is a single linear map on the concatenated feature vector \([\tau;\sigma]\), which is why the implementation fits one ridge regression rather than two independent heads. Fitting the two heads separately to the raw target would make each approximate the whole future and their sum double-count it; the demo's first version did exactly that and produced an in-sample MSE of \(273\) instead of \(0.091\).

    Ridge fitting #

    The features have different scales across lags and across channels, so the code standardises each column before solving. With \(z_i\) the standardised concatenation and \(Y\) the matrix of targets,

    \[\min_{b,W}\sum_{i}\lVert y_i-b-Wz_i\rVert_2^2+\lambda\lVert W\rVert_F^2, \qquad (6)\]

    where the intercept \(b\) is not penalised. Stacking a column of ones in front of \(Z\) and writing \(P=\mathrm{diag}(0,1,\dots,1)\) gives the closed form

    \[\begin{bmatrix}b\\ W\end{bmatrix} =\bigl(Z^\top Z+\lambda P\bigr)^{-1}Z^\top Y . \qquad (7)\]

    The code solves (7) with np.linalg.solve and falls back to lstsq if the Gram matrix is singular, which happens when a channel is constant or when \(L\) exceeds the number of windows. Standardisation is what makes a single \(\lambda\) sensible across columns; without it the penalty would depend on the units of each lag.

    NLinear #

    NLinear removes the level of each window before applying the map. Let \(\ell=x_t\) be the last observed value. The normalised input and target are

    \[\tilde x_{i}=x_i-\ell,\qquad \tilde y_{h}=x_{t+h}-\ell, \qquad (8)\]

    and the model is a single affine map \(g\) fitted to \((\tilde x,\tilde y)\). At prediction time the level is added back:

    \[\hat x_{t+1:t+H}=g(\tilde x)+\ell\,\mathbf 1 . \qquad (9)\]

    Substituting \(x_i+c\) for every input shifts \(\tilde x\) by nothing and \(\ell\) by \(c\), so the forecast shifts by exactly \(c\). That is the equivariance the demo measures at \(10^{-13}\). DLinear lacks it because \(b\) is fixed: its response to a uniform shift is \(c\) times the sum of its weights, which is close to but not exactly one.

    NLinear handles an additive level break that happened before the window. It does not handle a break inside the look-back: the differences then contain the jump, and the linear map extrapolates it as if it were a trend. In the demo the transition window is excluded for that reason; a real deployment should detect the break and wait for a clean window, or model the break explicitly.

    Explicit trend extrapolation #

    For moving_avg_decomp, the trend head is replaced by a line fitted to the trend values at positions \(0,\dots,L-1\):

    \[\hat\tau_{L+h}=\bar\tau+\hat\beta\,(L+h-\bar t),\qquad \hat\beta=\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{L-1}(t-\bar t)(\tau_t-\bar\tau)} {\sum_{t=0}^{L-1}(t-\bar t)^2}, \qquad (10)\]

    and the ridge head is fitted to the residual target \(y-\hat\tau\). The trend forecast is now extrapolation, so it degrades gracefully on a locally linear trend and badly at a turning point. This is the classical decomposition-plus-extrapolation idea, with the seasonal part still learned.

    Channel independence #

    For a series with \(C\) channels, the pooled model stacks all windows from all channels into one design matrix and solves (7) once. The channel-independent model solves one problem per channel:

    \[\hat x^{(c)}_{t+1:t+H}=W^{(c)}_\tau\tau^{(c)} +W^{(c)}_\sigma\sigma^{(c)}+b^{(c)}, \qquad c=1,\dots,C . \qquad (11)\]

    Pooling assumes that the same linear combination of lags is useful for every channel. That is a strong assumption when scales or periods differ, and a mild regulariser when they do not. The original DLinear is channel-independent; the pooled option is useful as a cheaper model and as a diagnostic for how much the channels actually share.

    Evaluation #

    All demo numbers are rolling-origin errors. For a set of origins \(\mathcal O\),

    \[\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal O\rvert H} \sum_{o\in\mathcal O}\sum_{h=1}^{H} \bigl\lvert\hat x_{o+h}-x_{o+h}\bigr\rvert, \qquad (12)\]

    with the model refitted once on the training prefix and only the forecast origin moved. A random train/test split would leak future information into the standardisation and into the window construction, and would flatter every variant.

    Code walkthrough #

    The decomposition is a sliding-window mean. Padding by edge replication keeps the output length equal to the input, which is what lets the trend and residual be subtracted elementwise:

    def _moving_average(values: np.ndarray, kernel: int) -> np.ndarray:
    """Centred moving average along the last axis, edge-padded."""
    pad = kernel // 2
    padding = [(0, 0)] * (values.ndim - 1) + [(pad, pad)]
    padded = np.pad(values, padding, mode="edge")
    view = np.lib.stride_tricks.sliding_window_view(padded, kernel, axis=-1)
    return view.mean(axis=-1)

    sliding_window_view returns a view, so the average is computed without materialising m copies of each window. Edge replication is a choice: it biases the trend toward the endpoint at the boundary, which is preferable to zero padding (which would introduce a fake jump) and simpler than fitting a local line there.

    Window construction is pure index arithmetic, vectorised over all origins:

        count = values.shape[0] - seq_len - horizon + 1
    if count < 2:
        raise ValueError("need at least two training windows")
    starts = np.arange(count)
    left = values[starts[:, None] + np.arange(seq_len)[None, :]]
    right = values[starts[:, None] + seq_len + np.arange(horizon)[None, :]]
    return left, right

    The result is (count, seq_len, channels) and (count, horizon, channels). Fancy indexing copies, so this is the memory hotspot: about \(nL\) doubles for the design matrix. For very long series, the same windows can be generated in batches, but the closed-form fit needs the full Gram matrix anyway.

    The explicit trend head is a vectorised least-squares line. Centring the index grid avoids forming a design matrix and makes the slope a single dot product:

        length = values.shape[1]
    grid = np.arange(length, dtype=float)
    centred = grid - grid.mean()
    slope = values @ centred / float(centred @ centred)
    intercept = values.mean(axis=1)
    future = length + np.arange(horizon) - grid.mean()
    return intercept[:, None] + slope[:, None] * future[None, :]

    Each row of values is one window, so the same expression fits every window at once. The future grid starts at length, the first unobserved index, and runs to length + horizon - 1.

    The ridge solver is the only numerical machinery in the file. It standardises features, leaves the intercept out of the penalty, and solves the normal equations directly:

            self.mean_ = features.mean(axis=0)
        scale = features.std(axis=0)
        self.scale_ = np.where(scale > 1e-12, scale, 1.0)
        design = np.column_stack(
            [np.ones(features.shape[0]), (features - self.mean_) / self.scale_])
        penalty = self.alpha * np.eye(design.shape[1])
        penalty[0, 0] = 0.0
        gram = design.T @ design + penalty
        moment = design.T @ targets
        try:
            self.coef_ = np.linalg.solve(gram, moment)
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
            self.coef_ = np.linalg.lstsq(gram, moment, rcond=None)[0]

    The scale > 1e-12 guard turns a constant feature into a zero column instead of dividing by zero; the intercept then absorbs its mean. The fallback is not decorative: with ridge=0 and more lags than windows the Gram matrix is singular, and solve raises rather than returning a least-norm solution.

    Fitting DLinear is three lines once the windows exist. The target is flattened in the same window-major, channel-minor order as the features, so row \(i\) of the design always corresponds to row \(i\) of the target:

                flat = left.transpose(0, 2, 1).reshape(-1, self.seq_len_)
            target = right.transpose(0, 2, 1).reshape(-1, self.horizon)
            trend, seasonal = _decompose(flat, self.kernel)
            if self.variant == "dlinear":
                design = np.column_stack([trend, seasonal])
                self.model_ = _Ridge(self.ridge).fit(design, target)
            else:
                self.trend_future_train_ = _line_forecast(trend, self.horizon)
                self.seasonal_model_ = _Ridge(self.ridge).fit(
                    seasonal, target - self.trend_future_train_)

    The column_stack is (5) in code: the two heads share one solve, so the fit allocates the error between them however it likes. The else branch is the only place where the trend is not learned; there the target residual is target - trend_future, which is why the seasonal head is fitted to a detrended target.

    NLinear's normalisation is symmetric between fit and predict. The last column of each window is the reference, and [:, None, :] re-inserts the axis that broadcasting needs:

            last = left[:, -1, :][:, None, :]
        features = (left - last).transpose(0, 2, 1).reshape(
            -1, self.seq_len_)
        targets = (right - last).transpose(0, 2, 1).reshape(
            -1, self.horizon)
        return features, targets

    Because the reference is subtracted from both sides, the learned map sees only within-window differences. The last feature column is identically zero, so the corresponding weight is unidentifiable; ridge drives it to zero, and the intercept carries the average change.

    Prediction inverts the same operation. The reshape restores the window/channel order before the level is added back, and the final transpose puts the horizon before the channels:

            if self.variant == "nlinear":
            last = left[:, -1, :][:, None, :]
            features = (left - last).transpose(0, 2, 1).reshape(
                -1, self.seq_len_)
            forecast = self.nlinear_.predict(features)
            forecast = forecast.reshape(count, channels, self.horizon)
            return forecast.transpose(0, 2, 1) + last

    The decomposition variants share a single _predict_windows path. The pooled model predicts all windows at once; the per-channel branch loops over channels and writes into a preallocated array:

            flat = left.transpose(0, 2, 1).reshape(-1, self.seq_len_)
        trend, seasonal = _decompose(flat, self.kernel)
        if self.variant == "dlinear":
            forecast = self.model_.predict(
                np.column_stack([trend, seasonal]))
        else:
            forecast = (_line_forecast(trend, self.horizon)
                        + self.seasonal_model_.predict(seasonal))
        return forecast.reshape(count, channels, self.horizon).transpose(0, 2, 1)

    Keeping the window axis in the reshape is what makes predict and the in-sample loss use the same function. The train_loss_ reported by fit is computed by calling _predict_windows on the training windows, so it cannot silently disagree with the forecasts the model actually produces.

    Complexity #

    With \(n\) observations, \(C\) channels, look-back \(L\), horizon \(H\), \(m=n-L-H+1\) windows and \(k\) the moving-average kernel:

    • Windowing: \(O(mLC)\) time and memory, the dominant cost for long series.
    • Decomposition: \(O(mLC)\) with the sliding-window view, plus \(O(mLC)\) for the residual.
    • One ridge solve: \(O(m(2L)^2+(2L)^3)\) time for pooled DLinear, with the same solve producing all \(H\) target columns.
    • channel_independent: \(C\) solves, so \(O(C(mL^2+L^3))\).
    • nlinear: one solve of the same size as DLinear without the doubled feature count.
    • Prediction: \(O(LC)\) to prepare and \(O(HLC)\) to apply the maps; a single forecast is a handful of dot products.
    • Memory: \(O(mLC)\) for the training windows, \(O((2L)H)\) for the weights.

    The demo's four fits on 720 points each take a few hundredths of a second. The model is small enough to run on a microcontroller, which is part of its appeal; the expensive part of a deployment is usually the windowing and the data plumbing, not the arithmetic.

    Failure modes #

    A linear map cannot extrapolate a nonlinear trend. DLinear learns weights that combine lags, so it follows a straight trend well and a curve poorly. If the trend accelerates, the forecast lags behind it; the residual grows with the horizon. moving_avg_decomp does not fix this in general — it fits a line to the trend window — but it makes the assumption explicit so you can test it. Difference the series or model the trend separately when curvature matters.

    The look-back must cover the longest period. A 48-step window sees only two cycles of a period-24 seasonal pattern. That is enough for the demo, but the weights are then estimated from few independent seasonal shapes. If the period is 168 (hourly data with a weekly cycle), a 48-step window is blind to it, and the model will fit noise. Set \(L\) to at least several periods, or feed the model seasonal features explicitly.

    Edge padding biases the decomposition. The moving average replicates the first and last values, so the trend at the boundary is pulled toward them and the residual there absorbs the error. The bias is \(O(k)\) observations wide. In the demo the interior of a pure ramp is recovered to \(3.6\times10^{-15}\), but the first and last few trend values are not exact. Do not read the boundary residual as a change in seasonality.

    NLinear's invariance is additive only. It handles a level shift, not a change in scale or in the seasonal amplitude. Multiply the series by three and the differences triple while the learned map still expects the old scale; the forecast is then too small by roughly a factor of three in the seasonal component. Detect scale changes separately, or normalise by a robust scale rather than by the last value alone.

    A break inside the window breaks both models. NLinear subtracts the last value, but if the jump occurred 20 steps ago the differences still contain it and the linear map extrapolates the jump as a trend. In the demo the transition window has to be excluded. A real system should detect the break, discard the contaminated window, and only forecast once the look-back is clean.

    Pooling channels can hide a bad channel. The pooled model minimises average error, so a high-variance channel dominates the fit and a low-variance channel is fitted almost by accident. In the demo the penalty is only 3%, but with channels whose scales differ by \(10^3\) the pooled weights are effectively chosen for the largest channel. Standardise per channel or use channel_independent.

    Random splits leak the future. Sliding windows overlap, so a random train/test split puts near-duplicate windows on both sides and the test error becomes an in-sample error in disguise. Always split by time and evaluate on rolling origins, as in (12). This is the single most common reason a DLinear result looks better than it is.

    The benchmark claim is conditional. DLinear beat several Transformers on the long-horizon benchmarks used in the 2023 paper, but later work showed that those benchmarks reward trend extrapolation and that tuned transformers can close the gap. DLinear is best read as a strong, cheap baseline that any sequence model must beat — not as proof that attention is useless.

    Applications #

    DLinear is used as the default baseline in long-term forecasting benchmarks and as a sanity check before training a deep model: if a linear map on the decomposed window matches the candidate, the candidate's extra capacity is not buying anything on that dataset. Its parameter count makes it attractive for edge deployment — smart meters, weather stations, industrial sensors — where the forecast runs on the device and retraining must be cheap.

    The decomposition variant is useful in energy load forecasting, where the trend is driven by slow economic and demographic change and the seasonal component by daily and weekly routines. In traffic forecasting the same structure appears at the scale of a road network, and channel-independent heads let each sensor keep its own scale. In retail demand the series are intermittent and the linear assumption is weak; there a count model or Croston's method is a better fit, and DLinear's failure is informative rather than embarrassing.

    The model is the wrong choice when the target depends on interactions between lags that are not additive, when the horizon is much longer than the look-back and the trend is nonlinear, or when exogenous drivers (weather, promotions, holidays) carry most of the signal. In those cases add the covariates explicitly, use a model with a nonlinear link, or move to a sequence model — but keep DLinear as the baseline that the more complex model has to justify itself against.
