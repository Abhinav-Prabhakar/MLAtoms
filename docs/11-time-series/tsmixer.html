    11 · Time series/tier 3/4 variants/source

    TSMixer — replace attention with two alternating perceptrons

    Intuition #

    Every transformer forecaster spends its parameters on pairwise interactions: token \(i\) against token \(j\). TSMixer asks what is actually being mixed, and answers that there are only two axes. A multivariate window is a matrix \(X\in\mathbb R^{L\times C}\) — time down the rows, channels across the columns. To forecast it you need to combine observations along time, and you need to combine channels. Those are two operations, and neither needs a quadratic matrix of interactions.

    So the block is two multi-layer perceptrons applied along the two axes. The time-mixing MLP takes the \(L\) values of one channel, projects them to a hidden width, and projects back — it can learn any linear filter over the window, and with the ReLU it can learn a nonlinear one. The feature-mixing MLP does the same across the \(C\) channels at a single timestep: it learns which channels predict which. Each is wrapped in a residual connection, and the stack of blocks alternates time, features, time, features.

    The cost is linear in both \(L\) and \(C\) rather than quadratic, and the model is a stack of matrix products — the kind of thing that runs in microseconds on a CPU. On the 289-window demo it reaches a held-out MAE of \(0.2282\) against \(1.3784\) for repeating the last value.

    The variants make the ablation explicit:

    • time_mix keeps only the time-axis MLP.
    • feature_mix keeps only the channel-axis MLP.
    • tsmixer alternates both.
    • tsmixer_ext also consumes exogenous features, in the feature branch and in the head.

    The demo's most interesting result is the negative one: time_mix alone reaches \(0.1912\) and beats the full model. The channel branch costs parameters and buys nothing when the channels are redundant. That is the honest headline, and it is the reason the ablation exists.

    Variants #

    variant time branch feature branch exog held-out MAE
    time_mix yes no no 0.1912
    feature_mix no yes no 0.2402
    tsmixer yes yes no 0.2282
    tsmixer_ext yes yes yes 3.3795 vs 4.0221

    time_mix owns 10 arrays and \(6892\) numbers, all of them in the two time matrices per block and the head. It is the best of the three on this data, which is unsurprising: the three channels are noisy affine copies of one another, so there is no independent cross-channel signal to learn.

    feature_mix also owns 10 arrays but only \(1042\) numbers, because the channel axis has three columns. It reaches \(0.2402\). Remarkably it is close to the full model — most of the useful fit is a per-timestep linear map plus the head.

    tsmixer owns 18 arrays and \(7346\) numbers: two time branches and two feature branches, plus one head. It reaches \(0.2282\), between the two ablations. Per block the time branch costs \(3152\) numbers and the feature branch \(227\), a ratio of fourteen to one.

    tsmixer_ext is trained on a different series, one with a random driver that the history cannot predict but the future does reveal. Without the driver the model reaches \(4.0221\) — worse than the last value, because the series is mostly unpredictable noise around a seasonal mean. With the driver it reaches \(3.3795\). The head widens from \(48\) to \(60\) columns: \(48\) look-back steps plus \(12\) future driver steps.

    Derivation #

    Notation #

    A training window is \(X\in\mathbb R^{L\times C}\) with target \(Y\in\mathbb R^{H\times C}\). \(L\) is the look-back, \(H\) the horizon, \(C\) the number of channels. A batch stacks \(B\) windows.

    Standardisation #

    Each window is standardised channel by channel, using its own look-back statistics:

    \[\tilde x_{tc}=\frac{x_{tc}-\mu_c}{\sigma_c},\qquad \mu_c=\frac1L\sum_{t=1}^{L}x_{tc},\qquad \sigma_c^2=\frac1L\sum_{t=1}^{L}(x_{tc}-\mu_c)^2 . \qquad (1)\]

    The same \(\mu_c,\sigma_c\) standardise the target. This is the "instance normalisation" that makes a non-stationary series stationary within each window: the network never sees an absolute level, only a shape. The forecast is mapped back with \(\hat y=\mu_c+\sigma_c\hat z\) at prediction time.

    Layer normalisation #

    Every mixing MLP is preceded by a normalisation over the axis it is about to mix:

    \[\mathrm{LN}(z)=\frac{z-\bar z}{\sqrt{\tfrac1d\sum_{j}(z_j-\bar z)^2+\epsilon}}, \qquad \bar z=\frac1d\sum_{j=1}^{d}z_j . \qquad (2)\]

    After transposing, the time MLP sees a vector of length \(L\) and the feature MLP a vector of length \(C\), so (2) is applied with \(d=L\) and \(d=C\) respectively. The normalisation is affine-free: the gain and bias are redundant with the following linear layer's own bias and with the residual scale, and dropping them removes two arrays per branch.

    Time mixing #

    For each channel \(c\), with \(W_t\in\mathbb R^{L\times h}\), \(V_t\in\mathbb R^{h\times L}\):

    \[a_c=\mathrm{ReLU}\bigl(\mathrm{LN}(X_{\cdot c})\,W_t+b_t\bigr)\in\mathbb R^{h}, \qquad (3)\]
    \[Z_{\cdot c}=X_{\cdot c}+a_c\,V_t+c_t . \qquad (4)\]

    The map \(X_{\cdot c}\mapsto a_cV_t\) is a rank-\(h\) linear operator on the window, gated by which of its \(h\) hidden units are active. With \(h\ge L\) it can represent any linear filter — moving average, difference, seasonal lag — and it learns the filter from data rather than from a fixed kernel. This is exactly what DLinear does with a fixed moving average plus a linear layer; TSMixer learns both parts jointly.

    Feature mixing #

    At each timestep \(t\), with \(W_f\in\mathbb R^{(C+A)\times h}\) and \(V_f\in\mathbb R^{h\times C}\):

    \[g_t=\mathrm{ReLU}\bigl(\mathrm{LN}(Z_{t\cdot})\,W_f+b_f\bigr)\in\mathbb R^{h}, \qquad (5)\]
    \[X'_{t\cdot}=Z_{t\cdot}+g_t\,V_f+c_f . \qquad (6)\]

    \(A\) is the number of exogenous features; they are concatenated to the normalised channels before \(W_f\), which is why that matrix is \((C+A)\times h\). Setting \(A=0\) recovers the plain model.

    Unlike channel independence, this branch gives every channel its own row of \(W_f\) and its own column of \(V_f\). The model is therefore not equivariant to permuting the channels: a permuted input meets a different set of weights. That is the price of letting the channels talk, and it is why the branch is a good place to attach exogenous variables — each one gets an identifiable coefficient.

    The block #

    One block is the composition of the two:

    \[X^{(r+1)}=\mathcal F^{(r)}\bigl(\mathcal T^{(r)}(X^{(r)})\bigr), \qquad r=1,\dots,R, \qquad (7)\]

    with \(X^{(1)}=\tilde X\). Order matters: time mixing sees channels with independent residuals, feature mixing then sees a representation already smoothed along time. Reversing the order is a different model; the demo's feature_mix and time_mix ablations are the two degenerate cases where one factor is the identity.

    The head #

    The final representation is flattened across channels and mapped to the horizon:

    \[F=\mathrm{reshape}_{C\times H}\!\left(\mathrm{vec}_C(X^{(R)})\,W_o+b_o\right) \in\mathbb R^{C\times H}, \qquad W_o\in\mathbb R^{L\times H}. \qquad (8)\]

    vec_C puts the \(L\) values of one channel contiguously, so the same \(W_o\) is applied to every channel. This is where TSMixer gets its parameter efficiency: one \(48\times12\) matrix forecasts all three channels.

    With exogenous features the head receives the concatenation

    \[\mathrm{vec}_C(X^{(R)})\;\|\;\mathbf 1_C\otimes \mathrm{vec}(U), \qquad U\in\mathbb R^{H\times A}, \qquad (9)\]

    where \(U\) is the standardised future driver and \(\mathbf 1_C\otimes\cdot\) repeats it for every channel. The driver rows of \(W_o\) are then per-channel regression coefficients on the known future, learned jointly with the rest.

    Objective #

    Training minimises mean squared error on standardised targets:

    \[L=\frac{1}{BCH}\sum_{b,c}\sum_{h=1}^{H} \bigl(\hat y_{bch}-y_{bch}\bigr)^2 . \qquad (10)\]

    Optimisation is full-batch Adam. The backward pass through a residual is the identity plus the branch Jacobian, which is why the residual gradient in the code is a sum:

    \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial X}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial X'} +\mathcal B^{\top}\!\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial X'}\right). \qquad (11)\]

    Code walkthrough #

    The whole architecture is two flags, so the three variants are not three code paths:

            self.use_time = variant != "feature_mix"
        self.use_feature = variant != "time_mix"

    Everything downstream — parameter creation, forward, backward, the optimiser's parameter dict — reads those flags. A variant that turns a branch off does not merely skip it; the branch's matrices are never allocated, which is why the demo can assert an exact array count for each variant.

    The time branch transposes so that the last axis is the one being mixed:

                if self.use_time:
                transposed = current.transpose(0, 2, 1)
                norm, norm_cache = _layer_norm(transposed)
                pre = norm @ p[f"T1_{index}"] + p[f"t1_{index}"]
                active = np.maximum(pre, 0.0)
                mixed = active @ p[f"T2_{index}"] + p[f"t2_{index}"]
                current = current + mixed.transpose(0, 2, 1)

    current is always (batch, time, channel). Transposing to (batch, channel, time) lets one matrix product apply the same filter to every channel without a loop, and the transpose back restores the layout the next branch expects. Two transposes per block are the entire cost of sharing.

    The feature branch needs no transpose because the channel axis is already last:

                if self.use_feature:
                norm, norm_cache = _layer_norm(current)
                joined = (np.concatenate([norm, aux], axis=2)
                          if self.aux else norm)
                pre = joined @ p[f"F1_{index}"] + p[f"f1_{index}"]
                active = np.maximum(pre, 0.0)
                mixed = active @ p[f"F2_{index}"] + p[f"f2_{index}"]
                current = current + mixed

    Concatenating the auxiliary features into the normalised channels means the exogenous inputs are normalised by the same layer norm, so they enter the third branch on the same scale as the series. That is a small choice with a large effect: an unnormalised driver of magnitude \(10^3\) would saturate the ReLU and stall training.

    The head folds the channel axis into the batch and then restores it:

            flat = current.transpose(0, 2, 1).reshape(-1, self.seq_len)
        if self.aux:
            repeated = np.repeat(future.reshape(x.shape[0], -1),
                                 self.channels, axis=0)
            head = np.concatenate([flat, repeated], axis=1)
        else:
            head = flat
        forecast = (head @ p["Wo"] + p["bo"]).reshape(
            x.shape[0], self.channels, self.horizon)
        return forecast.transpose(0, 2, 1), {"blocks": cache, "head": head}

    reshape(-1, L) after the transpose gives (batch * channel, L), so the head is one matrix product for all channels. The reshape back to (batch, channel, horizon) and the final transpose assume the channel order is preserved — which it is, because transpose and reshape are applied in the reverse order on the way out.

    The backward pass starts from the same fold. The head's input is cached because the residual makes it unrecoverable from the gradient alone:

            flat_grad = gradient.transpose(0, 2, 1).reshape(-1, self.horizon)
        g["Wo"] = cache["head"].T @ flat_grad
        g["bo"] = flat_grad.sum(axis=0)
        current_grad = (flat_grad @ p["Wo"][:self.seq_len].T).reshape(
            batch, self.channels, self.seq_len).transpose(0, 2, 1)

    The slice [:self.seq_len] is the part of the wide head that came from the series; the remaining rows belong to the exogenous features and have no path back into the mixer.

    The feature branch's backward is worth reading in full because it shows the residual sum:

                        width = active.shape[-1]
                    g[f"F2_{index}"] = (active.reshape(-1, width).T
                                        @ current_grad.reshape(-1, self.channels))
                    g[f"f2_{index}"] = current_grad.sum(axis=(0, 1))
                    delta = (current_grad @ p[f"F2_{index}"].T) * (pre > 0.0)
                    joined = entry["joined"]
                    g[f"F1_{index}"] = (joined.reshape(-1, joined.shape[-1]).T
                                        @ delta.reshape(-1, width))
                    g[f"f1_{index}"] = delta.sum(axis=(0, 1))
                    normalised_grad = (delta @ p[f"F1_{index}"].T)[
                        ..., :self.channels]
                    current_grad = current_grad + _layer_norm_backward(
                        normalised_grad, entry["cache"])

    delta is the gradient after the ReLU, normalised_grad is the gradient into the normalised channels, and the last line adds the branch's contribution to the identity path. The slice [..., :self.channels] drops the exogenous columns: their gradient exists but there is nothing upstream to receive it.

    For tsmixer_ext, the windows and the future rows are cut from one standardised matrix:

                exog_windows = standardised[
                starts[:, None] + np.arange(self.seq_len)[None, :], :]
            future = standardised[
                starts[:, None] + self.seq_len
                + np.arange(self.horizon)[None, :], :]

    Both are built from starts, the same sliding-window indices used for the series, which guarantees that the driver at time \(t\) lines up with the target at time \(t\). Getting that alignment wrong — using starts + 1 or forgetting the horizon offset — is the classic exogenous-forecasting bug, and it produces a model that looks excellent in training and useless out of sample.

    Complexity #

    With \(B\) windows, \(L\) look-back, \(C\) channels, \(A\) auxiliary features, \(R\) blocks, hidden width \(h\) and horizon \(H\):

    • Standardisation: \(O(BLC)\).
    • Time branch: \(O(B C L h)\) time and \(O(B C \max(L,h))\) memory for the activations; the same matrix serves every channel.
    • Feature branch: \(O(B L (C+A) h)\).
    • One block: the sum, so linear in \(L\), \(C\) and \(h\).
    • Head: \(O(B C L H)\).
    • Full forward pass: \(O\bigl(BR(C L h + L(C+A)h) + BCLH\bigr)\).
    • One epoch: three times the forward pass for the backward, plus the parameter count.
    • Prediction: one window, so the same expression with \(B=1\).

    The comparison that matters is against attention. PatchTST mixes \(N=L/P\) patches with an \(N\times N\) attention map, so its mixing cost is \(O(L^2 h/P^2)\) per channel; TSMixer's is \(O(Lh)\) per channel, smaller by a factor of \(L/P^2\). Against channel mixing attention, which is \(O(C^2h)\) per timestep, TSMixer pays \(O(Ch)\) — the same saving in \(C\).

    In absolute terms the demo's largest model has \(7346\) parameters and trains in about a second for 120 full-batch epochs over 289 windows. There is no attention map to materialise, so memory stays proportional to the activations and not to \(L^2\).

    Failure modes #

    Feature mixing overfits when the channels are redundant. The full model reaches \(0.2282\) and the time-only ablation \(0.1912\). The channel branch has per-channel weights and only three channels to estimate them from, so it fits noise that the time branch ignores. With three hundred channels the same branch would be the one carrying the signal; with three, it is a liability. Always run the ablation before trusting channel mixing.

    Instance normalisation discards the level. Because every window is standardised by its own mean, the model never sees an absolute value. A linear map can still extrapolate a trend, but it has to encode the drift as a fixed transformation of the shape, which a trending series defeats. If the level matters, add it back as an explicit covariate or use a model with a level component.

    No probabilistic output. The head emits a point forecast. Uncertainty would require a quantile head, a distributional loss, or a residual bootstrap — none of which the block computes. For decision-making under uncertainty this is the main limitation relative to DeepAR or a Gaussian state-space model.

    The head is \(L\times H\) per channel-set. At \(L=48\) it is 588 numbers; at \(L=512\) and \(H=96\) it would dominate the parameter count. Longer look-backs want a bottleneck or a patching scheme in front of the head.

    Stacking blocks does not monotonically help. On the demo configuration, one block reaches \(0.2573\), two reach \(0.2282\), three \(0.2606\) and four \(0.2401\), for \(3967\), \(7346\), \(10725\) and \(14104\) parameters. Depth buys capacity that a few hundred windows cannot identify, and the held-out error wanders rather than falling. Tune the block count; do not assume more is better.

    Reversal of the block order changes the model. Time-then-feature is not the same as feature-then-time; the second branch sees a different residual. If you change the order, retune, and do not assume the ablation results transfer.

    Layer norm over few channels is noisy. With \(C=3\) the normalisation has three numbers to estimate a variance from, so it is a coarse operation. With \(C=1\) it divides by the standard deviation of a single value — zero — and the code falls back to a unit scale. The model still trains, but the normalisation stops doing anything useful.

    Applications #

    TSMixer is aimed at the regime where attention is overkill: hundreds of related series, moderate horizons, and a hard latency budget. Retail demand (the M5 dataset in the paper), electricity load, traffic counts and weather station networks all fit. The model is small enough to run on a CPU at the edge or inside a database, and the alternating structure maps to two batched matrix products that vectorise well.

    The extended variant is for the case where the future is partly known: promotions and holidays in retail, temperature forecasts in load forecasting, scheduled maintenance in industrial telemetry. Its head puts a per-channel coefficient on each future driver, which makes the exogenous effect inspectable — a rare property in a deep forecaster.

    It is the wrong choice for a single short series with no cross-channel structure (there the time branch alone is a small MLP and a linear model is a fair fight), for long horizons on high-frequency data, and for anything that needs calibrated intervals.
