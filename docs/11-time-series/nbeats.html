    N-BEATS — residual basis expansion with small neural blocks

    Intuition #

    Most neural forecasters win by being large. N-BEATS wins by being small and stacked. A single block is a two-layer perceptron with a few thousand weights and two linear heads: one head reconstructs the input window, the other emits the forecast. Blocks are then chained, and each one sees only the part of the window that the previous blocks failed to reconstruct. The final forecast is the sum of every block's contribution.

    The trick is that both heads are linear in a basis. The perceptron does not output the forecast directly; it outputs coefficients, and a fixed matrix turns those coefficients into a backcast and a forecast. With an identity basis the block can produce anything, which is the generic variant. With a polynomial basis and a Fourier basis the coefficients become a trend slope and a set of seasonal amplitudes, which is the interpretable variant — the same architecture, but the forecast is now readable.

    The residual chain is what makes the stack behave like a decomposition. Block one removes the trend it can reconstruct; block two sees a detrended window and removes the seasonality; block three sees what is left. Nothing forces the blocks to specialise, but the architecture nudges them that way, and when the bases are interpretable the split is usually obvious.

    The four variants cover the paper's main configurations:

    • generic uses identity bases, so every coefficient is free.
    • interpretable alternates polynomial trend blocks and Fourier seasonal blocks, producing an additive trend-season forecast.
    • nbeatsx adds a ridge head on exogenous variables that are known for the forecast window but are not in the target's own history.
    • ensemble averages several independently initialised stacks.

    The demo trains on 348 points of a trend plus two harmonics and holds out 12. The generic stack reaches a held-out MAE of \(0.2466\) against \(0.4627\) for seasonal naive. The interpretable stack reaches \(0.3779\) — it pays about \(0.13\) for a forecast you can read.

    Variants #

    variant bases demo result
    generic identity MAE 0.2466, train MSE 0.0279
    interpretable trend and Fourier MAE 0.3779, train MSE 0.0642
    nbeatsx generic plus ridge head MAE 1.0332 vs 2.9888
    ensemble three generic stacks MAE 0.2408 vs 0.2466

    generic trains two blocks on 301 sliding windows. The in-sample mean squared error is \(0.0279\) in units of the per-window variance, so the stack explains over 97% of the variance of the standardised windows. The held-out MAE of \(0.2466\) is close to the simulated noise level (\(0.25\) per point in original units), and it beats both naive baselines by a wide margin.

    interpretable uses four alternating blocks with a linear trend basis and two Fourier harmonics. Its train MSE is \(0.0642\) — more than twice the generic error — and its held-out MAE is \(0.3779\). The constrained basis cannot bend to absorb the second harmonic as flexibly as a free linear map, so the parametric model is genuinely a compromise: a readable decomposition at a measurable cost.

    nbeatsx is fitted to the same trend-and-season signal plus a driver that is drawn independently at every step and enters with a coefficient of three. The driver is invisible in the target's past, so the blocks cannot anticipate it; only the exogenous head can. Held-out MAE falls from \(2.9888\) without the driver to \(1.0332\) with it, and the fitted head is \([-0.0371,\ 2.7271]\) on the standardised driver against a true effect of three.

    ensemble averages three stacks that differ only in their initial weights. Member MAEs are \(0.2466\), \(0.2277\) and \(0.2364\); the ensemble reaches \(0.2408\) against a member mean of \(0.2369\). Averaging beats the worst seed and beats the first seed, but it does not beat the average member — the members are highly correlated, so the variance reduction is modest.

    Derivation #

    The residual stack #

    Let \(x_0=Y_{1:L}\) be the standardised look-back window and \(y\) the target window. A stack of \(K\) blocks produces

    \[\hat y=\sum_{k=1}^{K} f_k(x_{k-1}), \qquad x_k=x_{k-1}-b_k(x_{k-1}), \qquad (1)\]

    where \(f_k\) is block \(k\)'s forecast and \(b_k\) its backcast. The backcast is a reconstruction of the block's own input, not of the target: subtracting it means each block passes forward only the structure it could not explain.

    One block #

    Hidden layers are plain ReLU perceptrons. With two hidden layers,

    \[h_1=\mathrm{ReLU}(W_1x+b_1),\quad h_2=\mathrm{ReLU}(W_2h_1+b_2), \qquad (2)\]

    and the two heads are linear maps of the last hidden layer:

    \[\theta^{f}=W_fh_2+c_f,\qquad \theta^{b}=W_bh_2+c_b . \qquad (3)\]

    The forecast and backcast are the coefficients times fixed basis matrices:

    \[f=V^{f}\theta^{f},\qquad b=V^{b}\theta^{b}, \qquad (4)\]

    with \(V^{f}\in\mathbb R^{H\times d_f}\) and \(V^{b}\in\mathbb R^{L\times d_b}\). Only \(\theta\) is learned. Choosing \(V\) is the entire difference between the generic and interpretable variants.

    The block loss #

    Because the head is linear, the block's objective is least squares in coefficient space plus a backcast penalty:

    \[L_k=\frac{1}{H}\lVert f_k-y_k\rVert_2^2 +\lambda\,\frac{1}{L}\lVert b_k-x_{k-1}\rVert_2^2, \qquad (5)\]

    where \(y_k\) is the target still unexplained when block \(k\) is trained and \(\lambda\) is backcast_weight. Both terms are averaged over the batch so that the gradient scale does not depend on the number of windows.

    Sequential training #

    The implementation trains the blocks greedily rather than jointly. Each block is fitted, then the running forecast and the running target are both reduced:

    \[y_{k+1}=y_k-f_k(x_{k-1}),\qquad x_k=x_{k-1}-b_k(x_{k-1}). \qquad (6)\]

    Reducing the target is the step that is easy to get wrong. If every block is trained against the original target, the forecasts add up and the stack over-predicts; the loss rises with every additional block. Training against \(y_k\) makes the contributions complementary by construction.

    The generic basis #

    For generic, both bases are identities:

    \[V^{f}=I_H,\qquad V^{b}=I_L . \qquad (7)\]

    The coefficients are then the forecast itself, so the linear head is just another fully connected layer. This is the most flexible setting and the one the paper uses for its pure accuracy benchmark.

    The trend basis #

    For a trend block of degree \(p\), let \(u_t=(t-L)/L\) so that the last observed step is \(u=0\) and the previous steps are negative. Then

    \[V^{b}_{t,j}=u_t^{\,j},\qquad V^{f}_{h,j}=\Bigl(\frac{h}{L}\Bigr)^{j}, \qquad j=0,\dots,p . \qquad (8)\]

    Both bases live in the same variable, so a linear coefficient vector continues the line into the future instead of restarting it. An earlier version placed the backcast at \(t/L\in(0,1]\) and the forecast at \((L+h)/L\approx10\); the two scales were so different that the polynomial coefficients had to cancel almost exactly, and the fit was numerically useless. Anchoring both at the end of the window fixes that.

    The seasonal basis #

    For a seasonal block with period \(m\) and \(J\) harmonics,

    \[V^{b}_{t,2j-1}=\cos\frac{2\pi jt}{m},\quad V^{b}_{t,2j}=\sin\frac{2\pi jt}{m}, \qquad (9)\]

    and the forecast basis uses the same formula at \(t=L+1,\dots,L+H\). The phase is therefore continuous across the boundary between the window and the horizon, which is what lets a block extrapolate a cycle it saw in the past.

    Standardisation #

    Each window is centred and scaled by its own statistics before it reaches the network:

    \[\tilde x=\frac{x-\bar x}{s_x},\qquad \tilde y=\frac{y-\bar y}{s_x}, \qquad (10)\]

    with \(s_x\) the window standard deviation, floored at \(10^{-8}\). Using the input window's scale for the target keeps the signal-to-noise ratio of a window intact: a quiet window and a volatile window both become unit-variance problems, and the same weights serve both.

    Backpropagation through a ReLU layer #

    With \(\delta_\ell\) the gradient of the loss with respect to the pre-activation \(z_\ell\),

    \[\delta_\ell=\bigl(W_{\ell+1}^{\top}\delta_{\ell+1}\bigr)\odot \mathbf 1[z_\ell>0],\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial W_\ell}=\delta_\ell a_{\ell-1}^{\top}, \qquad (11)\]

    where \(a_{\ell-1}\) is the previous activation. The code first forms the gradient with respect to the last hidden layer by combining the two heads, then walks backwards applying (11) once per layer.

    Adam #

    Every parameter is updated by

    \[m_t=\beta_1m_{t-1}+(1-\beta_1)g_t,\qquad v_t=\beta_2v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_2)g_t^2, \qquad (12)\]
    \[\theta_t=\theta_{t-1}-\eta\, \frac{m_t/(1-\beta_1^t)}{\sqrt{v_t/(1-\beta_2^t)}+\epsilon}, \qquad (13)\]

    with \(\beta_1=0.9\), \(\beta_2=0.999\) and \(\epsilon=10^{-8}\). The bias correction matters here because every block is trained for a few hundred full-batch steps, not hundreds of thousands; without it the first steps are far too large.

    The exogenous head #

    For nbeatsx, let \(X\) stack the standardised exogenous rows that lie inside each forecast window and \(r\) the residual \(y-\hat y\) of the block stack. The head is

    \[\hat\beta=\arg\min_\beta \lVert r-X\beta\rVert_2^2+\lambda\lVert \beta\rVert_2^2, \qquad \beta=(X^{\top}X+\lambda P)^{-1}X^{\top}r, \qquad (14)\]

    with \(P\) the identity except for a zero at the intercept. The fit alternates: fit the head, subtract its contribution from the target, retrain the stack, refit the head. Three rounds bring the coefficient from \(1.44\) (one round) to \(2.73\) against a true effect of three, because later rounds stop asking the blocks to memorise a driver they cannot generalise.

    The ensemble #

    The ensemble variant trains \(S\) stacks that differ only in their seeds and reports

    \[\hat y^{\mathrm{ens}}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\hat y^{(s)}. \qquad (15)\]

    Code walkthrough #

    The basis is the only place where the variants differ. The trend branch anchors the polynomial at the end of the window so that both bases share one variable:

            scale = float(length)
        back = (np.arange(1, length + 1, dtype=float) - length) / scale
        fore = np.arange(1, horizon + 1, dtype=float) / scale
        powers = range(degree + 1)
        return (np.column_stack([back ** power for power in powers]),
                np.column_stack([fore ** power for power in powers]))

    The seasonal branch evaluates the same Fourier functions at absolute times on both sides of the boundary, which is what makes the phase continuous:

        back = 2.0 * np.pi * np.arange(1, length + 1, dtype=float) / period
    fore = 2.0 * np.pi * np.arange(length + 1, length + horizon + 1,
                                   dtype=float) / period

    The forward pass keeps every pre-activation and activation, because the backward pass needs both: the activation for the weight gradient and the sign of the pre-activation for the ReLU mask:

        def _forward(self, values: np.ndarray):
        activations = [values]
        pre = []
        current = values
        for weight, bias in zip(self.weights, self.biases, strict=True):
            current = current @ weight.T + bias
            pre.append(current)
            current = np.maximum(current, 0.0)
            activations.append(current)
        theta_fore = current @ self.head_fore.T + self.offset_fore
        theta_back = current @ self.head_back.T + self.offset_back
        return theta_fore, theta_back, activations, pre

    Both heads read the same hidden state, which is the point: a block must reconstruct its input with one head while forecasting the target with the other, and the shared representation forces those two jobs to agree.

    The backward pass starts at the heads and folds the two gradients into one before entering the stack. The loop is (11) applied in reverse:

                top = activations[-1]
            weight_grads: list[np.ndarray | None] = [None] * self.depth
            bias_grads: list[np.ndarray | None] = [None] * self.depth
            hidden = (theta_fore_grad @ self.head_fore
                      + theta_back_grad @ self.head_back)
            for layer in range(self.depth - 1, -1, -1):
                delta = hidden * (pre[layer] > 0.0)
                weight_grads[layer] = delta.T @ activations[layer]
                bias_grads[layer] = delta.sum(axis=0)
                if layer > 0:
                    hidden = delta @ self.weights[layer]

    In-place parameter -= ... works because the parameters are numpy arrays stored in a list; the lists of moments are built with np.zeros_like, so the shapes cannot drift out of sync with the weights:

                for index, (parameter, gradient) in enumerate(
                    zip(parameters, gradients, strict=True)):
                moment1[index] = beta1 * moment1[index] + (1.0 - beta1) * gradient
                moment2[index] = beta2 * moment2[index] + (1.0 - beta2) * gradient ** 2
                corrected1 = moment1[index] / (1.0 - beta1 ** epoch)
                corrected2 = moment2[index] / (1.0 - beta2 ** epoch)
                parameter -= (learning_rate * corrected1
                              / (np.sqrt(corrected2) + epsilon))

    The stack is where the residual idea lives. remaining tracks the target the next block still has to explain; residual tracks the input it still sees. Both are reduced by the same block's output, and the forecast accumulates:

        def fit(self, inputs: np.ndarray, targets: np.ndarray) -> _Stack:
        residual = inputs.copy()
        remaining = targets.copy()
        forecast = np.zeros_like(targets)
        for block in self.blocks:
            block.fit(residual, remaining, epochs=self.epochs,
                      learning_rate=self.learning_rate,
                      backcast_weight=self.backcast_weight)
            contribution = block.forecast(residual)
            forecast = forecast + contribution
            remaining = remaining - contribution
            residual = residual - block.backcast(residual)
        self.loss_ = float(np.mean((forecast - targets) ** 2))
        return self

    Prediction is the same loop without training and without the target: only the backcast chain survives, because there is no future target to compare against:

        def predict(self, values: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        residual = values.copy()
        output = np.zeros((values.shape[0], self.horizon))
        for block in self.blocks:
            output = output + block.forecast(residual)
            residual = residual - block.backcast(residual)
        return output

    The exogenous head alternates between the regression and the blocks. The design matrix and its Gram matrix do not depend on the network, so they are computed once outside the loop:

            adjusted = targets.copy()
        contribution = np.zeros_like(targets)
        for _ in range(3):
            self.stack_ = self._new_stack(self.seed).fit(inputs, adjusted)
            base = self._normalised_forecasts(inputs) * scale + mean
            residual = targets * scale + mean - base
            coef = np.linalg.solve(gram, design.T @ residual.reshape(-1))
            contribution = (design @ coef).reshape(targets.shape)
            adjusted = targets - contribution / scale

    The gram matrix already contains the ridge penalty, so the solve is a single linear system per round. Subtracting contribution / scale converts the original-unit correction back into the standardised space the blocks train in.

    At prediction time the window is standardised with its own statistics, not with the training statistics, because that is what the blocks were trained on:

            window = values[-self.seq_len:]
        mean = float(window.mean())
        spread = float(window.std())
        if spread <= 1e-8:
            spread = 1.0
        normalised = (window - mean) / spread
        forecast = self._normalised_forecasts(normalised[None, :])[0]
        out = mean + spread * forecast
        if self.variant == "nbeatsx":
            design = self._exog_design(exog)
            out = out + design @ self.exog_coef_

    Using the training mean and scale here would break on a series with a slow level shift, and using the global standard deviation would make the forecast scale with the training period rather than with the recent window.

    Complexity #

    With \(n\) observations, \(W\) windows, \(L\) look-back, \(H\) horizon, \(K\) blocks, \(P\) parameters per block and \(E\) epochs:

    • Windowing: \(O(W(L+H))\) time and memory to materialise the sliding windows.
    • One forward pass: \(O(W(LP+H d_f+L d_b))\), dominated by the first weight matrix because \(L\) is the largest dimension.
    • One epoch: roughly three forward passes' worth of work for the backward pass, so about \(4\times\) the forward cost.
    • One block: \(O(EWLP)\); the demo's 301 windows, \(L=36\), width 24 and 150 epochs take a few milliseconds.
    • Full fit: \(O(KEWLP)\) for a stack, multiplied by \(S\) for the ensemble and by three for the alternating exogenous rounds of nbeatsx.
    • Prediction: \(O(KLP)\), independent of the training size.

    The whole demo — ten stack trainings including the deeper one — runs in about \(1.5\) seconds in pure numpy. The cost is linear in the number of blocks but cubic in the width only through the hidden-to-hidden matrices, which are small.

    Failure modes #

    The residual target is the easy bug. Training every block against the original target makes the stack additive in the wrong way: each block predicts the whole signal, the forecasts are summed, and the error grows with depth. The symptom is a training loss that increases as blocks are added. The fix is the remaining update in (6); after it, adding a block can only help the in-sample fit.

    The basis can be numerically catastrophic. A trend basis evaluated at \(t/L\) on the window and \((L+h)/L\) on the horizon spans a factor of ten in scale. The coefficients must cancel to four or five digits, so the forecast is dominated by rounding and by any slight miscalibration. Anchoring both bases at the end of the window, as in (8), is not cosmetic; without it the interpretable model is worse than seasonal naive.

    Depth buys in-sample fit, not accuracy. Four blocks of width 32 reach a train MSE of \(0.0158\) against \(0.0279\) for two blocks of width 24, but their held-out MAE rises from \(0.2466\) to \(0.3396\). The blocks have enough capacity to memorise individual windows. Early stopping, a wider validation split, or a smaller width is cheaper than adding blocks.

    The exogenous head is only as good as the alternation. Fitting the head once on the residuals of a fully trained stack gives a coefficient of about half the truth, because the blocks have already memorised part of the driver in sample. The three-round alternation in (14) largely fixes this, but the coefficient is still attenuated (\(2.73\) against \(3.00\)), and a driver that interacts with the target nonlinearly will not be captured by a linear head at all.

    Ensembles do not fix a biased member. Averaging reduces variance, not bias. If every seed overfits the same windows, the members are correlated and the ensemble is barely better than one of them: the demo's improvement is \(0.2408\) against a member mean of \(0.2369\). Gain comes from diversity in the initialisation and architecture, not from the number of seeds alone.

    One window's scale is one window's noise. Standardising each window by its own standard deviation makes the model robust to level and amplitude changes, but a window that happens to be unusually quiet gets amplified. On series with bursty variance this produces unstable forecasts near the end of the sample; clipping the scale, or standardising by a longer rolling estimate, is the usual remedy.

    The horizon is baked into the basis. A model trained with \(H=12\) has a forecast basis of twelve columns and cannot emit a different horizon. Recursive multi-step forecasting with a one-step model is an alternative, but it compounds errors, and it discards the direct multi-horizon structure that makes N-BEATS accurate.

    Applications #

    N-BEATS placed first in the M4 competition's pure machine-learning track and is used in production for retail demand, electricity load and financial volatility, usually as a strong benchmark that classical models must beat. The interpretable configuration is attractive where a forecast has to be explained to a planner: the trend block gives a slope, the seasonal block gives amplitudes, and the remainder is visible.

    The nbeatsx extension fits settings where future covariates are known — promotions and price plans in retail, calendar and weather forecasts in energy, scheduled maintenance in industrial telemetry. The covariate path is the reason the method is used instead of a pure autoregression: the model can react to a known future shock that never appears in its own history.

    The method is the wrong tool when the series is very short (a few dozen points, where a neural network has nothing to learn), when the data are intermittent (use Croston or TSB), or when a fully probabilistic forecast is required. It produces point forecasts and, at best, an ensemble spread; it does not give a likelihood. For intervals, combine it with conformal prediction or use a probabilistic model such as DeepAR.
