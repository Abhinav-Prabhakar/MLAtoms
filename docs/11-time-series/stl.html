    STL — seasonal-trend decomposition by smoothing, not by a model

    Intuition #

    STL is the answer to a practical question: given a series that clearly has a trend and a repeating shape, how do you split it into those two parts without assuming a parametric model for either? Cleveland's method is iterative backfitting. Guess the trend, subtract it, smooth each seasonal subseries, remove the low-frequency part of that seasonal estimate, subtract the result, and smooth the remainder to update the trend. Repeat.

    Two details make it work. The first is cycle-subseries smoothing: with period \(m\), split the data into \(m\) sequences (all Januaries, all Februaries, ...). Each sequence is short and smooth, so a local regression with a modest window captures how that month's effect evolves over the years. The second is the low-pass filter: the raw seasonal estimate also contains slow drift, which must be removed before the seasonal component is subtracted from the series. STL does that with three moving averages followed by a local regression, and the result is the part of the seasonal estimate that is genuinely periodic.

    The variants are the practical extensions of that skeleton:

    • robust adds an outer loop that downweights outliers, so a handful of spikes cannot bend the trend or inflate a month's seasonal effect.
    • mstl repeats the decomposition for several periods, subtracting the other seasonal components before estimating each one.
    • seasonal_windows chooses a separate seasonal smoothing window for each subseries by leave-one-out cross-validation, instead of forcing all months to share one window.

    The demo checks the split against the simulation truth, not just the additive identity: the identity holds by construction, while the correlation and RMSE figures are what would look wrong if the smoothing were broken.

    Variants #

    variant change demo result
    stl base iterative decomposition profile \(r=0.9993\)
    robust bisquare outer weights outlier weight 0.0000
    mstl several periods weekly \(r=0.9999\)
    seasonal_windows per-subseries span by CV RMSE 0.4738 vs 0.5024

    stl is the default. On 240 points with a linear trend, two harmonics and noise with variance \(0.1225\), it recovers the seasonal profile with correlation \(0.9993\) and the trend with RMSE \(0.1189\). The remainder variance is \(0.0665\), below the noise variance because the smoothers absorb some of the noise — a reminder that a low remainder variance is not by itself evidence of a good decomposition.

    robust fits the same series after twenty observations are shifted by \(+15\). The final weights at those observations are exactly zero, the trend RMSE falls from \(1.5137\) to \(0.1719\), and the seasonal RMSE falls as well. The remainder sum of squares is larger (\(4670.79\) against \(2491.88\)), because the outliers stay in the remainder; robustness protects the components, it does not remove the data.

    mstl handles a weekly and a monthly cycle on 420 daily points. The two extracted components correlate \(0.9999\) and \(0.9995\) with the truth, and the remainder variance is \(0.0213\) against a noise variance of \(0.0625\). It works by estimating each period on the series with the other seasonal components subtracted, then repeating the pass once.

    seasonal_windows is for the case where one part of the year is much noisier than another. The demo makes month 3 four times as noisy as the others; the variant chooses windows \((11,11,11,11,5,11,9,11,9,5,11,11)\) and lowers the seasonal RMSE from \(0.5024\) to \(0.4738\). Its in-sample RSS is slightly higher (\(138.84\) against \(132.39\)) because a smoother seasonal fit is not the same as a lower residual; the cross-validated window is choosing bias over variance.

    Derivation #

    The decomposition #

    All variants target the additive identity

    \[y_t=T_t+S_t+R_t, \qquad (1)\]

    where \(T\) is the trend, \(S\) is seasonal with period \(m\), and \(R\) is what is left. The identity is definitional: once \(T\) and \(S\) are chosen, \(R\) is determined. The modelling content is entirely in the two smoothers.

    Local regression #

    Both smoothers are LOESS. For a target \(z\), fit a local polynomial of degree \(d\) by weighted least squares:

    \[\min_{\beta_0,\beta_1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i(z) \bigl(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1(x_i-z)\bigr)^2, \qquad (2)\]

    and evaluate at \(z\) as \(\hat\beta_0\). The weights are tricube in the distance to the \(k\)-th nearest point:

    \[w_i(z)=\left(1-\left(\frac{\lvert x_i-z\rvert}{h(z)}\right)^3\right)^3 \mathbf 1[\lvert x_i-z\rvert

    where \(h(z)\) is the distance to the farthest of the \(k\) selected neighbours and \(k\) is the span. Only \(k\) points receive nonzero weight, so the cost per target is \(O(k)\) plus the small least-squares solve. Degree \(1\) follows local slopes; degree \(0\) is a weighted mean. The code uses degree \(1\) for the trend and for the seasonal subseries, and degree \(0\) when a subseries is treated as periodic or has fewer points than the requested span.

    Cycle-subseries smoothing #

    For each phase \(j=0,\dots,m-1\), let \(\mathcal I_j=\{t:t\equiv j\pmod m\}\) and let \(y^{(j)}\) be that subsequence. Smooth it with span \(s\):

    \[C_t=\mathrm{Loess}\bigl(\{y_i\}_{i\in\mathcal I_j}\bigr)(t), \qquad t\in\mathcal I_j, \qquad (4)\]

    and extrapolate the local fit one period before and one period after the observed range. The extended estimate \(\tilde C\) has length \(n+2m\); this is why the code builds positions = np.arange(-period + 1, n + period). The extrapolation matters at the boundaries, where the seasonal component would otherwise be undefined for the first and last partial cycle.

    Low-pass filtering #

    The extended seasonal estimate still contains slow variation. Remove it with

    \[L=\mathrm{Loess}_{l}\Bigl(\mathrm{MA}_{3} \bigl(\mathrm{MA}_{m}(\mathrm{MA}_{m}(\tilde C))\bigr)\Bigr), \qquad (5)\]

    where \(\mathrm{MA}_{k}\) is a centred moving average of length \(k\) and \(\mathrm{Loess}_{l}\) is a local linear smoother with span \(l\). The two length-\(m\) averages annihilate the period-\(m\) component; the length-\(3\) average removes the remaining high-frequency ripple; the final LOESS smooths what is left. The seasonal component is then

    \[S_t=\tilde C_t-L_t \qquad (6)\]

    on the observed range. Subtracting \(L\) is what prevents the seasonal component from absorbing the trend, and it is the step most naive implementations omit.

    Trend update #

    Deseasonalise and smooth:

    \[T_t=\mathrm{Loess}_{t}(y-S)(t). \qquad (7)\]

    The pair (6)–(7) is one inner iteration. STL runs it inner times (two by default), which is enough because the two smoothers are contractions on the components they do not own.

    Robust weights #

    After an inner pass, compute the remainder \(R=y-S-T\) and the bisquare weights

    \[w_t=\left(1-\left(\frac{\lvert R_t\rvert}{6\,\mathrm{median}\lvert R\rvert} \right)^2\right)^2 \mathbf 1\bigl[\lvert R_t\rvert<6\,\mathrm{median}\lvert R\rvert\bigr]. \qquad (8)\]

    These weights multiply the LOESS weights in (2) on the next outer pass. An observation beyond six median absolute deviations gets weight zero and cannot influence the seasonal or trend estimate. The median scale is recomputed each outer iteration, so the procedure adapts to the remaining noise level.

    Multiple periods #

    For periods \(m_1,\dots,m_P\), MSTL estimates one component at a time on the series with the others removed:

    \[S^{(k)}=\mathrm{STL}_{m_k}\Bigl(y-\sum_{j\ne k}S^{(j)}\Bigr), \qquad S=\sum_{k=1}^{P}S^{(k)} . \qquad (9)\]

    The code makes two passes over the periods, shortest to longest. The first pass gives each component a rough estimate; the second re-estimates it with the other components already subtracted. The trend is then smoothed on \(y-S\).

    Choosing seasonal windows #

    For seasonal_windows, each subseries gets its own span. For a candidate span \(s\), the leave-one-out error is

    \[\mathrm{CV}(s)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_j} \bigl(y_i-\hat y_i^{(-i)}(s)\bigr)^2, \qquad (10)\]

    where \(\hat y_i^{(-i)}\) is the local fit at \(i\) with point \(i\) removed. The variant evaluates five odd spans around the requested one, picks the minimiser for each phase, and uses that vector in the main decomposition. The candidate with span at least the subseries length is the periodic option, fitted as a weighted mean.

    Code walkthrough #

    The moving average is deliberately simple: reflect at the edges, then convolve. Reflection keeps the output the same length as the input and avoids the shrinking-window bias that a truncated average would introduce:

    def _moving_average(values: np.ndarray, length: int) -> np.ndarray:
    if length <= 1:
        return values.copy()
    pad = length // 2
    padded = np.pad(values, pad, mode="reflect")
    kernel = np.ones(length) / length
    return np.convolve(padded, kernel, mode="valid")

    The low-pass filter calls this three times. The first two calls use the seasonal period, which is what kills the seasonal signal; the third uses length three, which is what removes the ripple left by the first two.

    The LOESS core selects the nearest span points by absolute distance and forms tricube weights. The design is centred at the target, so the intercept is the prediction and the slope is only a nuisance parameter:

            distance = np.abs(x - target)
        take = np.argpartition(distance, span - 1)[:span]
        local = distance[take]
        radius = float(local.max())
        if radius <= 0.0:
            out[row] = float(np.average(y[take]))
            continue
        shape = np.clip(local / radius, 0.0, 1.0)
        w = (1.0 - shape ** 3) ** 3
        if weights is not None:
            w = w * weights[take]

    np.argpartition is \(O(n)\) per target instead of the \(O(n\log n)\) of a full sort, which matters because the trend smoother evaluates every observation. The radius <= 0 branch handles a target that coincides with every selected point, where dividing by the radius would be undefined.

    Cycle-subseries smoothing is the only place that knows about phases. The modular mask picks the targets that belong to phase \(j\), and the branch on seasonal_window implements the periodic option:

            for j in range(period):
            index = np.arange(j, n, period)
            if index.size < 2:
                continue
            targets = positions[(positions - j) % period == 0]
            if seasonal_window is None:
                span, degree = index.size, 0
            else:
                span = int(windows[j]) if windows is not None else seasonal_window
                degree = self.seasonal_degree
                if span >= index.size:
                    span, degree = index.size, 0

    Passing windows=None gives every phase the same span; passing a vector is what seasonal_windows does. The index.size < 2 guard skips a phase that appears only once, where a local fit is not identified.

    The low-pass filter is a compact transcription of (5), with the final LOESS evaluated on the same grid:

        def _low_pass(self, cycles: np.ndarray, period: int,
                  window: int) -> np.ndarray:
        values = _moving_average(cycles, period)
        values = _moving_average(values, period)
        values = _moving_average(values, 3)
        grid = np.arange(values.size, dtype=float)
        return _loess(grid, values, grid, window, 1)

    The input is the extended cycle estimate, so the output is also extended. The caller trims start = period - 1 from both the cycle estimate and the low-pass output, which aligns the first observation with index \(m-1\) of the extended grid.

    One inner iteration is four lines of component algebra:

            for _ in range(inner):
            detrended = y - trend
            cycles = self._cycle_smooth(detrended, period, windows,
                                        seasonal_window, weights)
            low = self._low_pass(cycles, period, low_pass_window)
            start = period - 1
            seasonal = cycles[start:start + n] - low[start:start + n]
            deseasonalised = y - seasonal
            trend = _loess(np.arange(n, dtype=float), deseasonalised,
                           np.arange(n, dtype=float), trend_window,
                           self.trend_degree, weights)

    detrended is the input to the seasonal smoother, not the original series; deseasonalised is the input to the trend smoother. Swapping those two lines produces a decomposition that still sums to \(y\) but has the trend and seasonal roles exchanged, which is why the identity alone is not a test.

    The robust outer loop recomputes the scale from the current remainder and restarts the inner loop with the new weights:

    def _bisquare(remainder: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    scale = 6.0 * float(np.median(np.abs(remainder)))
    if scale <= 0.0:
        return np.ones(remainder.size)
    shape = np.abs(remainder) / scale
    return np.where(shape < 1.0, (1.0 - shape ** 2) ** 2, 0.0)

    The scale <= 0 case is not hypothetical: a perfectly periodic series with no noise has a zero remainder, and the weights should then be all ones rather than a division by zero.

    MSTL is two passes over the periods, each estimating one component on the partial series:

            for _ in range(2):
            for k, period in enumerate(periods):
                partial = y.copy()
                for other, component in enumerate(seasonals):
                    if other != k:
                        partial = partial - component
                component, _, _ = self._run(
                    partial, period, None, self.seasonal_window_,
                    self.trend_window_, self.low_pass_window_, 1)
                seasonals[k] = component

    The None for windows is deliberate: each period has its own subseries length, so a single window vector of length self.period would index the wrong phase. The two passes are enough to separate cycles that are not exact multiples; cycles that share a harmonic, such as 7 and 14, are not identifiable from the sum alone.

    The window selection is a leave-one-out loop with five candidates:

                best, best_error = values.size, np.inf
            for span in candidates:
                error = self._cv_error(values, span)
                if error < best_error:
                    best, best_error = span, error
            chosen[j] = min(best, values.size)

    _cv_error refits the subseries with one point held out and predicts it, which is why the selected window can differ from phase to phase. The min against values.size keeps a span from exceeding the number of available points; the smoother itself also clamps, so this is belt and braces.

    Complexity #

    With \(n\) observations, period \(m\), seasonal span \(s\), trend span \(t\), inner iterations \(i\) and outer iterations \(o\):

    • One LOESS evaluation: \(O(k)\) to find neighbours plus \(O(k)\) for the weighted fit, with \(k\) the span.
    • Cycle-subseries smoothing: \(m\) subseries, each with about \(n/m\) points; one pass costs \(O(n s)\).
    • Low-pass: three moving averages at \(O(n)\) plus one LOESS at \(O(n l)\).
    • Trend smoothing: \(O(n t)\).
    • One inner pass: \(O(n(s+t+l))\).
    • Full fit: \(O(i(1+o)\,n(s+t+l))\).
    • mstl: \(P\) periods, two passes, so \(O(2P\,i\,n(s+t+l))\).
    • seasonal_windows: the CV selection adds \(O(m\,C\,n_j^2)\) for \(C\) candidates and \(n_j\approx n/m\) points per subseries, plus the fit.
    • Memory: \(O(n)\) for the components and \(O(n)\) for the extended cycle estimate.

    The demo's 240-point fit is dominated by the trend LOESS and runs in well under a second. The cubic term is in the robust outer count, not in \(n\): a series with 100 000 points and 15 outer iterations is still a linear scan per smoother, but the Python loops make it slow enough that a production implementation would batch the local fits.

    Failure modes #

    A wrong period splits one seasonal shape into two. If the true period is 12 and you use 6, the smoother can represent the fundamental but not the harmonic, so the leftover harmonic leaks into the trend and the remainder. The diagnostic is a trend that oscillates with the seasonal period, and a remainder with a large lag-\(m\) autocorrelation. Plot the trend's first difference; a periodic zig-zag there means the period is wrong.

    A short trend window absorbs the seasonality. The trend smoother and the seasonal smoother compete for the same low-frequency variation. If trend is close to period, the trend can follow the seasonal cycle and the seasonal component collapses. Keep the trend window well above the period — the default rule uses \(1.5m/(1-1.5/s)\) — and check that the seasonal profile has the amplitude you expect.

    Robustness is not outlier removal. The demo's robust fit has a larger remainder RSS than the non-robust one because the spikes remain in \(R\). If the goal is a clean series for forecasting, the outliers must be removed or modeled separately; robust STL only stops them from corrupting \(T\) and \(S\). The weight vector is the diagnostic: weights near zero mark the observations the fit ignored.

    MSTL cannot separate shared harmonics. Periods 7 and 14, or 12 and 24, produce seasonal subspaces that overlap. The two-pass subtraction then has an identifiability problem: many splits of the same signal fit equally well. Use the smallest set of periods that spans the observed cycles, and check that each extracted component has the expected shape rather than trusting the sum.

    Boundary extrapolation is an assumption. The cycle smoother extrapolates one period beyond each end, and the moving averages reflect at the edges. Both are reasonable but neither is estimated from data. The first and last few seasonal estimates are therefore less reliable than the middle, which matters when STL is used to seasonally adjust the most recent observations for a nowcast.

    Applications #

    STL is the standard preprocessing step for seasonal economic data. Statistical agencies decompose industrial production, retail sales and employment into trend, seasonal and irregular components; the seasonally adjusted series is then forecast with ARIMA or an exponential smoothing model on the adjusted scale. Its appeal is transparency: there is no likelihood, no latent state and no convergence to a local optimum, only two smoothers and a loop.

    In energy and utilities it separates weather-driven seasonal demand from the trend, and in traffic forecasting it removes the weekly and daily cycles before a short-horizon model. In sensor networks it is used to remove diurnal drift from a slowly failing sensor, where the robust variant matters because glitches are common. MSTL is the natural choice for series with more than one cycle, such as hourly electricity demand with daily and weekly periods.

    It is the wrong tool when you need a probabilistic forecast: STL produces components, not predictive distributions. It is also weak when the seasonality changes shape abruptly, because every subseries is smoothed with a fixed window (unless seasonal_windows is used) and the method has no notion of a breakpoint. For those cases a state-space decomposition or a regression with time-varying seasonal terms is more appropriate.
