    11 · Time series/tier 3/4 variants/source

    PatchTST — patch the window, attend within it, share across channels

    Intuition #

    A transformer on a long univariate series has a problem: the sequence of timesteps is short and each token is a single scalar. Attention over a thousand barely-informative scalars is expensive, and the self-attention pattern has little to latch onto. PatchTST changes the unit of attention from the timestep to the patch: cut the look-back window into overlapping chunks of twelve observations, embed each chunk, and attend over a handful of tokens instead of hundreds.

    Two consequences follow. First, a patch carries local shape — level, slope, curvature — so the token is informative before any attention happens. Second, the sequence length collapses from \(L\) to roughly \(L/P\), so the cost of attention falls by a factor of \(P^2\) and the model can afford a longer look-back.

    The second idea is channel independence. Given a multivariate series, do not mix the channels: treat each one as its own univariate problem and share the weights. The forecast for one channel never depends on the others, which is a strong prior — it says the relationship between channels is not something the model should learn. It sounds like a limitation, and it is, but it is also the regularisation that makes PatchTST hard to beat on standard benchmarks: a channel-mixing model can overfit spurious cross-correlations, while the independent one cannot.

    The variants cover the training protocol:

    • patchtst is the supervised model on one channel.
    • channel_independent applies the identical model to every channel with one set of weights, so permuting the channels permutes the forecast.
    • supervised masks patches, reconstructs them, then fine-tunes every weight on the forecasting loss.
    • self_supervised masks patches, then freezes the representation and fits only a linear head — the linear-probe test of what the pretraining learned.

    The demo trains on 348 points and holds out 12. The supervised model reaches a held-out MAE of \(0.2956\); fine-tuning after masked pretraining improves it to \(0.2691\), while a frozen linear probe reaches only \(0.4015\).

    Variants #

    variant representation trained held-out MAE
    patchtst one channel from scratch 0.2956
    channel_independent per channel, shared from scratch 0.9256 joint
    supervised masked pretraining all weights 0.2691
    self_supervised masked pretraining head only 0.4015

    patchtst builds 289 windows from the 348-point training span, cuts each 48-step window into four non-overlapping patches of twelve, and embeds them into 16 dimensions. The in-sample MSE is \(0.0561\) in per-window variance units, and the held-out MAE of \(0.2956\) beats both naive (\(1.5527\)) and seasonal naive (\(0.4627\)).

    channel_independent is fitted to two channels, the second an affine transform of the first plus small noise. The joint MAE of \(0.9256\) is dominated by the second channel, which has three times the scale. Swapping the columns of the input swaps the columns of the forecast with a maximum difference of exactly zero, because the channels never meet inside the network.

    supervised pretrains with 30% of the patches hidden and a reconstruction MSE of \(0.0713\), then fine-tunes every weight on the forecast. Fine-tuning moves the encoder by \(1.3013\) in maximum absolute value and lowers the held-out MAE from \(0.2956\) to \(0.2691\).

    self_supervised uses the same pretrained weights but freezes the encoder and the patch embedding, training only the \(12\times64\) linear head. The frozen representation changes by exactly zero, and the probe reaches \(0.4015\). On this small series the representation alone is weaker than supervised training — the pretraining has only 289 windows to learn from, and the probe cannot fix what the encoder never learned.

    Derivation #

    Patching #

    A window \(x\in\mathbb R^{L}\) is cut into \(N\) patches of length \(P\) with stride \(S\):

    \[N=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{L-P}{S}\Bigr\rfloor+1,\qquad p_i=x_{(i-1)S+1:(i-1)S+P},\qquad i=1,\dots,N . \qquad (1)\]

    With \(L=48\), \(P=12\) and \(S=12\) this gives \(N=4\) non-overlapping patches; with \(S=6\) it gives \(N=7\) overlapping ones. Non-overlapping patches are cheaper and, on the demo series, better: each patch then covers exactly one seasonal period.

    Patch embedding #

    Each patch is projected to a \(d\)-dimensional token and a learned positional vector is added:

    \[h_i=W_p p_i+b_p+e_i,\qquad W_p\in\mathbb R^{d\times P},\quad e_i\in\mathbb R^{d}. \qquad (2)\]

    The projection is linear, so the model can learn a filter bank — a patch of twelve points becomes twelve learned projections of that shape. No pooling is involved, which is why P is small; a large P would make \(W_p\) a wide matrix with little structure.

    Self-attention #

    With \(H_a\) heads and \(d_h=d/H_a\), the layer computes

    \[Q=hW_q^{\top}+b_q,\quad K=hW_k^{\top},\quad V=hW_v^{\top}+b_v, \qquad (3)\]

    and, per head,

    \[\mathrm{Attn}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}\!\Bigl(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_h}}\Bigr)V . \qquad (4)\]

    The key bias is omitted deliberately: adding a constant to a row of \(K\) adds a constant to every score in that row, and softmax is invariant to it. Including it would create a parameter with an identically zero gradient.

    The heads are concatenated and projected:

    \[\mathrm{MHA}(h)=\mathrm{Concat}\bigl(\mathrm{head}_1,\dots, \mathrm{head}_{H_a}\bigr)W_o^{\top}+b_o . \qquad (5)\]

    The encoder block #

    The implementation uses post-norm residual blocks with a ReLU feed-forward network:

    \[u=h+\mathrm{MHA}(\mathrm{LN}(h)), \qquad (6)\]
    \[\mathrm{Block}(h)=u+\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathrm{LN}(u)W_1^{\top}+b_1)W_2^{\top}+b_2 . \qquad (7)\]

    Layer normalisation is affine-free:

    \[\mathrm{LN}(z)=\frac{z-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}},\qquad \mu=\frac{1}{d}\sum_j z_j,\quad \sigma^2=\frac{1}{d}\sum_j (z_j-\mu)^2 . \qquad (8)\]

    Dropping the learnable gain and bias removes \(2d\) parameters per layer and, more importantly, removes two more gradient terms from an already long backward pass. On a model this small the gain is mostly in code clarity.

    The forecast head #

    The encoder output is flattened across patches and mapped to the horizon:

    \[\hat y=W_h\,\mathrm{vec}(h^{(N)})+b_h,\qquad W_h\in\mathbb R^{H\times Nd}. \qquad (9)\]

    Flattening rather than pooling keeps the position of each patch. A pooled representation would force the head to recover where in time each feature came from, which is exactly the information the head needs to place a forecast.

    The supervised objective #

    Training minimises the mean squared error over windows:

    \[L_{\mathrm{sup}}=\frac{1}{WH}\sum_{w=1}^{W}\lVert \hat y_w-y_w\rVert_2^2 . \qquad (10)\]

    Every parameter — patch projection, position, encoder, head — is updated by Adam on this loss.

    Channel independence #

    For a \(C\)-channel series, the channels are stacked into the batch:

    \[\{x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(C)}\}\longrightarrow \{(x^{(c)},y^{(c)})\}_{c=1}^{C}, \qquad (11)\]

    and the same weights are applied to each. The forecast is therefore equivariant to permutations of the channels: if \(\pi\) reorders the channels then \(\hat y(\pi x)=\pi\hat y(x)\). The demo checks this numerically and gets a difference of exactly zero, which is the strongest possible version of the claim.

    Masked pretraining #

    A mask \(m\in\{0,1\}^{N}\) hides a fraction \(\rho\) of the patches. The model sees \(x\odot(1-m)\) and must reconstruct the hidden patches:

    \[L_{\mathrm{mask}}= \frac{1}{|\mathcal M|P}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal M} \bigl(\hat p_{ij}-p_{ij}\bigr)^2, \qquad (12)\]

    where \(\mathcal M\) is the set of masked (patch, position) pairs. The mask is redrawn every epoch and every row is forced to hide at least one patch, so no window is left untouched.

    Linear probing #

    After pretraining, two protocols are possible. The supervised variant keeps the pretrained weights as an initialisation and fine-tunes everything on (10). The self_supervised variant freezes the encoder and the patch embedding and fits only \(W_h,b_h\):

    \[W_h^{\star}=\arg\min_{W_h}\sum_{w} \lVert W_h\,\mathrm{vec}(h_w)+b_h-y_w\rVert_2^2 . \qquad (13)\]

    Because the representation is fixed, (13) is a linear least-squares problem, and the probe measures the information the pretraining actually put into \(h\). The gap between the probe and fine-tuning is the value of the pretrained initialisation beyond its linear content.

    Code walkthrough #

    Patching is an index gather, which is both faster and clearer than a loop:

    def _patches(windows: np.ndarray, patch: int, stride: int) -> np.ndarray:
    count = windows.shape[0]
    number = _patch_count(windows.shape[1], patch, stride)
    starts = np.arange(number) * stride
    index = starts[:, None] + np.arange(patch)[None, :]
    return windows[:, index].reshape(count, number, patch)

    The gather produces (count, number, patch) directly, so no later reshape is needed. The same helper serves training windows and the single prediction window, which guarantees that the two views of a patch are identical.

    Layer normalisation is a free function rather than a layer, because it has no parameters. The cache keeps the centred input and the inverse standard deviation, which is everything the backward pass needs:

    def _layer_norm(x: np.ndarray):
    mean = x.mean(axis=-1, keepdims=True)
    centred = x - mean
    variance = (centred ** 2).mean(axis=-1, keepdims=True)
    inverse = 1.0 / np.sqrt(variance + 1e-5)
    return centred * inverse, (centred, inverse)

    The backward pass is the standard expression for a normalisation with no affine term. It has three pieces — the gradient itself, its mean, and its projection onto the centred input — because the normalisation couples every coordinate of a token:

    def _layer_norm_backward(gradient: np.ndarray, cache) -> np.ndarray:
    centred, inverse = cache
    width = gradient.shape[-1]
    scaled = (gradient * centred).sum(axis=-1, keepdims=True)
    total = gradient.sum(axis=-1, keepdims=True)
    return (inverse / width) * (
        width * gradient - total - centred * inverse ** 2 * scaled)

    Forgetting the total term is the usual bug: it still trains, but the gradient is wrong and the model silently learns a slightly different objective.

    The forward pass reads in the order of (3)–(7). Keeping the split heads in the cache lets the backward pass avoid re-splitting:

            normalised, norm_cache = _layer_norm(x)
        query = normalised @ p["Wq"] + p["bq"]
        key = normalised @ p["Wk"]
        value = normalised @ p["Wv"] + p["bv"]
        qh = _split_heads(query, self.heads)
        kh = _split_heads(key, self.heads)
        vh = _split_heads(value, self.heads)
        scores = qh @ kh.transpose(0, 1, 3, 2) / np.sqrt(self.width)
        attention = _softmax(scores)
        mixed = attention @ vh
        joined = _merge_heads(mixed, self.dim)
        attended = joined @ p["Wo"] + p["bo"]
        first = x + attended

    The attention backward is where most mistakes happen. The softmax Jacobian is not the elementwise product with 1 - a; it is \(\partial a_i/\partial s_j=a_i(\delta_{ij}-a_j)\), which collapses to \(a\odot(g-\langle g,a\rangle)\) when contracted with an upstream gradient:

            attention_grad = mixed_grad @ vh.transpose(0, 1, 3, 2)
        value_grad = attention.transpose(0, 1, 3, 2) @ mixed_grad
        centre = (attention_grad * attention).sum(axis=-1, keepdims=True)
        scores_grad = attention * (attention_grad - centre) / np.sqrt(self.width)
        query_grad = scores_grad @ kh
        key_grad = scores_grad.transpose(0, 1, 3, 2) @ qh

    The numeric gradient check in development matched all eleven parameter arrays to about \(10^{-8}\), which is what the finite-difference tolerance allows.

    Masked pretraining hides patches by zeroing their embedding input. A zero patch is not a special token — the mask is communicated by the position of the zero, and the reconstruction head learns to invert it:

                mask = self._rng.random((batch, count)) < self.mask_ratio
            empty = ~mask.any(axis=1)
            if empty.any():
                columns = self._rng.integers(0, count, size=int(empty.sum()))
                mask[empty, columns] = True
            visible = np.where(mask[:, :, None], 0.0, patches)

    Forcing at least one masked patch per row matters when \(N\) is small: with four patches and \(\rho=0.3\), the probability that a row hides nothing is \(0.7^4\approx0.24\), so without the guard a quarter of the batch would contribute no gradient.

    The two training protocols differ by one boolean. When it is off, the optimiser is given only the head, so the encoder is not merely frozen by a small learning rate — it is absent from the update entirely:

            parameters = {**self.head_parameters}
        if train_encoder:
            parameters = {**parameters, **self.embed_parameters,
                          **self.encoder.parameters}
        combined = _Adam(parameters, self.learning_rate)

    That is why the demo can assert an exact zero change in the frozen representation: no code path touches those arrays.

    Channel independence is implemented by folding the channel axis into the batch before patching and unfolding it after the head:

            windows = left.transpose(0, 2, 1).reshape(-1, self.seq_len)
        targets = right.transpose(0, 2, 1).reshape(-1, self.horizon)

    transpose(0, 2, 1) turns (window, time, channel) into (window, channel, time), so the reshape interleaves channels into the batch rather than mixing them into the patch. Every channel then becomes an independent row of the same matrix product.

    Complexity #

    With \(W\) windows, \(L\) look-back, \(N\) patches, patch size \(P\), width \(d\), \(H_a\) heads, feed-forward width \(f\) and \(E\) epochs:

    • Patching: \(O(WNP)\) memory for the gathered array.
    • Embedding: \(O(WNdP)\) for the projection and \(O(WNd)\) for the position.
    • Attention scores: \(O(WN^2d)\) time and \(O(WH_aN^2)\) memory for the attention maps; this is the term that patching shrinks, since \(N\approx L/P\).
    • Feed-forward: \(O(WNdf)\).
    • One epoch: \(O(WN(dP+Nd+df))\), dominated by \(dP\) and \(df\).
    • Full supervised fit: the above times \(E\).
    • Pretraining: the same per epoch over pretrain_epochs, plus the reconstruction head.
    • Prediction: one window, so \(O(NdP+N^2d+Ndf)\) and independent of the training size.

    With \(L=48\), \(N=4\), \(d=16\) and 289 windows, an epoch is a few hundred microseconds; the demo's four fits, including pretraining, take about \(4.5\) seconds. The patch count is the lever: stride six doubles \(N\) and roughly quadruples the attention cost for a small accuracy change.

    Failure modes #

    A patch shorter than the season is blind to it. With \(P=8\) and a period of twelve, no patch contains a full cycle, so the first token cannot represent the seasonal shape and the model must reconstruct it from attention. Patches of twelve on a period of twelve work well; patches of four do not. Match the patch length to the shortest structure you expect.

    Channel independence is an assumption, not a fact. If two channels are genuinely coupled — a price and its spread, a load and its temperature — the independent model cannot exploit the coupling. It will still fit, but it leaves accuracy on the table, and the gap is invisible unless you compare against a channel-mixing model. Use CI as the default and as a strong baseline, not as a universal truth.

    Pretrained features can be worse than supervised ones. The linear probe in the demo reaches \(0.4015\) against \(0.2691\) for fine-tuning and \(0.2956\) from scratch. Pretraining on 289 windows does not produce a representation that is linearly decodable; the encoder has learned features that only become useful when the head above them can move too. Self-supervision needs scale to pay off.

    The flatten head is large. \(W_h\) has \(H\times Nd\) entries, which for a long look-back and many patches is the biggest matrix in the model. It also has no structural prior: every output step mixes every patch with its own weights. Pooling or an attention-based decoder would shrink it, at the cost of losing positional detail.

    Post-norm blocks need a learning-rate that respects them. The residual path passes through a normalisation at every block, so gradients are rescaled on the way back. With 400 epochs and \(10^{-2}\) the demo trains; at \(3\times10^{-3}\) it underfits badly (\(0.79\) in-sample MSE after 120 epochs). Pre-norm would be more forgiving, at the cost of a different residual topology.

    Masking is a weak pretext task on short series. With four tokens and a 30% mask, each epoch hides about one patch per row. The reconstruction loss falls quickly to \(0.07\), but the representation it produces is not obviously better than random features. Masked pretraining is worth it when the unlabelled corpus is large; on a few hundred windows it is mostly a regulariser.

    One season of history is not enough for a transformer. Attention can compare patches, but with four patches there are only six pairs, and most of the model's capacity goes into the head. The architecture shines on windows of hundreds of steps; on a 48-step window a linear model with a seasonal term is a fair fight.

    Applications #

    PatchTST is used for medium- and long-horizon forecasting where the look-back is long enough to contain many cycles: electricity load and price, traffic, weather, retail demand at the aggregate level, and financial volatility. The channel-independent design makes it a natural fit for datasets with hundreds of related series — the weights are shared, so adding a series costs nothing but data.

    The self-supervised protocol is used for transfer: pretrain on a large, unlabelled corpus of series from the same domain, then fine-tune or probe on the small target series. The paper reports that this beats training from scratch when the target data are scarce — the opposite of what the demo shows, because the demo's pretraining corpus is the target itself.

    It is the wrong tool for very short series, for intermittent demand, and for problems where cross-channel structure is the signal. It is also not probabilistic: the head emits a point forecast. For intervals, add a quantile head or use a model with a likelihood.
