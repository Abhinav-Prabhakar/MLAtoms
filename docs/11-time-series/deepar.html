    11 · Time series/tier 3/5 variants/source

    DeepAR — forecast a distribution, then sample from it

    Intuition #

    A point forecaster answers "what is \(y_{T+1}\)?" A distributional forecaster answers "what is \(p(y_{T+1}\mid y_{\le T})\), and what does a plausible future look like?" DeepAR is the second kind. It runs a recurrent network over the series, and at every step the network emits the parameters of a probability distribution for the next observation. Training maximises the likelihood; forecasting draws from the network and feeds the draw back in.

    That feedback is the whole idea. A one-step distribution does not tell you what a twelve-month path looks like, because errors compound: a high draw today changes the state tomorrow, which changes the mean and the width of tomorrow's distribution. Sampling trajectories reproduces that compounding. The resulting band is wider than the one you get by adding one-step variances, and it has the right shape — skewed for counts, heavy-tailed for jumps — instead of being Gaussian by assumption.

    The network is deliberately small: one tanh layer of width sixteen. All the modelling lives in the likelihood, and the likelihood is where the variants differ.

    • gaussian_likelihood is the textbook version: mean and standard deviation per step, Gaussian log-likelihood.
    • deepar uses the same head but forecasts by Monte Carlo, feeding samples back into the state.
    • negbin_likelihood replaces the Gaussian with a negative binomial, which is the right shape for counts: non-negative, integer, variance growing with the mean.
    • student_t replaces the Gaussian with a heavy-tailed \(t\), so a spike in the training data is treated as a plausible draw rather than an impossible one.
    • quantile_output drops the likelihood entirely and trains five pinball losses, which is the distribution-free alternative.

    On 348 training points and a 12-step hold-out the Gaussian model reaches a mean absolute error of \(0.6048\) against \(0.8454\) for seasonal naive. The count model reaches \(1.0421\) against \(1.7500\) for the same weekday last week. The \(t\) head on a spike-riddled series reaches \(1.7220\) where the Gaussian head reaches \(2.7498\).

    Variants #

    variant likelihood prediction held-out MAE
    gaussian_likelihood Gaussian analytic mean path 0.6077
    deepar Gaussian Monte Carlo median 0.6048
    negbin_likelihood negative binomial mean path 1.0421
    student_t Student \(t\), unit scale mean path 1.7220
    quantile_output five pinball losses median path 0.9626

    gaussian_likelihood and deepar are the same fitted model. Both minimise the Gaussian negative log-likelihood, both use the same seed, and the demo asserts that their training losses are exactly equal. They diverge only at prediction time: the first recurses on the conditional mean, the second draws 400 trajectories and takes the median. The paths differ by up to a unit on this series, because the mean recursion ignores the variance that the sampling accumulates.

    negbin_likelihood is fitted to a weekly count series with a mean of \(3.971\). It reaches \(1.0421\) against \(1.7500\) for the same weekday last week, and its 80% band covers \(0.917\) of the hold-out while never going below zero — a property no transformed Gaussian can guarantee.

    student_t is fitted to the seasonal series with a spike of size eight injected every seventh point. The \(t\) head reaches \(1.7220\) against \(2.7498\) for the Gaussian head on the same data. The spikes stop distorting the fit because the likelihood can explain them.

    quantile_output trains five pinball losses at levels \(0.1,0.25,0.5,0.75,0.9\). It reaches \(0.9626\) — better than the last value but worse than seasonal naive. Five quantiles per step is a harder estimation problem than two likelihood parameters, and on 347 transitions the difference shows.

    Derivation #

    The autoregressive factorisation #

    A series is modelled as a product of one-step conditionals:

    \[p(y_{1:T}\mid y_{1:0})= \prod_{t=1}^{T}p\bigl(y_t\mid h_t\bigr), \qquad h_t=f_\theta(h_{t-1},y_{t-1}). \qquad (1)\]

    The state \(h_t\) is a deterministic summary of everything before \(t\). Nothing is assumed about the shape of \(p\) beyond the parametric family chosen for the head. In particular the variance is a function of the state, so the model is heteroscedastic by construction.

    The recurrence #

    With standardised observations \(z_t=(y_t-\mu)/\sigma\) and a hidden width \(d\):

    \[h_t=\tanh\!\bigl(w_x z_{t-1}+W_h h_{t-1}+b_h\bigr), \qquad h_t\in\mathbb R^{d}, \qquad (2)\]
    \[o_t=W_o h_t+b_o\in\mathbb R^{k}, \qquad (3)\]

    where \(k\) is the number of head outputs: two for the Gaussian and the negative binomial, one for the \(t\), five for the quantile grid. The input at step \(t\) is the previous observation, not the previous mean. Teacher forcing during training and free running during sampling is exactly the difference the two modes implement.

    Gaussian likelihood #

    \[\mu_t=o_{t,1},\qquad \sigma_t=\mathrm{softplus}(o_{t,2}), \qquad (4)\]
    \[\ell_t=\tfrac12\Bigl(\frac{z_t-\mu_t}{\sigma_t}\Bigr)^{2} +\log\sigma_t+\tfrac12\log 2\pi . \qquad (5)\]

    The softplus keeps \(\sigma_t>0\) without a clamp, and its derivative \(\sigma'(a)=\mathrm{sigmoid}(a)\) is what turns the gradient in \(\sigma\) into a gradient in \(a\):

    \[\frac{\partial\ell_t}{\partial o_{t,1}} =-\frac{z_t-\mu_t}{\sigma_t^{2}},\qquad \frac{\partial\ell_t}{\partial o_{t,2}} =\frac{1-r_t^{2}}{\sigma_t}\,\mathrm{sigmoid}(o_{t,2}), \qquad (6)\]

    with \(r_t=(z_t-\mu_t)/\sigma_t\). The factor \(1/\sigma_t\) in the second expression is easy to drop. Doing so still trains — the model simply learns a slightly different objective — which is why it survives code review. The finite-difference check is the only reliable detector.

    Student-\(t\) likelihood #

    \[\ell_t=c_\nu+\tfrac{\nu+1}{2}\log\!\Bigl(1+\frac{(z_t-\mu_t)^{2}}{\nu}\Bigr), \qquad c_\nu=\log\Gamma\!\bigl(\tfrac{\nu+1}{2}\bigr) -\log\Gamma\!\bigl(\tfrac{\nu}{2}\bigr)-\tfrac12\log(\nu\pi). \qquad (7)\]
    \[\frac{\partial\ell_t}{\partial\mu_t} =-\frac{(\nu+1)(z_t-\mu_t)}{\nu+(z_t-\mu_t)^{2}} . \qquad (8)\]

    The scale is fixed at one. That is not a convenience: with a free \(\sigma\) the \(t\) likelihood is unbounded below. As \(\sigma\to\infty\) the density at any fixed point grows like \(\sigma^{-1}\), so the negative log-likelihood decreases without limit and the optimiser runs away. The demo's earlier version learned the scale and produced an 80% band 79 units wide. Standardising the target fixes the scale at the only value that is identified, and the heavy tail is what remains.

    Negative binomial likelihood #

    For counts with mean \(\mu_t\) and dispersion \(r_t\):

    \[p(y_t)=\frac{\Gamma(y_t+r_t)}{\Gamma(r_t)\Gamma(y_t+1)} \Bigl(\frac{r_t}{r_t+\mu_t}\Bigr)^{r_t} \Bigl(\frac{\mu_t}{r_t+\mu_t}\Bigr)^{y_t}, \qquad (9)\]
    \[\mu_t=\exp(o_{t,1}),\qquad r_t=\exp(o_{t,2}), \qquad (10)\]

    trained by the negative log-likelihood. The gradients need the digamma function,

    \[\frac{\partial\ell_t}{\partial\mu_t} =\frac{r_t+y_t}{r_t+\mu_t}-\frac{y_t}{\mu_t}, \qquad (11)\]

    and a similar expression in \(r_t\). Both are implemented with a Lanczos \(\log\Gamma\) and a recurrence for \(\psi\), so nothing outside numpy is needed.

    The counts are not standardised. A count likelihood lives on the integers, and feeding it a \(z\)-score is meaningless — the demo's first attempt did exactly that and crashed inside \(\log\Gamma\). The cell's input is still rescaled by the series standard deviation, but the target and the output stay in count units.

    Quantile output #

    The head emits one number per level and the levels are made monotone by construction, not by sorting:

    \[q_{t,1}=o_{t,1},\qquad q_{t,k}=q_{t,k-1}+\mathrm{softplus}(o_{t,k}), \qquad k=2,\dots,K . \qquad (12)\]

    Each increment is positive, so the quantiles cannot cross and the gradient is well defined everywhere. The loss is the pinball loss averaged over levels:

    \[\ell_t=\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K} \rho_{\tau_k}\bigl(z_t-q_{t,k}\bigr),\qquad \rho_\tau(u)=u\bigl(\tau-\mathbb 1\{u<0\}\bigr). \qquad (13)\]

    Because \(q_{t,k}\) is a cumulative sum, the gradient for \(o_{t,k}\) is the sum of the pinball gradients at all levels above it, scaled by the softplus derivative:

    \[\frac{\partial\ell_t}{\partial o_{t,k}} =\mathrm{sigmoid}(o_{t,k})\sum_{j\ge k} \frac{\partial\ell_t}{\partial q_{t,j}} . \qquad (14)\]

    Backpropagation through time #

    With \(g_t=\partial\ell_t/\partial o_t\), the state gradient obeys

    \[\delta_t=(1-h_t^{2})\odot\bigl(W_o^{\top}g_t+W_h^{\top}\delta_{t+1}\bigr), \qquad \delta_{T+1}=0, \qquad (15)\]

    and the parameter gradients are outer products accumulated over \(t\):

    \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_h}=\sum_t h_{t-1}\delta_t^{\top},\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_x}=\sum_t z_{t-1}\delta_t^{\top}. \qquad (16)\]

    One detail matters in practice: \(h_t\) enters the recurrence twice, once through \(W_h h_t\) at the next step and once through \(W_o h_t\) in the output, and the second term is what makes \(\delta_t\) different from a plain RNN without a head.

    Monte Carlo versus analytic paths #

    The deepar forecast draws

    \[\tilde z_t\sim p\bigl(\cdot\mid h_t\bigr),\qquad h_{t+1}=f_\theta(h_t,\tilde z_t), \qquad (17)\]

    repeatedly and reports the empirical median at each horizon. The gaussian_likelihood forecast instead plugs in the conditional mean:

    \[\bar z_t=\mathbb E[z_t\mid h_t],\qquad h_{t+1}=f_\theta(h_t,\bar z_t), \qquad (18)\]

    and approximates the predictive variance by accumulating one-step variances along that mean path:

    \[v_h=\sum_{j=1}^{h}\sigma_j^{2},\qquad \mathrm{band}_h=\bar z_h\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{v_h}. \qquad (19)\]

    Equation (19) is not exact — it ignores the fact that the variance changes the path — and the demo shows the consequence: the analytic band is \(0.8535\) wide on average where the sampled band is \(1.4790\). The missing width is the path-dependence that (17) captures and (18) discards.

    Gradient clipping #

    Each gradient entry is clipped to \([-5,5]\) before the Adam update. Without it, a hidden state that saturates at \(\pm1\) produces a spike in the backward pass and the run diverges; with it, the same configuration trains. DeepAR's own implementation does the same thing.

    Code walkthrough #

    Every likelihood returns a per-step loss and a per-step gradient in the head output, which keeps the recurrent code independent of the distribution. The Gaussian is the template:

        mu = output[:, 0]
    activation = output[:, 1]
    sigma = _softplus(activation) + 1e-6
    standardised = (target - mu) / sigma
    loss = 0.5 * standardised ** 2 + np.log(sigma) + _HALF_LOG_2PI
    gradient = np.empty_like(output)
    gradient[:, 0] = -(target - mu) / sigma ** 2
    gradient[:, 1] = ((1.0 - standardised ** 2) * _sigmoid(activation)
                      / sigma)
    return loss, gradient

    The + 1e-6 prevents a zero standard deviation from producing an infinite gradient on the first step, when the head is still near zero and \(\sigma=\mathrm{softplus}(0)\approx0.69\).

    The \(t\) head needs only one output, because the scale is fixed:

        offset = target - output[:, 0]
    constant = (math.lgamma(0.5 * (nu + 1.0)) - math.lgamma(0.5 * nu)
                - 0.5 * math.log(nu * math.pi))
    loss = constant + 0.5 * (nu + 1.0) * np.log1p(offset ** 2 / nu)
    gradient = np.empty_like(output)
    gradient[:, 0] = -(nu + 1.0) * offset / (nu + offset ** 2)
    return loss, gradient

    np.log1p rather than np.log(1 + ·) matters when the offset is small, which is most of the time: it keeps full precision on the typical residuals instead of losing it to cancellation.

    The quantile head builds monotonicity into the parametrisation:

        quantiles = np.empty_like(output)
    quantiles[:, 0] = output[:, 0]
    for index in range(1, quantiles.shape[1]):
        quantiles[:, index] = (quantiles[:, index - 1]
                               + _softplus(output[:, index]))
    error = target[:, None] - quantiles
    loss = np.where(error >= 0.0, levels * error, (levels - 1.0) * error)

    The first output is the lowest quantile, not the median, and each later one is the previous plus a positive increment. Sampling from this head is then a one-dimensional interpolation, which is why the same sample method works for all five variants.

    The forward pass is a single loop that stores every state, because the backward pass needs all of them:

            for step in range(steps):
            states[step + 1] = np.tanh(inputs[step] @ p["w_x"]
                                       + states[step] @ p["W_h"] + p["b_h"])
            outputs[step] = states[step + 1] @ p["W_o"] + p["b_o"]

    inputs[step] is a length-one vector, so the matrix product is a row times a matrix — no batch axis. That is deliberate: DeepAR trains on one long sequence, not on shuffled windows, and the whole series is the batch.

    The backward loop is (15) and (16) written out:

            carry = np.zeros(p["b_h"].size)
        for step in range(steps - 1, -1, -1):
            total = outputs_grad[step] @ p["W_o"].T + carry
            delta = total * (1.0 - states[step + 1] ** 2)
            g["w_x"] += inputs[step][:, None] * delta[None, :]
            g["W_h"] += states[step][:, None] @ delta[None, :]
            g["b_h"] += delta
            carry = delta @ p["W_h"].T

    carry is \(\delta_{t+1}\) propagated through \(W_h\). The states[step + 1] indexing is the easiest place to introduce an off-by-one: states[0] is the initial zero state, so the state produced at step t lives at t + 1, and the derivative of the tanh must use that same entry.

    Sampling is a nested loop over trajectories and horizons, with the draw assigned back to current:

            for index in range(n_samples):
            current_state = state
            current = previous
            for step in range(horizon):
                current_state, output = self._step(current_state, current)
                current = self._draw(output, rng)
                paths[index, step] = current

    Each trajectory starts from the same encoded state and the same last observation, and diverges only through the draws. That is what makes the sample a draw from the predictive distribution rather than a perturbation of a fixed path.

    The single place where model space and input space differ is the count model:

            p = self.cell_.parameters
        feed = previous
        if self.variant == "negbin_likelihood":
            feed = (previous - self.input_mean_) / self.input_scale_

    Continuous variants live in standardised units, so the previous value is already what the cell wants. The count model keeps its observations in count units because the likelihood demands it, and rescales only on the way into the recurrence.

    Complexity #

    With sequence length \(T\), hidden width \(d\), \(k\) head outputs, \(E\) epochs and \(N\) sampled trajectories of length \(H\):

    • One forward step: \(O(d^{2}+kd)\) — the state matrix dominates.
    • One epoch: \(O(T(d^{2}+kd))\) for the forward pass and the same again for the backward pass, so \(O(Td^{2})\) in the width.
    • Full fit: the above times \(E\). For \(T=347\), \(d=16\), \(E=300\) the demo's six fits take about five seconds.
    • Prediction: \(O(Hd^{2})\) for the mean path; \(O(NHd^{2})\) for the samples.
    • Memory: \(O(Td)\) for the stored states, which is what makes the backward pass possible and what limits the sequence length.

    The cost is linear in \(T\), unlike attention, and quadratic in \(d\), unlike a wide MLP. For long series that is the right trade: the state carries the history, so nothing needs to look back over the raw sequence.

    Sampling multiplies the prediction cost by \(N\). With 400 trajectories and a 12-step horizon the demo's band is computed in a few milliseconds, but a production system sampling thousands of paths for a thousand series would feel it. The analytic band in (19) exists precisely for that case.

    Failure modes #

    The mean recursion can diverge. Feeding the conditional mean back into the network is not the same as feeding a draw, and the map can have a fixed point far from the data. In development, a learning rate of \(10^{-2}\) with 400 epochs produced a mean path whose MAE was \(4.5\) on a series whose seasonal naive error is \(0.85\). The training loss had also risen, so the failure is visible in the loss — but only if you look. A stable configuration is a small learning rate, a conservative hidden width, and gradient clipping.

    Long sequences make the gradient fragile. Backpropagation through 347 steps multiplies 347 Jacobians. A tanh state with a spectral radius near one keeps them alive; above one they explode. Nothing in the architecture enforces stability, so the initialisation and the clip do the work. Truncated backpropagation through time over windows of 50–100 steps is the standard remedy.

    A free scale makes the \(t\) likelihood improper. As shown in (7)–(8), the negative log-likelihood of a \(t\) with learned scale decreases as the scale grows, because the log-density at a fixed point scales like \(-\log\sigma\). The optimiser finds this direction and the predictive band explodes — the demo's first version produced a band 79 units wide on a series whose standard deviation is about four. Fixing the scale, or bounding it, is not optional.

    Counts cannot be standardised. The negative binomial is defined on non-negative integers. Standardising the target turns counts into real numbers and the likelihood into nonsense; the demo originally crashed inside \(\log\Gamma\) because a scaled count was negative. The input to the cell may be rescaled, the target may not.

    Calibration needs more than twelve points. The demo reports 80% band coverage of \(0.583\) for the Gaussian model, \(0.917\) for the count model and \(0.500\) for the quantile model. On twelve points the standard error of a coverage estimate is about \(0.14\), so none of these is a calibration claim. Coverage has to be measured over hundreds of windows and many series.

    The quantile head is data-hungry. Five quantiles per step, each with its own pinball loss, is more parameters to identify than a two-parameter likelihood on the same 347 transitions. The demo's quantile median reaches \(0.9626\) against \(0.8454\) for seasonal naive. It is the right tool when the shape is unknown and the data are plentiful, not when the data are scarce.

    One series is one sample. The model is trained on a single sequence, so every gradient step sees the same \(347\) transitions. That is fine for a demo and wrong for production: DeepAR's advantage comes from training one model across thousands of related series, where the shared weights learn the shape and the state adapts per series.

    There is no seasonality prior. The network must encode a period-twelve pattern in its hidden state from scratch. A seasonal lag in the input, or a seasonal naive residual as the target, would make the same model much easier to fit. The demo's modest error against seasonal naive is the cost of leaving that structure out.

    Applications #

    DeepAR's home is large-scale probabilistic forecasting: thousands of SKUs in retail, thousands of meters in electricity, thousands of series in cloud telemetry. One model is trained across all of them, with the series identity carried by the state or by covariates, and the output is a distribution per series per horizon. Decisions — safety stock, staffing, capacity — need the distribution, not the mean.

    The count head is used for demand that arrives in units: units sold per day, calls per hour, failures per machine. The negative binomial captures the over-dispersion that a Poisson misses, and the non-negativity is structural rather than clipped.

    The heavy-tailed head is used where spikes are real: promotional sales, traffic incidents, sensor glitches. Treating a spike as an outlier to be removed throws away information; treating it as a \(t\) draw keeps it and stops it from dominating the fit.

    The quantile head is used when the shape is unknown or the loss is asymmetric. Predicting a 0.9 quantile directly optimises the quantity a newsvendor cares about, and it does not require the distribution to belong to a named family.

    It is the wrong tool for a single short series with a strong seasonal pattern and no covariates, where a state-space model or a seasonal naive is a better use of the data, and for very long horizons where the recursion's compounding error dominates.
