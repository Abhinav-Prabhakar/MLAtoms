    11 · Time series/tier 3/4 variants/source

    Theta — a trend you can see through exponential smoothing

    Intuition #

    Exponential smoothing is a good short-term forecaster and a bad trend extrapolator: the level lags a moving series, so a flat forecast trails behind a rising one. The theta method fixes that by adding back half the slope of a straight line fitted to the whole record. It is one of the most successful entries in the M3 competition, and its structure is almost embarrassingly simple: smooth the series, add a drift, done.

    The name comes from the theta line. Given a series \(Y\), define a family \(Z(\theta)\) whose second differences are \(\theta\) times those of \(Y\). At \(\theta=0\) the line has no curvature and is a straight line; at \(\theta=2\) the local bends are doubled. The original method decomposes the series into the \(\theta=0\) and \(\theta=2\) lines, forecasts the first with simple exponential smoothing and the second with a linear regression, and averages the two. A little algebra collapses that recipe into SES plus half the OLS slope, which is what this file implements.

    The variants add the pieces the original paper left fixed:

    • theta is the standard method: \(\theta=2\), half the OLS drift, and a fitted smoothing parameter.
    • optimised_theta searches \(\theta\) and the smoothing parameter jointly, so a series with more curvature can use a larger \(\theta\) than two.
    • dstm is the Dynamic Standard Theta Model: the intercept and slope of the line are re-estimated at every observation from data seen so far, so the drift can follow a changing trend.
    • dynamic_theta is the same state-space recursion with \(\theta\) free, which is the Dynamic Optimised Theta Model of Fiorucci et al.

    The demo checks the closed-form equivalence between the theta-line implementation and SES-with-drift to \(3.6\times10^{-15}\), shows that optimising \(\theta\) lowers both the in-sample sum of squares and the held-out error on a curved series, and measures the small but consistent gain from a dynamic drift when the trend changes slope.

    Variants #

    variant what moves demo result
    theta \(\theta=2\), static drift MAE 1.0994 vs 1.2859
    optimised_theta \(\theta\) searched \(\theta=4\), MAE 2.2349 vs 2.7054
    dstm dynamic intercept and slope one-step MSE 0.3959 vs 0.4024
    dynamic_theta dynamic states, free \(\theta\) MAE 1.1569 vs 1.3479

    theta fits a linear trend with noise and finds \(\alpha=0.2120\) and an initial level of \(10.1867\). The one-step residual standard deviation is \(0.5345\), close to the simulated noise scale of \(0.5\). On the 24 held-out points its MAE is \(1.0994\) against \(1.2859\) for the naive last-value forecast. The gain is not huge because the series is dominated by noise, but it is the right sign: smoothing plus half a slope beats repeating the last observation.

    optimised_theta faces a quadratic trend, where a fixed \(\theta=2\) cannot bend enough. The search chooses \(\theta=4.00\), the top of the grid, and lowers the in-sample sum of squares from \(109.25\) to \(101.51\) and the held-out MAE from \(2.7054\) to \(2.2349\). The boundary solution is a signal, not a theorem: the grid should be extended, or the trend modelled with a quadratic term, if a larger theta keeps helping.

    dstm updates the line coefficients at every step. On a trend with a slow wave, its one-step mean squared error is \(0.3959\) against \(0.4024\) for the static model, and its 24-step MAE is \(2.4905\) against \(2.4956\). The improvement is real but small: a state-space drift can track a slowly changing slope, not a full seasonal cycle, and most of the remaining error is the wave the model cannot see.

    dynamic_theta is fitted to a series whose slope jumps from \(0.02\) to \(0.09\) halfway through. It chooses \(\theta=4.00\) and \(\alpha=0.2220\), lowers the sum of squares from \(72.13\) to \(69.99\), and improves the held-out MAE from \(1.3479\) to \(1.1569\). Freeing theta helps because the post-break trend is steeper than the pre-break trend, and a fixed \(\theta=2\) cannot represent that asymmetry.

    Derivation #

    The theta line #

    For a series \(Y_t\) and a parameter \(\theta\ge1\), the theta line is defined by

    \[\nabla^2Z_t(\theta)=\theta\,\nabla^2Y_t, \qquad (1)\]

    where \(\nabla^2\) is the second difference. Any two series with proportional second differences differ by a straight line, so

    \[Z_t(\theta)=\theta Y_t+(1-\theta)(A+Bt) \qquad (2)\]

    for some intercept \(A\) and slope \(B\). The natural choice is the OLS line of \(Y\):

    \[(A,B)=\arg\min_{a,b}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(Y_t-a-bt)^2, \qquad (3)\]

    which makes \(Z(0)\) exactly the long-term linear component and \(Z(2)\) the short-term component with doubled curvature. A larger \(\theta\) keeps more of the original shape; \(\theta=1\) returns the series unchanged.

    Simple exponential smoothing #

    For a series \(Z\) and smoothing parameter \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), SES is

    \[\ell_t=\alpha Z_t+(1-\alpha)\ell_{t-1}, \qquad (4)\]

    with initial level \(\ell_0\). The recursion is linear in \(\ell_0\), a fact the fitting code exploits. For a straight line \(a+bt\), the steady-state level lags the line by \((1-\alpha)b/\alpha\), which is exactly the bias the theta drift is designed to remove.

    The static theta forecast #

    Apply (4) to the theta line and forecast it flat, and forecast the linear part by its own line. Since \(Y=(1/\theta)Z+(1-1/\theta)(A+Bt)\),

    \[\hat Y_{n+h}=\frac{1}{\theta}\ell_n^Z +\Bigl(1-\frac1\theta\Bigr)\bigl(A+B(n+h)\bigr). \qquad (5)\]

    For \(\theta=2\) this is half the smoothed theta line plus half the OLS line. It is the implementation's entire forecast: two terms, no iteration.

    Equivalence to SES with drift #

    When the initial level is placed on the line, (5) collapses to the familiar form. For \(\theta=2\),

    \[\ell_n^Z=2\ell_n^Y-\ell_n^L,\qquad \ell_n^L=A+Bn-\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}B, \qquad (6)\]

    where \(\ell_n^Y\) is the SES level of the original series. Substituting into (5) gives

    \[\hat Y_{n+h}=\ell_n^Y+\frac{B}{2} \Bigl(h-1+\frac1\alpha\Bigr), \qquad (7)\]

    which is the Hyndman–Billah result: the theta method is SES with drift \(B/2\). The demo checks (7) numerically and finds a maximum difference of \(3.55\times10^{-15}\). The identity is the reason the method needs no separate trend model: the drift is just half the slope of a line through the data.

    Fitting the initial level #

    Both \(\ell_0\) and \(\alpha\) are estimated by minimising the one-step sum of squares. For fixed \(\alpha\) and \(\theta\), every fitted value is affine in \(\ell_0\):

    \[\hat Y_t=\mu_t^0+u_t\ell_0, \qquad (8)\]

    where \(\mu_t^0\) is the one-step mean with \(\ell_0=0\) and \(u_t\) is the coefficient of the initial level. For the static model \(u_t=(1-\alpha)^{t-1}/\theta\); for the dynamic model \(u_t=(1-\alpha)^{t-1}\). Minimising \(\sum_t(Y_t-\mu_t^0-u_t\ell_0)^2\) gives the closed form

    \[\ell_0^\star= \frac{\sum_t u_t\,(Y_t-\mu_t^0)}{\sum_t u_t^2}, \qquad (9)\]

    so only \(\alpha\) (and \(\theta\), when free) needs a search. The code scans \(\alpha\) on a grid of step \(0.02\) and then refines around the winner with steps \(0.01,0.005,0.002\). The resulting \(\ell_0\) is exact for the chosen \(\alpha\), not a grid approximation.

    Optimised theta #

    Freeing \(\theta\) gives the optimisation

    \[\min_{\theta\ge1,\ \alpha\in(0,1]} \sum_{t=1}^{n}\bigl(Y_t-\hat Y_t(\theta,\alpha)\bigr)^2, \qquad (10)\]

    with \(\ell_0\) profiled out by (9). The objective is not convex in \(\theta\) and the search is a grid, not a gradient method. The implementation evaluates THETA_GRID, a fixed set of eleven values from one to four, and keeps the best. A larger grid is cheap; a finer one is not necessarily better, because the in-sample optimum can overfit the curvature of the training window.

    The dynamic state space #

    The dynamic models replace the fixed line with coefficients estimated from data up to \(t\). The states are the level \(\ell_t\), the mean \(\bar Y_t\), and the line coefficients \(A_t,B_t\). The recursions are

    \[\ell_t=\alpha Y_t+(1-\alpha)\ell_{t-1}, \qquad (11)\]
    \[\bar Y_t=\frac{(t-1)\bar Y_{t-1}+Y_t}{t}, \qquad (12)\]
    \[B_t=\frac{1}{t+1}\Bigl((t-2)B_{t-1} +\frac{6}{t}\bigl(Y_t-\bar Y_{t-1}\bigr)\Bigr), \qquad (13)\]
    \[A_t=\bar Y_t-\frac{t+1}{2}B_t . \qquad (14)\]

    Equation (13) is a recursive least-squares slope: the factor \(6/t\) makes \(B_t\) converge to the slope of a local line, and (14) puts the intercept at the midpoint of the observations seen so far. The one-step mean is

    \[\mu_t=\ell_{t-1}+\Bigl(1-\frac1\theta\Bigr) \Bigl[(1-\alpha)^{t-1}A_{t-1} +\frac{1-(1-\alpha)^t}{\alpha}B_{t-1}\Bigr], \qquad (15)\]

    which is (5) with the fixed line replaced by the state at \(t-1\). The coefficient of \(A_{t-1}\) decays geometrically because the influence of the initial intercept fades; the coefficient of \(B_{t-1}\) tends to \(1/\alpha\), the same lag correction as in (7).

    For \(h\ge2\) the recursion is iterated with the forecast standing in for the unobserved \(Y\): compute \(\mu_{n+h}\) from the current states, append it, update \(\ell,\bar Y,A,B\) with \(\mu_{n+h}\) in place of \(Y\), and repeat. The dynamic forecast is therefore a deterministic path, not a closed form.

    The state space is initialised at \(A_0=B_0=\bar Y_0=0\), which makes \(B_1=3Y_1\) and produces large one-step errors at the start. The implementation excludes the first \(\max(3,\lceil2/\alpha\rceil)\) residuals from the sum of squares, so the fit is not dominated by the transient. The forecasts still use the states after the transient, so only the objective is trimmed.

    Prediction intervals #

    Fiorucci et al. give the conditional variance

    \[\mathrm{Var}(Y_{n+h}\mid Y_1,\dots,Y_n) =\bigl[1+(h-1)\alpha^2\bigr]\sigma^2, \qquad (16)\]

    where \(\sigma^2\) is the one-step residual variance. The interval is Gaussian: \(\hat Y_{n+h}\pm z_{1-\gamma/2}\sqrt{1+(h-1)\alpha^2}\,\sigma\). It ignores parameter uncertainty and the correlation between successive forecast errors, so it is a lower bound on the true predictive width, not a calibrated interval.

    Code walkthrough #

    The OLS line is computed once for the static variants. Centring the time grid avoids forming a design matrix and makes the slope a single dot product:

        n = y.size
    grid = np.arange(1, n + 1, dtype=float)
    centred = grid - grid.mean()
    slope = float(centred @ (y - y.mean()) / (centred @ centred))
    intercept = float(y.mean() - slope * grid.mean())
    return intercept, slope

    The \(1,\dots,n\) indexing matches the state-space equations of Fiorucci et al., where \(\bar Y_t\) is the mean of the first \(t\) observations. Using \(0,\dots,n-1\) would shift every intercept by one slope and silently change the forecast.

    The static engine is (2), (4) and (5) in one loop. The coefficient array holds \(u_t\) so that (9) can be applied after the pass, when the fitted values with \(\ell_0=0\) are known:

        mean = np.empty(n)
    coefficient = np.empty(n)
    level = 0.0
    for index in range(n):
        mean[index] = level / theta + weight * line[index]
        coefficient[index] = powers[index] / theta
        level = alpha * z[index] + (1.0 - alpha) * level
    if ell0 is None:
        ell0 = float(np.sum(coefficient * (y - mean))
                     / np.sum(coefficient * coefficient))

    The alternative would be to run SES twice, once with \(\ell_0=0\) and once with \(\ell_0=1\), and subtract. Carrying coefficient is cheaper and makes the affine dependence explicit.

    The dynamic engine adds the state updates (11)–(14). The order matters: B uses the mean before the current observation is folded in, so average_previous is saved first:

        for index in range(n):
        t = index + 1
        drift = weight * (
            powers[index] * intercept_state
            + (1.0 - powers[index] * (1.0 - alpha)) / alpha * slope_state)
        mean[index] = level + drift
        coefficient[index] = powers[index]
        level = alpha * y[index] + (1.0 - alpha) * level
        average_previous = average
        average = ((t - 1) * average_previous + y[index]) / t
        slope_state = ((t - 2) * slope_state
                       + 6.0 / t * (y[index] - average_previous)) / (t + 1)
        intercept_state = average - 0.5 * (t + 1) * slope_state

    powers[index] is \((1-\alpha)^{\text{index}}=(1-\alpha)^{t-1}\), and the factor (1.0 - powers[index] * (1.0 - alpha)) / alpha is \((1-(1-\alpha)^t)/\alpha\). Both appear directly in (15), so the loop is a transcription rather than a derivation.

    The alpha search is a coarse scan followed by local refinement. Refining after the scan matters because the sum of squares is flat near the optimum and a \(0.02\) grid can miss the best value by more than the improvement being measured:

        best = None
    for alpha in np.arange(0.02, 0.99, 0.02):
        result = _static(y, theta, float(alpha), ell0)
        if best is None or result["sse"] < best["sse"]:
            best = result
    assert best is not None
    centre = best["alpha"]
    for step in (0.01, 0.005, 0.002):
        for alpha in (centre - step, centre + step):
            if 0.0 < alpha <= 1.0:
                result = _static(y, theta, float(alpha), ell0)
                if result["sse"] < best["sse"]:
                    best = result
        centre = best["alpha"]

    The search is nested inside the theta loop, so optimised_theta performs eleven alpha fits and dynamic_theta seven, which is why those variants cost more than the fixed-theta ones. Each alpha fit is a single \(O(n)\) pass, so the total is still small.

    The variant grid is chosen once in fit. The two standard models fix \(\theta=2\); the two optimised models scan their grids; an explicit theta in the constructor overrides both:

            if self.variant in ("theta", "dstm"):
            grid = (2.0,)
        elif self.variant == "optimised_theta":
            grid = THETA_GRID
        else:
            grid = DYNAMIC_GRID
        if self.theta is not None:
            grid = (self.theta,)

    The distinction between THETA_GRID and DYNAMIC_GRID is deliberate: the dynamic state space is more expensive per evaluation, so its grid is coarser. Both grids include \(\theta=1\) and \(\theta=2\), which means the optimised models can always fall back to SES or to the standard theta forecast.

    Prediction for the static models is a vectorised evaluation of (5). The line is extrapolated at the absolute future times \(n+1,\dots,n+h\), not at \(1,\dots,h\):

            if self.variant in ("theta", "optimised_theta"):
            intercept, slope = self.line_
            weight = 1.0 - 1.0 / self.theta_
            steps = np.arange(1, horizon + 1)
            line = intercept + slope * (self.n_ + steps)
            return self.level_ / self.theta_ + weight * line

    Using steps alone is the classic off-by-one-slope bug: the forecast would be shifted down by \(nB\), which on a series of length 240 with slope \(0.05\) is a constant error of 12. The demo's equivalence check catches exactly this.

    The dynamic prediction repeats the state recursion into the future. Each forecast is appended before the state update, so the update sees the forecast it just produced:

            for step in range(1, horizon + 1):
            t = self.n_ + step
            decay = (1.0 - self.alpha_) ** (t - 1)
            drift = weight * (
                decay * intercept
                + (1.0 - (1.0 - self.alpha_) ** t) / self.alpha_ * slope)
            mean = level + drift
            out[step - 1] = mean
            level = self.alpha_ * mean + (1.0 - self.alpha_) * level
            average_previous = average
            average = ((t - 1) * average_previous + mean) / t
            slope = ((t - 2) * slope
                     + 6.0 / t * (mean - average_previous)) / (t + 1)
            intercept = average - 0.5 * (t + 1) * slope

    At step=1, t = n+1 and decay is \((1-\alpha)^n\), the same coefficient as in (15). At later steps the state has been updated with forecasts, so the drift evolves even though no new data arrive.

    The interval is the variance formula (16) evaluated on the forecast path:

            forecast = self.predict(horizon)
        steps = np.arange(horizon)
        scale = np.sqrt(1.0 + steps * self.alpha_ ** 2) * self.sigma_
        z = NormalDist().inv_cdf(0.5 + level / 2.0)
        return forecast - z * scale, forecast + z * scale

    The width grows like \(\sqrt{1+(h-1)\alpha^2}\), which is much slower than the \(\sqrt{h}\) of a random walk. That is appropriate for a model whose forecast is a smooth extrapolation, and misleading if the residuals are autocorrelated; check the residual autocorrelation before trusting the band.

    Complexity #

    With \(n\) observations, \(G\) values of \(\theta\), \(A\) values of \(\alpha\) and \(H\) forecast steps:

    • One static pass: \(O(n)\) time and \(O(n)\) memory for the arrays.
    • One dynamic pass: the same, with a slightly larger constant.
    • Alpha search: about \(50+6\) passes per theta, so \(O(An)\).
    • Full fit: \(O(GAn)\); with \(G=11\) and \(A\approx56\) this is a few hundred passes, independent of the forecast horizon.
    • Prediction: \(O(H)\) for the static models and \(O(H)\) for the dynamic recursion, since the state updates are scalar.
    • Interval: \(O(H)\) plus one normal quantile.

    The demo's four fits on 264 points take about a quarter of a second in pure Python. The dominant cost is the nested alpha search, not the state recursion; if the grid were replaced by a golden-section search the fit would be roughly ten times faster, at the cost of a unimodality assumption that the sum of squares does not always satisfy.

    Failure modes #

    Theta is not a seasonal model. It has no seasonal component, so applying it directly to monthly data with a strong calendar pattern produces a forecast that ignores the pattern entirely. The standard practice is to deseasonalise first with STL or seasonal means, forecast the adjusted series, and add the seasonal component back. The demo uses non-seasonal series for that reason.

    The drift is half a global slope. Equation (7) uses the OLS slope of the whole record. If the trend changed direction, the global slope is an average of two regimes and the forecast is wrong in both. The dynamic variants address this, but only partially: their slope state is a local linear fit, not a breakpoint detector. A structural-break test, or a model with a time-varying trend, is the right tool when the break is the phenomenon of interest.

    A boundary theta is a warning. optimised_theta choosing \(\theta\) at the top of the grid means the objective still wants more curvature. Extending the grid is legitimate, but a very large \(\theta\) makes the theta line close to the original series and the forecast close to SES, which may be worse out of sample even when the in-sample sum of squares improves. Check the held-out error, as the demo does, before accepting a boundary solution.

    The dynamic model has a violent transient. The state-space initialisation sets \(A_0=B_0=0\), so \(B_1=3Y_1\) and the first few one-step forecasts are far from the data. The implementation trims those residuals from the objective, but the states still carry the transient for a while. If the series starts near zero, or has a huge first observation, the early forecasts are unusable. Fit on a longer prefix, or initialise the states from a preliminary OLS line.

    The interval is too narrow. Equation (16) accounts for the smoothing recursion but not for parameter uncertainty, model misspecification, or autocorrelated residuals. On a series with a cycle the residuals are strongly autocorrelated, and the true predictive interval is much wider than the printed one. Use the interval as a relative width, not as a coverage guarantee.

    Theta and SES share a blind spot. Both forecast a level, and both lag a trend by roughly \((1-\alpha)/\alpha\) times the slope. The theta drift corrects the average lag, but it cannot correct a lag that changes over time. If the residuals of the fitted model still show a trend, the drift is mis-specified; plot the residuals against time before trusting the forecast.

    Alpha near one. A fitted \(\alpha\) close to one means the model is almost the naive forecast; the drift term then dominates, and the method is effectively a random walk with a fixed slope. That is sometimes right, but it is worth checking whether the series is better described as a random walk with no drift at all.

    Applications #

    Theta is a workhorse in the M-competitions and in production forecasting stacks for retail and demand planning, where its accuracy is close to much more elaborate models at a fraction of the cost. The forecTheta package and the statsforecast implementations are widely used, and the method is often the first non-naive benchmark in a forecasting comparison.

    In energy and utilities it is applied to seasonally adjusted load, where the trend is slow and the seasonal pattern is removed first. In finance it is used on log prices and on realised volatility, both of which have a strong local trend and no stable seasonal component. In inventory control it forecasts the demand rate for slow-moving items, usually after intermittent-demand handling such as Croston's method.

    It is the wrong tool when seasonality is the signal, when the trend has a turning point inside the forecast horizon, or when the series is intermittent. It is also not a probabilistic model: the interval in (16) is a rough Gaussian band around a deterministic path, not a likelihood. For those cases use a state-space ETS model, a seasonal ARIMA, or a count model, and keep theta as the baseline that they must beat.
