    Naive baselines — the forecasts your model has to beat

    Intuition #

    A forecast is a claim about the future, and the cheapest possible claim is that nothing changes:

    \[\hat y_{T+h}=y_T\qquad\text{for every }h\ge1. \qquad (1)\]

    That is the naive forecast, and on the M4 and M5 competitions it is embarrassing how much of the field it beats. The reason is not that the world is static; it is that a random walk is a good approximation for prices, inventories, click counts and demand, and for a random walk the last value is not merely a baseline — it is the conditional mean. Any model that loses to it has explained nothing.

    This file contains the five baselines that every forecasting result should be reported against, plus the two aliases the literature uses for them, and the predictive intervals that separate them. The interesting part is not the point forecasts, which are one line each, but the variance: the naive forecast and the random-walk forecast are the same number, yet one assumes the forecast error is constant in \(h\) and the other assumes it grows like \(\sqrt h\). Over a horizon of ten, one of those is wrong by a factor of three.

    Variants #

    variant point forecast error scale distribution
    naive \(y_T\) \(\hat\sigma\) constant none
    random_walk \(y_T\) \(\hat\sigma\sqrt h\) Gaussian
    drift \(y_T+h\hat b\) \(\hat\sigma\sqrt{h(1+h/(T-1))}\) Gaussian
    mean \(\bar y\) \(\hat\sigma\sqrt{1+1/T}\) Gaussian
    seasonal_naive \(y_{T+h-m\lceil h/m\rceil}\) \(\hat\sigma\) constant none
    snaive same as seasonal_naive same same

    naive is (1). Use it as the denominator of every accuracy ratio, and use its in-sample residuals to see whether the series has structure left.

    random_walk is the same point rule with the model behind it made explicit: \(y_t=y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\) with \(\varepsilon_t\) i.i.d. Then the \(h\)-step error is a sum of \(h\) innovations, so its variance is \(h\sigma^2\) and the interval fans out. The demo's band is 3.243 wide at \(h=1\) and 10.254 at \(h=10\), exactly \(\sqrt{10}\) times wider.

    drift adds the average historical change, \(\hat b=(y_T-y_1)/(T-1)\), which is the maximum-likelihood slope of a random walk with drift. It wins on the demo's trending series (MAE 1.3616 against 1.3939) and loses badly on anything that mean-reverts.

    mean is the forecast for a stationary series: the sample average repeated. It is the only variant whose interval includes the error in estimating the mean, the \(1/T\) term, and it is usually the worst of the five in practice because economic series are rarely stationary.

    seasonal_naive recycles the last observed cycle. With a period of 12 on the demo's seasonal series its MAE is 0.5068 against 1.3939 for the naive, and on the seasonal series with no trend the gap is 0.145 against 1.515. Getting \(m\) right is the entire method.

    snaive is the name the R ecosystem uses for the same estimator, so the implementation returns the same forecasts and the demo asserts it. It is listed separately because readers search for it, not because it computes anything else.

    Derivation #

    With observations \(y_1,\dots,y_T\) and horizon \(h=1,2,\dots\), the four distinct point rules are

    \[\hat y_{T+h}=y_{T+h-m\lceil h/m\rceil} \qquad (2)\]

    for the seasonal naive (the observation one cycle back, \(m\) being the period),

    \[\hat y_{T+h}=y_T+h\hat b,\qquad \hat b=\frac{y_T-y_1}{T-1} \qquad (3)\]

    for drift, and

    \[\hat y_{T+h}=\bar y=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_t \qquad (4)\]

    for the mean. The naive rule is (1). All four are linear in the data with coefficients that do not depend on \(h\) except through (3), which is why they are the right thing to compute before anything expensive.

    The error scale #

    Each rule has a one-step residual it implies, and \(\hat\sigma\) is the sample standard deviation of that residual:

    \[\hat\sigma^2= \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_t \hat e_t^2,\qquad \hat e_t= \begin{cases} y_t-y_{t-1}, & \text{naive, random walk}\\ y_t-y_{t-1}-\hat b, & \text{drift}\\ y_t-y_{t-m}, & \text{seasonal}\\ y_t-\bar y, & \text{mean} \end{cases} \qquad (5)\]

    The ddof=1 matters: with \(T=20\) the difference between dividing by 19 and 20 is 5% of the variance and 2.5% of the interval width, which is the same order as the effect being measured.

    Predictive variance #

    For the random walk, the \(h\)-step error is \(\sum_{j=1}^{h}\varepsilon_{T+j}\), so

    \[\mathrm{Var}(\hat y_{T+h}-y_{T+h})=h\sigma^2 . \qquad (6)\]

    For the drift method the slope is estimated, and the estimation error accumulates with the forecast, giving the standard approximation

    \[\mathrm{Var}=h\sigma^2\Big(1+\frac{h}{T-1}\Big). \qquad (7)\]

    For the mean, the forecast error is the deviation of one new observation from an estimated mean:

    \[\mathrm{Var}=\sigma^2\Big(1+\frac1T\Big). \qquad (8)\]

    For naive and seasonal naive the implementation uses the constant \(\hat\sigma^2\) of (5): the naive method has no model that would justify a growing variance, and pretending otherwise would be an assumption, not a baseline. That is the honest asymmetry between naive and random_walk in the table — same number, different claim.

    The band is Gaussian,

    \[\hat y_{T+h}\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\hat\sigma_h, \qquad (9)\]

    with \(z\) from the standard normal quantile. For the random walk that is not an approximation but the exact predictive law when the innovations are Gaussian, which is why the demo can test it: 400 simulated walks give 0.948 coverage at \(h=1\) and 0.965 at \(h=10\) against a nominal 0.95.

    Code walkthrough #

    fit computes every statistic any variant could need and then the one scale that variant implies. The seasonal branch is where the period enters:

            if self.variant in SEASONAL:
            if self.season > self.n_ - 3:
                raise ValueError("season leaves too few seasonal differences")
            gap = y[self.season:] - y[:-self.season]
            self.sigma_ = float(np.std(gap, ddof=1))
            self.seasonal_ = y[-self.season:].copy()

    gap is the vector of \(y_t-y_{t-m}\), computed with two slices rather than a loop; self.seasonal_ keeps the last cycle so that predict can recycle it without knowing anything else. The guard is not cosmetic: with season > n_ - 3 there are fewer than three seasonal differences and ddof=1 is undefined.

    Drift estimates its slope from the endpoints, which is the least-squares slope only because the regressor is time:

            elif self.variant == "drift":
            if self.n_ < 3:
                raise ValueError("the drift method needs at least three points")
            self.slope_ = (y[-1] - y[0]) / (self.n_ - 1)
            residual = np.diff(y) - self.slope_
            self.sigma_ = float(np.std(residual, ddof=1))

    The residual is the difference minus the slope, not the level minus a fitted line. That is the one-step error the method actually makes, and it is the right scale for (7).

    predict is a dispatch on the variant, with the seasonal branch expressed as an index into the stored cycle:

            steps = np.arange(1, horizon + 1, dtype=float)
        if self.variant in ("naive", "random_walk"):
            return np.full(horizon, self.last_)
        if self.variant == "mean":
            return np.full(horizon, self.mean_)
        if self.variant == "drift":
            return self.last_ + steps * self.slope_
        index = (np.arange(horizon) % self.season)
        return self.seasonal_[index]

    np.arange(horizon) % self.season is the whole of (2) once the cycle is stored in order: \(h=1\) reads seasonal_[0], which is \(y_{T-m+1}\), and \(h=m\) reads seasonal_[m-1], which is \(y_T\). Writing the index from 1 and forgetting the shift is the usual off-by-one here, and it shows up as a forecast one period out of phase.

    The interval is the only place the variants diverge after fit, and each branch is one line of (6)–(8):

            if self.variant == "random_walk":
            sd = self.sigma_ * np.sqrt(steps)
        elif self.variant == "drift":
            sd = self.sigma_ * np.sqrt(steps * (1.0 + steps / (self.n_ - 1)))
        elif self.variant == "mean":
            sd = np.full(horizon, self.sigma_ * np.sqrt(1.0 + 1.0 / self.n_))
        else:
            sd = np.full(horizon, self.sigma_)
        z = NormalDist().inv_cdf(0.5 + level / 2.0)
        return forecast - z * sd, forecast + z * sd

    NormalDist is in the standard library and removes any temptation to hardcode 1.96; the quantile is exact for any level. Note that steps is float, so np.sqrt broadcasts without an integer division trap on older NumPy.

    The guards are two small functions, and the split between them is deliberate:

        def _check_horizon(self, horizon) -> int:
        if not isinstance(horizon, (int, np.integer)) or horizon < 1:
            raise ValueError("horizon must be a positive integer")
        return int(horizon)

    A float horizon of 2.5 raises rather than truncating: silently forecasting 2 steps when 2.5 were asked for is a bug that surfaces as a shape mismatch three layers up. The isinstance test also rejects True, which is an int in Python and would otherwise be a one-step forecast.

    Complexity #

    With \(T\) observations and horizon \(h\):

    • fit: \(O(T)\) time and \(O(T)\) memory for the copy of the series; the seasonal branch stores only \(m\) values.
    • predict: \(O(h)\).
    • interval: \(O(h)\).
    • All variants together: the whole demo, including 400 simulated random walks of length 120 and the coverage check, is a few hundred thousand floating point operations.

    This is the cheapest atom in the book, and that is the point: any model that costs more must be justified against a forecast that costs nothing.

    Failure modes #

    Naive on a trending series. If the series has a stable trend, last-value is biased by exactly the trend per step, and the error grows linearly with \(h\) rather than staying constant. The demo shows the drift method winning on its trending series, but the margin is small (1.3616 against 1.3939) because the season dominates; on a pure trend the gap is the trend itself. Diagnose by plotting the residuals of the naive forecast against \(h\): a fan shape means drift, a constant band means a random walk.

    The wrong seasonal period is worse than no season. A seasonal naive with \(m\) off by one forecasts the wrong phase, and the result can be worse than the naive. The demo asserts the reverse ordering on data where \(m=12\) is correct. Diagnose by comparing MAE with the naive on held-out data before believing any seasonal model; if seasonal naive loses, the period or the phase is wrong.

    Intervals that ignore regime change. Equation (6) assumes the innovations are stationary. A volatility break makes the band too narrow, and the coverage check that passes on simulated walks fails on real data. The diagnostic is a rolling coverage plot, not a single number, and no baseline can fix it: the interval is only as good as the stationarity assumption behind \(\hat\sigma\).

    Drift extrapolates a slope that has already changed. The estimate uses only the first and last observation, so a single outlier at either end moves the whole forecast path. With \(T=20\) one bad endpoint can flip the sign. Diagnose by recomputing \(\hat b\) on the second half of the series; if it changes materially, drift is not a baseline but a bet.

    Applications #

    Every forecasting benchmark is scored relative to these methods: MASE divides by the in-sample MAE of the naive (or seasonal naive) forecast, and the M4/M5 leaderboards are read that way. Reporting a model without the naive number is reporting an unnormalised error.

    Operationally, random_walk is the null model for prices, inventories and anything with a unit root, and the interval of (6) is what a "no change" scenario should look like in a planning tool. seasonal_naive is the standard for weekly and yearly cycles — call volumes, retail, energy — and is often the production model for a new SKU with no history. drift is the honest default for a slowly moving KPI over a short horizon.

    They are a trap as probabilistic forecasts on autocorrelated data, where the residuals are not iid and every interval here is too narrow, and as models for series with a known driver (price, promotion, weather) where the whole point is to use it.
