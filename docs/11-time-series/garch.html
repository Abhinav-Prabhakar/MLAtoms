    GARCH — variance that clusters, reacts to bad news and remembers

    Intuition #

    Returns are hard to forecast; their variance is not. Large moves follow large moves, small moves follow small moves, and the autocorrelation of squared returns is one of the most reliable facts in finance. A GARCH model writes that down as a recursion: today's conditional variance is a constant plus a weighted average of last period's squared shock and last period's variance.

    That single change — letting \(\sigma_t^2\) depend on the past — turns a homoskedastic regression into a model of volatility clustering. It also explains why the model is fitted by maximum likelihood rather than least squares: each observation has its own variance, and the likelihood is the product of \(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma_t^2)\) densities with \(\sigma_t^2\) generated recursively from the parameters.

    The variants each answer a different stylised fact:

    • arch is the original: variance depends on recent squared shocks only. It needs many lags to capture persistence, which is why GARCH replaced it.
    • garch adds lagged variance, giving a two-parameter filter that matches the slow decay of volatility with \(\alpha+\beta\) near one.
    • gjr_garch adds a term for negative shocks, the leverage effect: a fall raises future volatility more than an equally sized rise.
    • egarch models \(\log\sigma_t^2\), so variance stays positive without constraints and the asymmetry enters through a signed \(z_{t-1}\).
    • igarch sets \(\alpha+\beta=1\) exactly: shocks never decay, and the variance forecast becomes a random walk with drift \(\omega\).
    • garch_t replaces the Gaussian innovation with a standardised Student-t, which is what makes the model usable on daily equity returns.

    The demo fits each variant to data simulated from it, checks the conditional variance recursion by hand, and checks the two limits that matter: GARCH forecasts mean-revert to \(\omega/(1-\alpha-\beta)\), while IGARCH forecasts grow linearly.

    Variants #

    variant variance recursion demo result

    garch plus \(\sum\beta_j\sigma^2_{t-j}\) \(\alpha+\beta=0.9780\)

    arch estimates a variance that is a distributed lag of squared shocks. On 1 000 points with true \(\omega=0.5,\alpha=0.5\) it returns \(0.4973\) and \(0.4403\); the one-step forecast is exactly \(\omega+\alpha e_T^2\). It is the right diagnostic for short memory and the wrong model for daily equities, where the estimated \(\alpha\) is small and the fit is poor: on the GARCH data below its AIC is 3663.49 against 3526.52.

    garch is the default. The demo's fit has \(\alpha=0.1038\), \(\beta=0.8742\) against a true \((0.08,0.9)\), and persistence \(0.9780\). Its variance forecast converges to \(2.5438\), matching \(\omega/(1-\alpha-\beta)\) to six digits, and its standardised residuals have variance \(0.993\) — the recursion is not leaving structure behind.

    egarch writes \(\log\sigma_t^2\) as an AR(1) with an asymmetric term. On a simulated path with \(\gamma=-0.08\) it recovers \(\gamma=-0.0742\) and \(\beta=0.9039\). The estimate of \(\alpha\) is poor (\(0.0123\) against \(0.1\)) because the intercept and \(\alpha\) trade off against each other; the leverage sign, which is the point of the model, is recovered.

    gjr_garch adds a non-negative \(\gamma\) on negative squared shocks. The demo's true \(\gamma=0.1\) gives an estimate of \(0.0734\), and the model beats the symmetric one on AIC by only 1.63 points. That margin is a useful warning: with 1 000 observations, leverage is real but not precisely estimated.

    igarch fixes \(\beta=1-\alpha\), so a shock has a permanent effect on the variance level. The demo recovers \(\alpha=0.2240\) and \(\beta=0.7760\), and every forecast step adds exactly \(\omega=0.0899\) — the linear growth that makes IGARCH useful for risk over short horizons and dangerous over long ones.

    garch_t estimates the degrees of freedom by likelihood. On data with \(\nu=5\) it returns \(4.1032\) and improves the log-likelihood from \(-1905.96\) to \(-1846.06\). The 95% interval at one step is \(1.96\) times the Gaussian width; that extra tail is the reason the Gaussian model understates risk on daily returns.

    Derivation #

    Mean and variance #

    Write the return as a constant mean plus a scaled innovation:

    \[r_t=\mu+e_t,\qquad e_t=\sigma_t z_t,\qquad \mathbb E[z_t]=0,\quad \mathrm{Var}(z_t)=1 . \qquad (1)\]

    The whole family differs only in how \(\sigma_t^2\) is generated from \(\mathcal F_{t-1}\), the information available at \(t-1\).

    GARCH(p, q) #

    The baseline recursion is

    \[\sigma_t^2=\omega+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_i e_{t-i}^2 +\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_j\sigma_{t-j}^2, \qquad (2)\]

    with \(\omega>0\), \(\alpha_i\ge0\) and \(\beta_j\ge0\). For \(p=1,q=0\) this is ARCH(\(q\)), and for \(p=q=1\) it is the familiar GARCH(1,1). Taking expectations under stationarity gives

    \[\mathbb E[\sigma^2]=\frac{\omega}{1-\sum_i\alpha_i-\sum_j\beta_j}, \qquad (3)\]

    so the denominator must be positive. The quantity \(\sum\alpha_i+\sum\beta_j\) is the persistence: it is the decay rate of a shock to the variance, and it is the number the demo prints first.

    An equivalent way to see the memory is to substitute \(e_t^2=\sigma_t^2z_t^2\): for GARCH(1,1), \(e_t^2=\omega+(\alpha+\beta)e_{t-1}^2+\eta_t\) with \(\eta_t\) a martingale difference. Squared returns are therefore ARMA(1,1)-like, which is why they have a slowly decaying autocorrelation even when returns themselves are white noise.

    GJR-GARCH #

    The leverage variant adds an indicator for the sign of the last shock:

    \[\sigma_t^2=\omega+\alpha e_{t-1}^2+\beta\sigma_{t-1}^2 +\gamma e_{t-1}^2\mathbf 1[e_{t-1}<0]. \qquad (4)\]

    For symmetric \(z\), \(\mathbb E[e^2\mathbf 1[e<0]]=\sigma^2/2\), so the persistence becomes \(\alpha+\beta+\gamma/2\). The code uses exactly that expression in the stationarity constraint and in persistence_. A positive \(\gamma\) means a negative shock raises variance more than a positive one of the same size — the leverage effect.

    EGARCH #

    Nelson's exponential form models the log variance:

    \[\log\sigma_t^2=\log\omega +\alpha\bigl(\lvert z_{t-1}\rvert-\mathbb E\lvert z\rvert\bigr) +\gamma z_{t-1}+\beta\log\sigma_{t-1}^2, \qquad (5)\]

    with \(\mathbb E\lvert z\rvert=\sqrt{2/\pi}\) for Gaussian \(z\). Positivity is automatic because the right-hand side is exponentiated. A negative \(\gamma\) means a negative \(z_{t-1}\) raises log variance, so the leverage sign is the opposite of GJR's \(\gamma\). The \(\alpha\) term captures the size of the shock independent of its sign; the \(\gamma\) term captures the sign.

    IGARCH #

    The integrated model imposes

    \[\alpha+\beta=1, \qquad (6)\]

    so (2) has a unit root in the variance. The forecast does not revert to a constant: at horizon \(h\),

    \[\hat\sigma_{T+h}^2=\hat\sigma_{T+1}^2+(h-1)\omega, \qquad (7)\]

    which is the linear growth the demo verifies by differencing the forecast path. IGARCH is the volatility analogue of a random walk with drift, and it should be read the same way: fine for a few steps, meaningless for a long horizon.

    Likelihood #

    For Gaussian innovations, the conditional log-likelihood is

    \[\ell(\theta)=-\frac12\sum_{t=s}^{n}\left[ \log2\pi+\log\sigma_t^2+\frac{e_t^2}{\sigma_t^2}\right], \qquad (8)\]

    where the first \(s\) observations are used to initialise the recursion but excluded from the sum. The code sets \(s=\max(p,q,10)\) capped at \(n/4\).

    For Student-t innovations, standardise \(z=t_\nu/\sqrt{\nu/(\nu-2)}\) so that \(\mathrm{Var}(z)=1\). The density of \(e_t\) is then

    \[f(e_t)=\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right)} {\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)\sqrt{\pi(\nu-2)}\,\sigma_t} \left(1+\frac{e_t^2}{(\nu-2)\sigma_t^2}\right)^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}}, \qquad (9)\]

    and (8) is replaced by the sum of its logarithms. The degrees of freedom are estimated jointly with the variance parameters; nu_ is the fitted value.

    Forecasting #

    Because \(e_{T+h}^2=\sigma_{T+h}^2z_{T+h}^2\) has conditional expectation \(\sigma_{T+h}^2\), the GARCH forecast is deterministic:

    \[\hat\sigma_{T+h}^2=\omega +\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_i\,\mathbb E[e_{T+h-i}^2] +\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_j\,\hat\sigma_{T+h-j}^2, \qquad (10)\]

    with observed values used where the index is at or before \(T\) and forecasts used afterwards. For GJR the future asymmetric term contributes \(\gamma\hat\sigma^2/2\), and for IGARCH the same recursion with \(\beta=1-\alpha\) produces (7).

    For EGARCH, \(\mathbb E[\alpha(\lvert z\rvert-\mathbb E\lvert z\rvert)+\gamma z]=0\), so for \(h\ge2\) the log variance follows \(\log\hat\sigma_{T+h}^2=\log\omega+\beta\log\hat\sigma_{T+h-1}^2\). The code returns the exponential of this path. That is the conditional median of \(\sigma^2\), not its mean: exponentiating an expectation of a log is not the expectation of the level. The distinction is small at short horizons and grows with \(\beta\), and it is the one place where predict returns an approximation rather than an exact conditional moment.

    Information criteria #

    With \(k\) estimated parameters — \(\mu\), \(\omega\), the \(\alpha\)'s, the \(\beta\)'s, plus \(\gamma\) or \(\nu\) where present — the demo compares

    \[\mathrm{AIC}=-2\ell+2k,\qquad \mathrm{BIC}=-2\ell+k\log n_{\text{used}} . \qquad (11)\]

    The likelihoods are on the same return scale, so the comparison is valid within a data set. It is not valid across different mean specifications, and it is not a test: the GJR improvement of 1.63 AIC points is suggestive, not decisive.

    Code walkthrough #

    The parameter vector is a flat array, and _unpack turns it into named quantities. The IGARCH constraint lives here rather than in the likelihood, which keeps the search one-dimensional in \(\alpha\):

        def _unpack(self, theta: np.ndarray) -> dict:
        index = 2
        if self.variant == "arch":
            alpha = theta[index:index + self.q].copy()
            beta = np.zeros(0)
            index += self.q
        else:
            alpha = theta[index:index + self.p].copy()
            index += self.p
            if self.variant == "igarch":
                beta = np.asarray([1.0 - float(alpha[0])])
            else:
                beta = theta[index:index + self.q].copy()
                index += self.q

    Index \(0\) is \(\mu\) and index \(1\) is \(\log\omega\); the logarithm is what keeps \(\omega\) positive without a constrained optimiser. For IGARCH the \(\beta\) slot is not even allocated, so the reported \(\alpha+\beta\) is exactly one by construction, not by a penalty.

    The core recursion is the model written as code. The t >= lag guards are what let the first few observations use the sample variance as a backcast instead of wrapping around to the end of the array:

                arch_lags = self.q if self.variant == "arch" else self.p
            for t in range(1, n):
                value = omega
                for lag in range(1, arch_lags + 1):
                    past = errors[t - lag] ** 2 if t >= lag else scale
                    value += alpha[lag - 1] * past
                if beta.size:
                    for lag in range(1, self.q + 1):
                        past = variance[t - lag] if t >= lag else scale
                        value += beta[lag - 1] * past
                if gamma:
                    value += gamma * errors[t - 1] ** 2 * (errors[t - 1] < 0.0)

    The GJR term is the last two lines: errors[t - 1] < 0.0 is False or True, and multiplying by the boolean is the indicator. A naive implementation that forgets this branch still fits a GARCH and looks plausible; only the asymmetry diagnostics reveal the missing parameter.

    EGARCH is a separate branch because the state is the log variance, not the variance:

                for t in range(1, n):
                z = errors[t - 1] / math.sqrt(variance[t - 1])
                log_variance = (math.log(omega) + alpha[0] * (abs(z) - SQRT_2_OVER_PI)
                                + gamma * z + beta[0] * log_variance)
                if not np.isfinite(log_variance) or log_variance > 50.0:
                    return None
                variance[t] = math.exp(log_variance)

    The clip at \(50\) is a guard against an explosive candidate during the search; a log variance above \(50\) means a variance above \(5\times10^{21}\), which is numerically useless and should be rejected rather than propagated.

    The objective is the negative log-likelihood, with the stationarity constraints checked before the recursion:

            if (self.variant in ("garch", "garch_t")
                and params["alpha"].sum() + params["beta"].sum() >= 0.999):
            return math.inf
        if self.variant == "gjr_garch":
            total = (params["alpha"].sum() + params["beta"].sum()
                     + 0.5 * params["gamma"])
            if total >= 0.999:
                return math.inf

    Returning inf rather than raising is deliberate: the pattern search treats an infeasible point as a bad point and steps away, while an exception would abort the whole fit. The 0.5 * gamma is the expectation of the leverage term under a symmetric innovation law.

    The two likelihoods differ only in the per-observation constant and the power on the ratio:

            if self.variant == "garch_t":
            nu = params["nu"]
            constant = (math.lgamma(0.5 * (nu + 1.0)) - math.lgamma(0.5 * nu)
                        - 0.5 * math.log(math.pi * (nu - 2.0)))
            loglik = float(np.sum(constant - 0.5 * np.log(var)
                                  - 0.5 * (nu + 1.0) * np.log1p(ratio / (nu - 2.0))))
        else:
            loglik = float(-0.5 * np.sum(np.log(2.0 * math.pi) + np.log(var)
                                         + ratio))

    np.log1p(ratio / (nu - 2.0)) is the log of \(1+e^2/((\nu-2)\sigma^2)\); using log1p instead of log(1 + ...) matters when the ratio is tiny, which happens for observations in a quiet period. The lgamma terms are the normalising constant of the \(t\) density.

    Because the objective is cheap and low-dimensional, the fit uses coordinate pattern search with step halving rather than a gradient method:

            for _ in range(70):
            improved = False
            for index in range(best.size):
                for delta in (steps[index], -steps[index]):
                    trial = best.copy()
                    low, high = bounds[index]
                    trial[index] = min(max(trial[index] + delta, low), high)
                    if trial[index] == best[index]:
                        continue
                    value = self._objective(trial, r)
                    if value < score - 1e-10:
                        best, score, improved = trial, value, True
            if not improved:
                steps *= 0.5
                if steps.max() < 1e-5:
                    break

    The bounds are the positivity and stationarity constraints in disguise, and steps.max() < 1e-5 is the stopping rule. Pattern search is slower than a gradient by a constant factor but it does not need derivatives through the recursion, which is where a hand-written GARCH gradient usually goes wrong.

    Forecasting reuses the recursion with observed values for the early lags and forecasts afterwards. The future list holds \(\hat e^2\), which equals \(\hat\sigma^2\) under (10):

                if self.beta_.size:
                for lag in range(1, self.q + 1):
                    past = out[h - lag] if lag <= h else variance[h - lag]
                    value += self.beta_[lag - 1] * past
            if self.gamma_:
                if h == 0:
                    value += (self.gamma_ * observed[-1]
                              * (errors[-1] < 0.0))
                else:
                    value += self.gamma_ * 0.5 * future[h - 1]

    At \(h=0\) the sign of the last observed shock is known, so the indicator is used directly. For every later step the sign is unknown and the code uses the symmetric expectation \(1/2\), which is why the leverage forecast decays at the rate \(\alpha+\beta+\gamma/2\).

    The interval uses the innovation law, not the variance recursion:

            variance = self.predict(horizon)
        if self.variant == "garch_t":
            z = _t_quantile(0.5 + level / 2.0, self.nu_
                            ) * math.sqrt(self.nu_ / (self.nu_ - 2.0))
        else:
            z = NormalDist().inv_cdf(0.5 + level / 2.0)
        spread = z * np.sqrt(variance)
        return self.mu_ - spread, self.mu_ + spread

    The extra factor \(\sqrt{\nu/(\nu-2)}\) converts the raw \(t\) quantile to the quantile of the standardised \(t\) used in (9). Omitting it makes the band too wide by a factor that grows as \(\nu\) falls, which is the opposite of the intended correction.

    Complexity #

    With \(n\) returns, \(p+q\) variance lags, \(H\) forecast steps and \(P\) searched parameters:

    • One variance path: \(O(n(p+q))\) time and \(O(n)\) memory.
    • One likelihood: the same, plus \(O(n)\) for the log terms.
    • One fit: about \(70\times2P\) objective evaluations before halving; with \(P\le6\) that is under a thousand paths, so \(O(nP^2)\) in practice.
    • Forecast: \(O(H(p+q))\) after the fit.
    • Interval: \(O(H)\) plus one \(t\) quantile, which is 120 bisection steps on the incomplete beta function.
    • Memory: \(O(n)\) for the variance, residuals and standardised residuals.

    The demo's seven fits on 1 000 points take about four seconds in pure Python; the recursion is a scalar loop, and that is the price of keeping it readable. For \(n\) in the millions the loop should be moved to a compiled kernel, but the model is usually fitted to daily or monthly data, where \(n\) is in the thousands.

    Failure modes #

    Persistence near one. A fitted \(\alpha+\beta=0.99\) implies a half-life of \(\log0.5/\log0.99\approx69\) days and a long-run variance \(\omega/(1-\alpha-\beta)\) that is extremely sensitive to the denominator. The demo's \(0.9780\) is healthy; a value of \(0.999\) on the same data usually means the sample contains a structural break, and the forecast will look calm for a while and then be wrong by a large factor. Check the persistence and the long-run variance together, not just the likelihood.

    Leverage is hard to identify. The GJR improvement in the demo is 1.63 AIC points on 1 000 observations with a true \(\gamma=0.1\). On shorter samples the sign can flip. A positive \(\gamma\) in one sample and a negative one in the next is not evidence that the market changed; it is the standard error doing its job.

    The mean is still a model. (1) assumes a constant mean. If returns have an AR component, or a day-of-week effect, the residuals feed a biased \(e_t^2\) into the variance recursion and the persistence estimate absorbs the mean misspecification. The standard fix is ARMA-GARCH: fit the conditional mean first, then the variance on its residuals.

    EGARCH forecasts are medians. As noted under (10), predict exponentiates the expected log variance. \(\mathbb E[\exp(X)]\ge\exp(\mathbb E[X])\), so the reported variance is below the true conditional mean, and the gap grows with the variance of \(\log\sigma^2\). For risk applications, either simulate the log-variance path or apply the lognormal correction; do not treat the printed number as \(\mathbb E[\sigma^2]\).

    Fat tails are not the same as volatility clustering. A Student-t fit can raise the likelihood substantially even when the data have no GARCH structure, because the tail parameter is doing the work. Compare garch_t against garch on the same data, as the demo does: the t model wins by 59.9 log-likelihood points here, but the variance parameters still have to be significant on their own.

    Zero or constant returns. The fit refuses a constant series because the initial variance is zero. A series with long runs of exact zeros — a low-price stock, a thinly traded asset — will fit a tiny \(\omega\) and a persistence near one for the wrong reason. Aggregate or model the zero process separately.

    Applications #

    GARCH is the standard language of financial risk. Value-at-Risk uses the one-step variance forecast and a tail quantile; expected shortfall uses the same recursion with a different tail integral. The 2008 crisis made the distinction between Gaussian and Student-t innovations a practical matter: Gaussian GARCH understated daily losses precisely when the tail mattered, and the t variant is now the default in most risk systems.

    Option pricing uses GARCH as a discrete-time stochastic volatility model: Duan's (1995) transform gives a risk-neutral recursion, and the model reproduces the volatility smile without continuous-time Brownian motion. Volatility targeting in portfolio construction uses the forecast as a denominator, scaling exposure inversely to predicted risk.

    Outside finance, the same recursion models inflation uncertainty, the volatility of electricity prices, and the variance of sensor noise. The model is the wrong tool when the variance is driven by an observable covariate rather than by its own past — then a regression with heteroskedastic errors, or a stochastic volatility model with a latent AR(1) log variance, is the better starting point. GARCH is also a poor choice for point forecasts of returns: its mean forecast is \(\mu\), and the interesting content is entirely in the second moment.
