    Calibration

    Intuition #

    A probability is a promise about frequency. If a model says 0.9 on a thousand different cases, roughly nine hundred of them should be positive. Nothing in the training objective enforces that: a network trained to convergence on separable data drives its logits to infinity, and boosted trees average log-odds rather than probabilities, so both end up confidently wrong in opposite directions.

    Calibration is a second, tiny model fitted after the first one, whose only job is to repair the mapping from score to probability:

    base model  ──►  score s  ──►  calibrator g  ──►  p = g(s)
                                    ▲
                          fitted on held-out (s, y)

    Every variant here is a different g, and all of them are fitted by minimising log-loss on predictions the base model has never seen. The demo starts from a model that is exactly twice as sharp as the truth (logit(p) = 2 × logit(p_true)), which gives a clean target: the correct calibrator has to learn a slope of 0.5. Platt lands on 0.541, temperature on 2.014 when the truth is 2.0, and both cut expected calibration error from 0.090 to 0.031.

    Two things calibration does not do. It does not change accuracy — Platt, isotonic and temperature are strictly increasing, so the ranking of scores is untouched, and dirichlet moves it only slightly (0.800 → 0.805 in the demo). And it does not generalise across distributions: a calibrator fitted on last quarter's traffic encodes last quarter's base rates.

    Variants #

    Variant Map Parameters Fixes
    platt σ(a·logit(p) + b) 2 a global over/under-confidence
    isotonic monotone step function O(n) breakpoints any monotone distortion
    temperature softmax(z / T) 1 one shared over/under-confidence
    beta σ(a·log p + b·log(1−p) + c) 3 asymmetric distortion
    dirichlet softmax(W log p + b) k² + k per-class distortion
    reliability_diagram none — diagnostic 0 measures, does not fix

    platt and temperature are almost the same model. On binary probabilities, σ(logit(p)/T) is Platt with b = 0, so temperature is the one-parameter version and Platt the two-parameter version; on k classes only temperature generalises (softmax(z/T)), because a single scalar cannot rescale a matrix.

    isotonic is the non-parametric extreme: it fits any non-decreasing function, so it cannot be wrong about a monotone distortion — and it will happily fit noise. In the demo it uses 1000 breakpoints on 1000 training rows, and it still wins on test ECE (0.014 against Platt's 0.031) because the true distortion is monotone and the sample is large. Halve the calibration set and that ranking flips; isotonic is the variant that needs the most data per unit of flexibility.

    beta generalises Platt by allowing the two tails to be corrected differently: the features are log p and log(1−p), so it can fix a model that is overconfident near 0 and underconfident near 1. Its fitted coefficients in the demo (0.615, −0.479, intercept 0.200) are not equal to the "true" (0.5, −0.5, 0), because over the observed range of p the three parameters are nearly collinear — but the function they define is within 0.018 of the true probability. Fit quality is a property of the map, not of the coefficients.

    dirichlet is the multiclass version: a full k×k matrix applied to the log-probabilities. It contains temperature as W = I/T, and it can express per-class temperature and per-class bias. The demo's 3-class fit cuts ECE from 0.095 to 0.029 against temperature's 0.036, at the cost of nine parameters and a fixed-point optimiser instead of a one-dimensional search.

    reliability_diagram is the odd one out: it fits nothing and transforms nothing. It bins the predictions by confidence and reports the gap between confidence and accuracy in each bin — the diagnostic that tells you whether any of the others is needed.

    Derivation #

    What calibration means. A model is perfectly calibrated if

    \[P\bigl(Y = 1 \mid \hat p(X) = p\bigr) = p \quad \text{for all } p \in [0,1]\]

    This is a statement about conditional frequencies, so it can only be checked by grouping: bin the predictions, compare the mean prediction in each bin with the observed positive rate. That is the reliability diagram, and its weighted average gap is the expected calibration error:

    \[\mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{n_b}{n}\,\bigl|\mathrm{acc}(b) - \mathrm{conf}(b)\bigr|, \qquad \mathrm{MCE} = \max_b \bigl|\mathrm{acc}(b) - \mathrm{conf}(b)\bigr|\]

    ECE is a weighted mean of bin gaps and MCE the worst bin. Neither is a proper scoring rule: they depend on the binning, and they can be reduced by a degenerate predictor that puts everything in one bin. They are diagnostics, not objectives — which is why every calibrator here is fitted by minimising log-loss instead.

    Why log-loss. A scoring rule S(p, y) is proper if its expectation is minimised by the true probability. Log-loss is:

    \[\mathbb{E}\bigl[-\log \hat p\bigr] = H(Y) + \mathrm{KL}\bigl(P(Y\mid X)\,\|\,\hat p\bigr)\]

    The entropy term does not depend on the model and the KL is minimised (at zero) exactly when p̂ = P(Y|X). So minimising log-loss is minimising the KL to the true conditional distribution — the strongest possible statement that "the calibrated probabilities are right", and it holds for every bin simultaneously rather than on average over bins.

    Platt scaling. Fit p = σ(A·f + B) where f is the score (here f = logit(p)) by minimising −Σ log p_i. The gradient conditions are the logistic regression normal equations:

    \[\frac{\partial}{\partial A} = -\sum_i f_i\,(t_i - p_i) = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial B} = -\sum_i (t_i - p_i) = 0\]

    with t_i ∈ {0,1}. Platt's contribution is the target smoothing: replacing t by

    \[t_+ = \frac{N_+ + 1}{N_+ + 2}, \qquad t_- = \frac{1}{N_- + 2}\]

    Without it, a separable problem drives |A| → ∞ (the likelihood keeps increasing as the sigmoid steepens) and the calibrator becomes a step function. The smoothed targets are the posterior means of t under a Beta(1,1)-like prior on the class frequencies, which caps the slope at a finite value. The demo's data is not separable, so the correction is small, but it is the difference between a usable fit and a divergence on real SVM outputs.

    Isotonic regression. Find the non-decreasing g minimising

    \[\sum_i \bigl(y_i - g(p_i)\bigr)^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad g(p_{(1)}) \le g(p_{(2)}) \le \cdots\]

    after sorting by p. The solution is a step function whose blocks are the maximal segments over which the cumulative mean is monotone. Pool Adjacent Violators computes it in one pass: append each point as its own block, and whenever the previous block's value exceeds the current one, merge them and use the weighted mean. Each merge reduces the number of blocks by one, so the whole algorithm is O(n) after the sort.

    The fit is the maximum-likelihood estimate under the monotonicity constraint, and it is consistent (Grenander's theorem) but not smooth: with n distinct scores it has up to n breakpoints, which is exactly what the demo sees. That is also why it overfits small calibration sets — each block is fitted to the rows inside it, and a block with three rows has a standard error of about 0.3.

    Temperature scaling. For logits z with p = softmax(z), fit the single scalar T:

    \[p_T = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{z}{T}\right), \qquad T^\star = \arg\min_T \; -\sum_i \log p_T(y_i \mid x_i)\]

    T > 1 softens (fixes overconfidence), T < 1 sharpens (fixes underconfidence), and T = 1 is the identity. Dividing logits by a constant is a monotone operation per row, so the argmax — and therefore accuracy — is untouched by construction. Temperature is the standard tool for neural networks because one parameter cannot overfit a validation set, which is exactly the regime where the calibration set is small.

    Beta calibration. With s = log p and t = log(1−p), fit

    \[\mathrm{logit}(\hat p) = a\,s + b\,t + c\]

    Kull et al. motivate it from the Beta family: if the score density is Beta(α, β) and the class-conditional densities are also Beta, the posterior log-odds is linear in s, t, c. Two consequences are worth knowing: it contains Platt scaling (a = −b), and it maps the boundary values correctly — p → 0 gives −∞ whatever the coefficients, whereas Platt on logit(p) needs a clipped score to avoid infinities.

    Dirichlet calibration. Let z = log p and fit

    \[\hat p = \mathrm{softmax}\bigl(W z + b\bigr), \qquad W \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times k}\]

    This is multinomial logistic regression with the log-probabilities as features. It contains temperature (W = I/T, b = 0) and per-class Platt scaling (diagonal W). The softmax parameterisation has a redundancy — adding a constant to every row of W and to b leaves softmax unchanged — so the objective needs a tie-break; here an L2 penalty toward (I, 0) supplies one and also keeps W from collapsing. Kull et al.'s "off-diagonal regularisation" penalises only the off-diagonal entries, on the argument that a good calibrator mostly rescales each class and only mildly mixes them; the demo uses the simpler shrink-toward-identity version.

    Why these are all "post-hoc". Each variant is fitted to the pair (score, label) and never touches the base model. That is what makes them cheap and composable — and what makes the held-out requirement absolute: the calibrator is fitting the base model's errors, and in-sample errors are not the ones it will make in production.

    Code walkthrough #

    The diagnostic is a binned comparison, and its whole content is three bincount calls:

        edges = np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, n_bins + 1)
    idx = np.clip(np.digitize(conf, edges[1:-1]), 0, n_bins - 1)
    counts = np.bincount(idx, minlength=n_bins).astype(float)
    conf_sum = np.bincount(idx, weights=conf, minlength=n_bins)
    acc_sum = np.bincount(idx, weights=correct, minlength=n_bins)

    np.digitize returns 0 for values below the first interior edge and n_bins above the last, so the clip folds the overflow into the end bins. bincount with weights sums the confidences and the correctness indicators per bin in one pass each; dividing gives the bin's mean confidence and mean accuracy. keep = counts > 0 then drops empty bins, which matters because an empty bin has 0/0 and would poison the ECE with a nan.

    The ECE is the weighted mean gap, and the weights are the reason it is computable from bins:

        gaps = np.abs(mean_acc - mean_conf)
    ece = float(np.sum(counts[keep] / n * gaps))
    mce = float(gaps.max()) if gaps.size else 0.0

    counts[keep] / n is the fraction of the sample in each occupied bin, so ECE is the sample-weighted average of |acc − conf| — the quantity a reliability plot shows as the area between the diagonal and the curve.

    Platt scaling is logistic regression on the logit, with the smoothed targets:

            n_pos = float(y.sum())
        n_neg = float(y.size - n_pos)
        target = np.where(y > 0.0, (n_pos + 1.0) / (n_pos + 2.0), 1.0 / (n_neg + 2.0))
        F = np.column_stack([_logit(p), np.ones(p.size)])
        w = _fit_logistic(F, target, self.l2)

    np.where builds the two smoothed targets in one expression — positives get (N₊+1)/(N₊+2), negatives 1/(N₋+2). The design matrix is [logit(p), 1], so the fitted w[0] is the slope A and w[1] the intercept B. Fitting on target rather than y is the entire Platt correction; everything else is ordinary logistic regression.

    That regression is Newton's method, five lines of it:

            p = _sigmoid(F @ w)
        grad = F.T @ (p - y) + l2 * w
        weight = np.maximum(p * (1.0 - p), EPS)
        hess = (F * weight[:, None]).T @ F + l2 * np.eye(F.shape[1])
        step = np.linalg.solve(hess, grad)
        w -= step

    The Hessian of the logistic loss is Fᵀ diag(p(1−p)) F, which is exactly (F * weight[:, None]).T @ F — the weight column broadcast into F before the product. np.maximum guards the saturated case where p is exactly 0 or 1 and the Hessian becomes singular; with l2 > 0 the ridge term makes it invertible anyway. Convergence is quadratic and takes 5–8 iterations on these problems.

    Isotonic regression is the pooling loop:

        blocks: list[list[float]] = []       # [fitted value, weight, first unique index]
    for j in range(uniq.size):
        blocks.append([float(means[j]), float(counts[j]), j])
        while len(blocks) > 1 and blocks[-2][0] > blocks[-1][0]:
            v2, w2, _ = blocks.pop()
            v1, w1, start = blocks.pop()
            total = w1 + w2
            blocks.append([(v1 * w1 + v2 * w2) / total, total, start])

    Each block carries its own start index so the final expansion knows which scores it covers. The while is what makes it PAVA rather than a single merge: pooling two blocks can create a new violation with the block before them, and the loop keeps merging leftward until monotonicity is restored. Keeping the earlier start (start from the popped v1 block) is what extends the merged block over both ranges. > rather than >= keeps equal neighbours as separate blocks, which is harmless and keeps the output stable.

    Fitted values are then scattered back to the unique scores, and the transform is a lookup:

                idx = np.searchsorted(self.x_, p, side="right") - 1
            out = self.y_[np.clip(idx, 0, self.y_.size - 1)]

    side="right" - 1 gives the index of the largest fitted score ≤ p, which is the piecewise-constant reading of the isotonic fit: a value between two breakpoints takes the lower block's probability. Clipping handles p below the smallest or above the largest observed score. (scikit-learn interpolates linearly between block endpoints instead; the step is what the isotonic solution actually is, and the difference is confined to the gaps between blocks.)

    Temperature scaling is a one-dimensional search, and the search is over log T:

            grid = np.linspace(-3.0, 3.0, 601)
        vals = np.array([loss(np.array([g])) for g in grid])
        best = float(grid[int(np.argmin(vals))])
        self.temperature_ = float(np.exp(best))

    log T rather than T makes the grid symmetric around the identity: T = 0.05 and T = 20 are equally far from 1, which they are not on the linear scale. A 601-point grid over [e⁻³, e³] resolves T to about 1%, which is far finer than its standard error. The loss is the mean negative log-probability of the true class, computed in log-softmax form so no probability ever underflows.

    Dirichlet calibration is the one variant that needs a real optimiser, so it uses gradient descent with a backtracking line search:

            def grad(theta: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
            W, b = unpack(theta)
            s = _softmax(z @ W.T + b)
            g = (s - target) / y.size
            gW = g.T @ z + 2.0 * self.l2 * (W - np.eye(k))
            gb = g.sum(axis=0) + 2.0 * self.l2 * b
            return np.concatenate([gW.ravel(), gb])

    (s - target) is the softmax regression gradient — the same p − y as the binary case, one row per sample. g.T @ z contracts over samples to give a k×k matrix, and the regulariser pulls W toward the identity rather than toward zero, which is the prior "the calibrator is close to changing nothing". Flattening W and b into one vector lets a single generic minimiser handle it.

    The transform for the multiclass variants keeps probabilities normalised end to end:

                q = np.clip(p, EPS, 1.0)
            q = q / q.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
            out = _softmax(np.log(q) @ self.matrix_.T + self.bias_)

    Renormalising before the log is not cosmetic: if a caller passes probabilities that sum to 0.98 (a common artefact of ensembling), the log-probabilities are shifted, softmax absorbs the shift only if all classes share it — and they do not, because the shortfall is distributed unevenly. Normalising first makes the result independent of the input's rounding.

    Complexity #

    Variant Fit Transform Memory
    platt Newton, O(n·d²) for d = 2, ~8 iterations O(n) O(1) params
    beta same, d = 3 O(n) O(1)
    isotonic O(n log n) sort + O(n) pooling O(n log n) binary search, or O(n) on sorted input O(n) breakpoints
    temperature 601 × O(n k) O(n k) O(1)
    dirichlet ~400 × O(n k²) O(n k²) O(k²)
    reliability_diagram O(n + B) identity O(B)

    All of these are negligible next to one forward pass of the base model. isotonic is the memory outlier because it stores the entire step function — up to one breakpoint per row — which is also its overfitting risk. dirichlet is the compute outlier at O(k²) per sample per iteration, which matters only for many-class problems (language models with k = 50k do not use it; they use temperature or top-k variants).

    Failure modes #

    1. Fitting on the training set. The calibrator then learns the base model's training errors — usually near zero — and concludes the model needs no correction. Fit on a held-out split or on out-of-fold predictions.
    2. Distribution shift. Calibration is a property of a dataset, not of a model. A calibrator fitted on a balanced benchmark is wrong on imbalanced production traffic because the base rate changed. Re-fit whenever the deployment distribution moves.
    3. Isotonic overfitting. The demo's fit has 1000 breakpoints on 1000 rows; with 200 rows the same procedure memorises the calibration set and can increase test ECE. Use isotonic only with thousands of points, or smooth/pre-bin first.
    4. Temperature cannot fix shape. One scalar fixes a uniform over- or under-confidence. If the model is overconfident in the middle and underconfident in the tails, temperature makes one end worse; use beta or dirichlet.
    5. dirichlet changes predictions. The full matrix is not monotone per class, so the argmax can move — the demo's accuracy shifts 0.800 → 0.805. Penalise off-diagonals toward zero if you need the ranking preserved.
    6. ECE is not a loss. It depends on the number and placement of bins, it ignores the ordering within a bin, and it can be gamed by a constant predictor. Report log-loss or Brier score alongside it; optimise those, not ECE.
    7. Prior shift from resampling. Fitting a calibrator on a class-balanced subsample changes the base rate, so the calibrated probabilities are calibrated for that subsample. Correct for the known prior ratio afterwards, or fit on a representative sample.
    8. Softmax identifiability. In dirichlet, adding a constant to every row of W and to b leaves the output unchanged, so without regularisation the parameters are arbitrary and the optimiser wanders. The L2 penalty breaks the tie; do not remove it.
    9. Clipping at the extremes. Any variant that takes log p needs p clipped away from 0 and 1. With p = 0 exactly, the logit is −∞ and the fit silently drops the row (or produces nan); the EPS clip is load-bearing.
    10. Calibration is not fairness. Global calibration says nothing about subgroups; a model can be perfectly calibrated overall and badly miscalibrated for a minority group. Check the reliability diagram per group, not just pooled.

    Applications #

    • Clinical risk scores. A predicted 20% five-year risk has to mean 20% for the score to inform a decision, and the calibration curve is a standard part of any risk-model report.
    • Credit and fraud. Calibrated default probabilities are needed for expected-loss calculations; a ranking is not enough because the decision is an expectation.
    • Churn and propensity models. Marketing budgets are allocated by expected value, which multiplies probability by margin — so the probability scale matters, not just the order.
    • Model ensembling. Averaging probabilities from differently calibrated models is dominated by the most overconfident one; calibrate each before averaging.
    • Threshold selection. Choosing a cut-off from a cost matrix needs probabilities; with uncalibrated scores the threshold has to be tuned empirically for every deployment.
    • Neural network confidence. Temperature scaling is the standard post-hoc fix for overconfident classifiers, and the cheapest baseline any uncertainty method must beat.
    • Distillation and compression. A distilled model is trained on the teacher's softened probabilities, and temperature is the knob that decides how much of the dark knowledge survives.
