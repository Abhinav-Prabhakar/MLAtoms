    Resampling

    Intuition #

    A model's score on the data it was trained on is a lie. It has already seen those rows, so the number measures memory, not generalisation. Resampling is the machinery that manufactures the missing evidence: hold part of the data back, fit on the rest, score on the held-out part, and repeat with different parts.

    The variants are not different algorithms so much as different answers to what is allowed to leak between the fit and the score:

    • holdout — one cut. Cheapest, noisier than it looks: with n = 200 and a 30% test set, the standard error of an MSE estimate is large enough that two models differing by 10% can look identical.
    • kfold — cut into k pieces and score each piece once with the other k−1 used to fit. Every row is a test row exactly once, so no data is wasted and the estimate uses the whole sample.
    • stratified — keep the class ratio inside every fold. An unstratified 5-fold split of 190 negatives and 10 positives can put all ten positives in one fold (the demo does exactly this by keeping the rows in class order: [0, 0, 0, 0, 10]), which makes four folds unable to measure the minority class at all.
    • group — no patient, user, device or document may appear on both sides of the cut. Otherwise the model memorises the group and the fold score measures recall of an identity, not skill.
    • time_series_split — the training set is always in the past. A random split of a time series trains on the future and tests on the past, which is not a question anyone can ask in production.
    • nested_cv — the inner folds choose hyperparameters, the outer folds score the whole procedure. Without the nesting, the selection step sees the scoring data and the reported number is optimistic.
    • repeated_cv — repeat the whole k-fold partition with different shuffles and average. The point estimate barely moves; the variance of the estimate drops like 1/R, which is what lets you tell a real 1% difference from a lucky split.
    • leave_one_out — k = n. Nearly unbiased for the training-set size, but each fit differs from the final model by one point, so for unstable learners the estimate has high variance. For linear least squares it is free: one closed-form formula replaces n fits.

    The two that stop silent errors are group and time_series_split. The rest reduce variance; those two decide whether the number is measuring anything at all.

    Variants #

    Variant Assumption it protects Cost Estimate variance
    holdout nothing 1 fit high
    kfold rows are i.i.d. k fits moderate
    stratified rows are i.i.d., classes imbalanced k fits moderate, lower on rare classes
    group rows are i.i.d. within group k fits moderate, correct under dependence
    time_series_split train precedes test n_splits fits moderate, pessimistic
    nested_cv hyperparameters are chosen, not given k·(1 + c·k_inner) fits low, unbiased for the procedure
    repeated_cv the partition is random k·R fits ≈ σ²/(kR)
    leave_one_out rows are i.i.d. n fits (O(1) for OLS) low bias, high variance

    holdout and kfold are the same idea at two granularities: holdout is k = 2 with an unequal split. kfold with k = n is leave_one_out. The interesting cases are the conditioning ones — stratified, group, time_series_split — and nested_cv, which fixes a bias rather than a variance.

    One detail that catches people: nested_cv and repeated_cv reuse the machinery of kfold. In this file repeated_cv is literally n_repeats k-fold partitions with different seeds, and nested_cv is a k-fold outer loop plus an inner k-fold partitioner exposed as an attribute. That is not a shortcut; it is the definition.

    Derivation #

    The estimand. Let R(θ) = E_{(x,y)~P}[ℓ(y, f_θ(x))] be the risk under the data distribution, and let R̂_S(θ) be the average loss on a finite sample S. Every resampling scheme is an attempt to estimate R(θ) using only one sample D of size n.

    Holdout. Split D = T ∪ V with |V| = m. Then R̂_V(θ_T) is unbiased for the risk of a model trained on n − m points, and its variance is Var(ℓ)/m, estimated by the empirical variance of the test losses. Two problems are visible in that sentence: the training set is smaller than the final one (bias), and with m = 0.3n the variance is large. The bias is O(1/n) for a stable learner but can be substantial when the learning curve is steep.

    k-fold. Partition into K folds F_1…F_K of size ≈ n/K; for each j, fit on D \ F_j and score on F_j. Every row is scored exactly once, so the estimate uses all n scores:

    \[\hat R_{\text{cv}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell\bigl(y_i, f_{\theta_{D\setminus F(i)}}(x_i)\bigr)\]

    with F(i) the fold containing i. The bias is that of a model trained on n(1−1/K) points, which shrinks as K grows; the variance comes from two sources — the test losses themselves and the correlation between the K fitted models, which share n(1−2/K) points. That correlation is why k = 10 usually beats both k = 5 and k = n: it trades bias down and correlation up, and the optimum is around 5–10 for most learners.

    Stratification. Suppose the folds are formed by assigning each class's rows to folds independently. Then the number of minority rows in fold j is hypergeometric with mean n_rare/K and variance n_rare (n−n_rare)/(K−1) · …, so a fold can receive zero minority rows whenever n_rare < K. Stratifying makes that count constant (n_rare // K, plus one for the first n_rare % K folds), which removes the between-fold variance of the class ratio entirely. The demo's 190/10 split gives [2,2,2,2,2] stratified and [0,0,0,0,10] unstratified — the first four folds of the unstratified split cannot compute a minority-class recall at all.

    Groups. If rows are exchangeable only within a group g — repeated measurements of the same patient, multiple sessions of the same user — then a row from g in the test set is predictable from its siblings in the training set regardless of the target relationship. The fold score then estimates "can the model identify the group", which is usually near-perfect and always useless. Grouped splitting makes the folds a partition of groups, so the test rows come from groups the model has never seen. The demo measures the leak directly: k-fold shares 133 groups between the training and test sides, grouped splitting shares 0.

    Time series. For a series y_1…y_n, the random-fold assumption fails twice: the rows are dependent (so test losses are correlated and the variance formula is wrong), and a random split lets the model fit on y_{t+1} to predict y_t. Forward chaining fixes both: window i trains on [0, w(i+1)) and tests on the following block, so the training set only ever grows into the past. The gap parameter drops rows immediately before the test window — the forecast horizon, an embargo, or the label-delay window. Without it, the last training label may be computed from data that overlaps the test period, which is a leak that survives even forward chaining.

    Nested CV. Let c index a candidate set and S_j be the outer fold. The flat procedure is

    \[\hat R_{\text{flat}} = \min_c \frac{1}{K}\sum_j \ell_{j}(c)\]

    and min is a nonlinear function, so it does not commute with expectation:

    \[\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\min_c \hat R(c)\Bigr] \;\le\; \min_c \mathbb{E}\bigl[\hat R(c)\bigr]\]

    The left side is what flat CV reports; the right side is the risk of the best candidate. The gap is the selection bias, and it grows with the number of candidates and with the noise in each estimate. Nested CV reports instead

    \[\hat R_{\text{nested}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_j \ell_{j}\bigl(\hat c_{-j}\bigr), \qquad \hat c_{-j} = \arg\min_c \frac{1}{K_{\text{in}}}\sum_{i \in \text{inner}} \ell_i(c)\]

    where ĉ_{−j} is chosen without touching S_j. Each term is an honest score for the procedure that picks c from data, which is the thing that will run in production.

    The demo makes the bias visible with ten candidates that are all equally useless: ten disjoint pairs of pure-noise features, y independent of X. Every candidate has true risk exactly 1.0. Flat CV's best of ten reports 0.984; the nested estimate reports 1.089, and it selects 3 different subsets across the five outer folds — the selection is unstable, which is precisely when the flat minimum is most optimistic.

    Repeated CV. A single k-fold estimate is a random variable because the partition is random. If a repeat has variance σ², then averaging R independent repeats has variance σ²/R, so the standard error falls by √R:

    \[\mathrm{se}\bigl(\hat R^{(R)}\bigr) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{kR}}\]

    Two distinct 10-repeat estimates should therefore agree about three times more closely than two distinct 1-repeat estimates. The demo measures that directly: over 20 seeds the spread is 0.0039 for one repeat and 0.0011 for ten, a ratio of 0.29 ≈ 1/√10.

    Leave-one-out and the hat matrix. LOO is K = n: fit on all but row i, score row i. Naively that is n fits, but for linear least squares there is an identity. Let β̂ be the full fit, e_i = y_i − x_iᵀβ̂ the full residual, and h_i = x_iᵀ(XᵀX)⁻¹x_i the leverage (the i-th diagonal of the hat matrix H = X(XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ). Then

    \[\hat\beta_{-i} = \hat\beta - \frac{(X^\top X)^{-1}x_i\, e_i}{1-h_i}, \qquad \hat y_{-i}(x_i) = x_i^\top\hat\beta_{-i} \;\Longrightarrow\; e_i^{(-i)} = \frac{e_i}{1-h_i}\]

    The second identity is the useful one: the LOO residual is the full residual inflated by 1/(1−h_i). So

    \[\text{LOO-MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \Bigl(\frac{e_i}{1-h_i}\Bigr)^2\]

    with no refitting at all. The demo checks the implementation against it and gets 0.198063 from both routes to six decimals. The h_i → 1 case is a high-leverage point: the inflation factor blows up, and the LOO estimate becomes unstable, which is LOO's known weakness rather than a bug.

    Code walkthrough #

    One split generator dispatches on the variant, so every consumer — the demo, the two scoring harnesses, any caller — sees the same (train_idx, test_idx) contract:

            if v == "holdout":
            yield from [self._holdout(n, y, rng)]
        elif v == "kfold":
            yield from self._kfold(n, self.n_splits, rng)

    yield from [... ] with a one-element list is deliberate: split must always be a generator, otherwise callers that call next(iter(...)) and callers that loop would need different code paths. n comes from the first axis of X, or from X itself when it is an integer, so index-only callers (the time-series demo) do not have to fabricate an array.

    The k-fold split is a permutation plus a size vector:

            perm = rng.permutation(n) if self.shuffle else np.arange(n)
        sizes = np.full(k, n // k)
        sizes[: n % k] += 1
        start = 0
        for size in sizes:
            test = np.sort(perm[start:start + size])
            mask = np.ones(n, dtype=bool)
            mask[test] = False
            yield np.flatnonzero(mask), test
            start += size

    sizes[: n % k] += 1 is what makes folds differ by at most one row: with n = 203, k = 5 the sizes are [41, 41, 41, 40, 40]. The mask-and-flatnonzero construction gives sorted train indices without np.setdiff1d, which would sort on every fold and is much slower. np.sort(perm[...]) sorts the test indices too; callers index with them, so order does not matter, but reproducible output does.

    Stratification loops over classes and slices each one into k chunks:

            folds: list[list[int]] = [[] for _ in range(k)]
        for cls in np.unique(y):
            ids = np.flatnonzero(y == cls)
            ids = ids[rng.permutation(ids.size)]
            for j, chunk in enumerate(np.array_split(ids, k)):
                folds[j].extend(chunk.tolist())

    np.array_split splits into k nearly equal pieces, so a class with m rows puts ⌈m/k⌉ or ⌊m/k⌋ in each fold — the constant-proportion guarantee. Shuffling per class before the split is what makes it random rather than dependent on row order. Note the loop cannot produce an empty fold for a class with fewer than k rows; it produces folds with zero of that class, which is the unavoidable floor and exactly the case where stratification has nothing left to give.

    Grouped splitting is a greedy load-balance over whole groups:

            load = np.zeros(k, dtype=int)
        assign: dict[Any, int] = {}
        for g in uniq:                       # longest-processing-time: biggest first
            j = int(np.argmin(load))
            assign[g] = j
            load[j] += int((groups == g).sum())
        fold_of = np.array([assign[g] for g in groups])

    This is longest-processing-time scheduling: each group goes to the currently lightest fold, which keeps fold sizes within one group's size of each other. Since the folds are a partition of groups, no group can appear on both sides of a cut — that is the whole guarantee, and it holds regardless of how balanced the folds are. fold_of is a per-row array, so the yielded train/test masks are computed by equality rather than by set operations.

    Time-series windows are an expanding prefix and a following block:

            width = max(1, n // (self.n_splits + 1))
        for i in range(self.n_splits):
            train_end = width * (i + 1)
            test_start = train_end + self.gap
            test_end = min(test_start + width, n)
            if test_start >= n:
                break
            yield np.arange(train_end), np.arange(test_start, test_end)

    n // (n_splits + 1) reserves one window for the first training block plus one test window per split, so the last test block ends at the data. gap is added between train_end and test_start and is not part of either side — those rows are dropped, which is the embargo. np.arange(train_end) is a prefix, so the training set grows by width each iteration and the test block slides forward: exactly forward chaining.

    The stratified holdout is the same idea at one cut, and its max(1, ...) guard is subtle:

            for cls in np.unique(y):
            ids = np.flatnonzero(y == cls)
            take = max(1, int(round(self.test_size * ids.size)))
            test.extend(rng.permutation(ids)[:take].tolist())

    Without max(1, ...), a class with two rows and test_size = 0.25 contributes round(0.5) = 0 rows and disappears from the test set entirely. The guarantee that every class is represented costs one row of imbalance — a class with one row ends up in the test set regardless of test_size, which is why the demo's split has 60 test rows rather than 59.

    The scoring harness rebuilds the model for every fold:

        scores = []
    for train, test in resampling.split(X, y):
        model = make_model()
        model.fit(X[train], y[train])
        scores.append(scoring(y[test], model.predict(X[test])))
    return np.asarray(scores, dtype=float)

    make_model() must be a factory, not an instance: reusing a fitted estimator across folds keeps the state from the previous fit, so fold 2 would start from fold 1's coefficients. This is the most common bug in hand-rolled CV, and it is silent — the scores still look plausible.

    Nested CV is the outer loop plus an inner selection, and the inner partition is taken from the outer training rows:

                for itr, ite in resampling.inner.split(Xtr, ytr):
                model = make_model(**params)
                model.fit(Xtr[itr], ytr[itr])
                inner_scores.append(scoring(ytr[ite], model.predict(Xtr[ite])))
            mean = float(np.mean(inner_scores))
            if best is None or mean < best[0]:
                best = (mean, dict(params))

    Xtr[itr] — not X[itr] — is the load-bearing line. Splitting the inner fold over the full dataset would put outer-test rows into the inner training set, and the selection would see the data it is later scored on. That single indexing mistake reintroduces the exact bias nesting exists to remove. dict(params) copies the candidate so a later mutation cannot change the record of what was chosen.

    The LOO branch is two lines, and its cost profile is the reason the closed form matters:

            else:                                   # leave_one_out
            for i in range(n):
                yield np.delete(np.arange(n), i), np.array([i])

    np.delete allocates a new array of length n−1 per iteration, so the generator alone is O(n²) memory traffic before any fitting. For n = 10⁴ that is 800 MB of copying; for linear models use the hat-matrix formula instead, and for anything else use k = 10 and accept the bias.

    Complexity #

    Variant Fits Total cost Memory
    holdout 1 T(n(1−p)) O(n)
    kfold k k·T(n(1−1/k)) O(n)
    stratified k k·T(n(1−1/k)) + O(n log n) sort O(n)
    group k k·T(·) + O(G log G) for the group sort O(n + G)
    time_series_split s Σ_i T(w(i+1)) — cheapest early, dearest late O(n)
    nested_cv k(1 + c·k_in) k·T(·) + k·c·k_in·T(·) O(n)
    repeated_cv kR R× k-fold O(n)
    leave_one_out n n·T(n−1); O(nd²) for OLS via H O(n²) naive, O(n) closed form

    T(m) is the cost of one fit on m rows. Nested CV is the expensive one: with k = 5, k_in = 4 and 10 candidates it is 5 + 200 = 205 fits, which is why people nest only the few hyperparameters that matter. Repeated CV is embarrassingly parallel across repeats. The LOO row is the interesting one: for OLS the closed form makes n apparent fits cost one d×d inverse, so the "expensive" variant becomes the cheapest per unit of information.

    Failure modes #

    1. Leakage through preprocessing. Scaling, imputing, feature selection or target encoding fitted on the full dataset before splitting leaks test information into training. Put every transformation inside the fold: fit on train, apply to test. This is the single most common way a CV score is spectacularly wrong.
    2. Grouped data split randomly. The demo's k-fold shares 133 of 40 groups across cuts — every fold has seen siblings of its test rows. Scores look excellent and deployment collapses. Any column that identifies a repeated unit (patient, session, device) belongs in groups.
    3. Time leakage. Random splits, shuffle=True on ordered data, or features computed over the full series (a rolling mean that includes future rows) all train on the future. Use time_series_split with a gap at least as large as the label horizon.
    4. Tuning and scoring on the same folds. Flat CV's min over candidates is biased low by construction; the demo's best-of-ten reports 0.984 when the truth is 1.000. The bias is largest exactly when the candidates are near-tied and the estimates are noisy — the situation where choosing matters most.
    5. k too small or too large. k = 2 wastes half the data per fit and has high variance; k = n makes the fits almost identical to each other (high correlation) and for unstable learners has higher variance than k = 10. Five or ten is the default for a reason.
    6. A single split reported as a result. holdout with test_size = 0.2 has a standard error several times larger than 10-fold on the same data. Report the spread across folds, not just the mean; a bare mean invites over-reading.
    7. Class imbalance without stratification. As above: folds with zero minority rows make per-class metrics undefined or nan, and the mean over folds silently ignores them.
    8. LOO with high leverage. 1/(1−h_i) explodes for a point that nearly determines its own fit, so the LOO estimate is dominated by a few rows. If max(h_i) is close to 1, prefer k-fold.
    9. Reusing a fitted model across folds. Passing an instance instead of a factory keeps warm-started state and quietly reports the score of a model trained on the union of folds. make_model() every fold.
    10. n_splits larger than the data. k > n produces empty test folds and division by zero in the mean. The constructor rejects k < 2; it cannot know n, so guard at the call site.

    Applications #

    • Model selection. Compare candidate models on the same folds and report mean ± standard error across folds; with repeated_cv the error bar is small enough to resolve 1% differences.
    • Hyperparameter search. nested_cv is the only honest way to report the score of a tuned model; flat CV is for ranking candidates, not for publishing a number.
    • Clinical and user-level prediction. Grouped splitting is mandatory whenever a patient or user contributes multiple rows, and it is the difference between a paper that replicates and one that does not.
    • Forecasting. Forward chaining is the standard backtest for time-series models, and the gap encodes the forecast horizon.
    • Rare-event detection. Stratified folds keep fraud, failure and disease rates measurable in every fold, which is what per-class metrics need.
    • Cheap model diagnostics. LOO via the hat matrix gives a per-row error estimate in one fit, useful for spotting influential points and for comparing linear models on small data.
    • Baseline establishment. A plain 5-fold CV with a fixed seed is the minimum evidence any new model should beat before it is worth deploying.
