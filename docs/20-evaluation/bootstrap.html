    Bootstrap

    Intuition #

    The bootstrap answers the only question that matters after you compute a number: how much would it move if I had drawn a different sample? You cannot draw a new sample, so you redraw the one you have — with replacement — and watch the statistic move.

    The mechanics are almost embarrassingly simple. Resample n rows with replacement, recompute the statistic, repeat B times, and look at the spread:

    observed data  x₁ x₂ … xₙ                     statistic  θ̂ = 1.005
                     │
     ┌───────────────┼───────────────┐         B resamples of size n,
     ▼               ▼               ▼         each row drawn with replacement
  x₃ x₃ x₇ … x₁   x₂ x₅ x₅ … x₉   x₁ x₁ x₄ … x₂
     │               │               │
     ▼               ▼               ▼
    θ*₁             θ*₂             θ*_B          → percentile interval

    Why this works: the empirical distribution F̂ puts mass 1/n on each observed point, and sampling from F̂ mimics sampling from the true F. That substitution — F → F̂ — is the bootstrap principle, and it is exact for statistics that are smooth functions of sample moments. The demo's percentile interval for a mean is [0.863, 1.152] while the textbook CLT interval is 0.282 wide against the bootstrap's 0.288: they agree to 2%, which is what theory predicts for n = 200.

    The variants exist because the substitution is not always the same substitution:

    • percentile reads quantiles off the bootstrap distribution. Simple, transformation respecting, first-order accurate.
    • bca adjusts which quantiles it reads, correcting for median bias and for skewness. Second-order accurate; it needs a jackknife pass.
    • paired changes what a row is: two measurements of the same unit are resampled together, so the interval is on their difference, not on two independent estimates.
    • block_bootstrap changes what a row is the other way: for dependent data, rows are not independent draws, so blocks of consecutive rows are. The demo's AR(1) series with ρ = 0.85 gets an interval 3.5× wider than the iid bootstrap's — exactly the √((1+ρ)/(1−ρ)) = 3.5 that the autocorrelation implies.
    • bayesian_bootstrap replaces the multinomial draw with Dirichlet weights. It is the posterior of a nonparametric model rather than a resampling scheme, and it never assigns zero weight to an observed point.

    Variants #

    Variant Resamples Interval Assumption it fixes
    percentile rows iid quantiles α/2, 1−α/2 nothing beyond iid rows
    bca rows iid adjusted quantiles from z₀, a skewness and median bias
    paired row pairs jointly quantiles of the difference two vectors measured on the same units
    block_bootstrap contiguous blocks quantiles serial dependence
    bayesian_bootstrap Dirichlet weights quantiles of the weighted statistic no resampling, no zero mass

    percentile is the default and the one to reach for first. bca costs one extra pass of n statistic evaluations and is worth it when the statistic is skewed — a variance, a ratio, a quantile, or the mean of an exponential. In the demo, BCa moves the lower bound up by 0.010 on the same data, and over 200 replications its coverage is 0.950 against the percentile's 0.940: at n = 100 both are close to nominal, and the gap widens as n falls.

    paired is the variant people forget. If you evaluate two models on the same test set, their errors are correlated — a hard row is hard for both — and treating the two error vectors as independent samples throws that correlation away. The demo makes the point numerically: the same comparison gives a width of 0.097 when paired and 0.487 when resampled independently, a factor of five. Pairing is not a refinement; it is the difference between detecting a 0.054 MSE gap and not.

    block_bootstrap is the only correct choice for ordered data. The iid bootstrap destroys the dependence structure: it draws x_t and x_s independently even though corr(x_t, x_s) = ρ^|t−s|. The block version keeps runs of block_size rows intact, and circular wrapping ((s + j) % n) means every row has the same chance of being inside a block. The cost is a bias-variance trade in the block length: too short and dependence survives, too long and there are too few distinct blocks.

    bayesian_bootstrap uses w ~ Dirichlet(1, …, 1) and reports the weighted statistic. It is a genuinely different justification — the Dirichlet is the posterior over the probability vector of a nonparametric categorical model — but the result is numerically close to the percentile interval (0.286 against 0.288 width here). Its practical advantage is smoothness: no row ever gets exactly zero weight, so a statistic that is sensitive to duplicates or to ties behaves better.

    Derivation #

    The plug-in principle. Let θ = T(F) be the functional you care about — the mean is T(F) = ∫x dF(x). You observe F̂, the empirical distribution, so you estimate θ̂ = T(F̂). The bootstrap asks: what is the distribution of T(F̂*) − T(F̂) when F̂* is drawn from F̂? The claim of the bootstrap is that this mimics the distribution of T(F̂) − T(F) when F̂ is drawn from F. For the mean, both are asymptotically N(0, σ²/n), which is why the percentile interval reproduces the CLT interval to O(n^{-1/2}).

    Percentile interval. With θ*_1…θ*_B the bootstrap replicates,

    \[CI_{\text{pct}} = \bigl[\hat q_{\alpha/2},\; \hat q_{1-\alpha/2}\bigr], \qquad \hat q_p = \text{empirical } p\text{-quantile of } \{\theta^*_b\}\]

    It is transformation respecting: if you bootstrap log θ and exponentiate the interval, you get the same answer as bootstrapping θ directly. That property is why it is the default for ratios and variances, where a symmetric interval would be nonsense.

    Why percentile can be wrong, and what BCa does. Write the bootstrap distribution's quantiles in terms of a monotone transformation g that makes the statistic normal:

    \[g(\hat\theta) - g(\theta) \sim N(-z_0,\; 1), \qquad \text{Var}\bigl(g(\hat\theta)\bigr) \text{ depends on } \theta \text{ through } a\]

    z_0 is the median bias — where θ̂ sits in its own bootstrap distribution — and a is the acceleration, the skewness of the statistic as seen by the jackknife. Both are estimable without knowing g:

    \[\hat z_0 = \Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\#\{\theta^*_b < \hat\theta\} + \tfrac12\#\{= \hat\theta\}}{B}\right), \qquad \hat a = \frac{\sum_i (\bar\theta_{(\cdot)} - \theta_{(i)})^3} {6\Bigl[\sum_i (\bar\theta_{(\cdot)} - \theta_{(i)})^2\Bigr]^{3/2}}\]

    where θ_(i) is the statistic with row i deleted (the jackknife) and θ̄_(·) its mean. The adjusted quantiles are

    \[\alpha' = \Phi\!\left(z_0 + \frac{z_0 + z_\alpha}{1 - a(z_0 + z_\alpha)}\right), \qquad CI_{\text{BCa}} = \bigl[\hat q_{\alpha'},\; \hat q_{1-\alpha''}\bigr]\]

    with the same formula at 1 − α. The correction is a monotone remapping of the quantile levels: it shifts the interval away from the side where the bootstrap distribution is compressed, which is exactly the side where the percentile interval undercovers. Its coverage error is O(n^{-1}) against the percentile's O(n^{-1/2}) — one order better, which is why the demo's two numbers separate as n shrinks.

    The sign of z₀ matters and is easy to get wrong: use strict < with a half-count for ties, not ≤. Counting ties as "above" biases z₀ downward by half the tie mass, which on a discrete statistic can move the interval by more than the correction it is meant to apply.

    Paired resampling. For two vectors measured on the same n units, the statistic is a function of the pairs (a_i, b_i). Resampling a common index set preserves the pairing:

    \[\theta^* = T\bigl(\{(a_{i_1}, b_{i_1}), …, (a_{i_n}, b_{i_n})\}\bigr), \qquad i_k \sim U\{1..n\}\]

    The variance of a difference is Var(a − b) = Var(a) + Var(b) − 2Cov(a,b), so pairing removes the covariance term. When the two measurements share most of their noise — the same rows, the same difficulty — that term dominates and the paired interval is far narrower. When they are genuinely independent samples, Cov ≈ 0 and pairing changes nothing; it never hurts, which is why "resample the test rows, not the two error vectors" is the right default for model comparison.

    Block bootstrap. For a stationary series, dependence means the effective sample size is not n. For AR(1) with autocorrelation ρ,

    \[\text{Var}(\bar x) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\cdot\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}\]

    so the honest standard error is √((1+ρ)/(1−ρ)) times the iid one — 3.5× at ρ = 0.85, which is precisely the ratio the demo measures. The block bootstrap reproduces that factor by keeping b consecutive rows together: resampling ⌈n/b⌉ blocks with uniformly random circular starts and truncating to n preserves the within-block autocovariance. The usual choice is b ≈ n^{1/3}, which balances the bias from dependence left inside a block against the variance from having only n/b effectively independent blocks.

    Bayesian bootstrap. Model the data as x_i ~ F, F ~ DP(α, F₀); the posterior over the probability vector at the observed points is Dirichlet(1, …, 1) when α → 0 (Rubin's choice). A draw is a weight vector w, and the induced estimate is Σ w_i x_i. Its variance, computed from the Dirichlet's covariance, is

    \[\text{Var}\Bigl(\sum_i w_i x_i\Bigr) = \frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2 \,\bigl(1 + O(1/n)\bigr)\]

    which is the bootstrap variance to first order. The two intervals therefore agree asymptotically — the demo's 0.286 versus 0.288 — while the Bayesian one has a cleaner justification and no resampling artefacts.

    Does the interval earn its nominal coverage? The demo runs 200 independent samples of size 100 from Exp(1), builds a 95% interval for the mean each time, and counts how often it contains 1.0: 0.940 for percentile and 0.950 for BCa. At n = 50 the same experiment gives 0.915 for percentile — the undercoverage the acceleration term is designed to fix. Coverage is the property that distinguishes a confidence interval from a plausible-looking range, and it is cheap to check by simulation for any statistic you actually report.

    Code walkthrough #

    Every variant produces an index array, so a single loop can evaluate them all:

        def _indices(self, rng: np.random.Generator) -> np.ndarray:
        n = self.n_
        if self.variant == "block_bootstrap":
            b = min(self.block_size, n)
            n_blocks = -(-n // b)
            starts = rng.integers(0, n, size=n_blocks)
            return np.concatenate([(s + np.arange(b)) % n for s in starts])[:n]
        return rng.integers(0, n, size=n)

    -(-n // b) is ceiling division without math.ceil: block count times block size must cover n, and the final [:n] truncates the overshoot. (s + np.arange(b)) % n is the circular block: a block starting near the end wraps to the beginning, so the first rows are not systematically under-sampled, which the linear version would do. rng.integers(0, n, size=n) is the iid bootstrap — note it draws n indices with replacement from range(n), not a permutation, which is the whole difference between the bootstrap and cross-validation.

    Paired data is indexed uniformly because a row is a tuple of observations:

        def _take(self, idx: np.ndarray) -> float:
        if self.variant == "paired":
            return float(self._statistic(tuple(a[idx] for a in self._arrays)))
        return float(self._statistic(self._data[idx]))

    tuple(a[idx] for a in self._arrays) applies the same index array to every vector, so a resample can never pair y_true[3] with pred_a[7]. That single line is what makes the paired interval 0.097 wide instead of 0.487: the covariance between the two error vectors survives the resampling.

    BCa needs two extra statistics, both cheap:

            below = np.sum(dist < self.theta_)
        ties = np.sum(dist == self.theta_)
        z0 = _norm_ppf((below + 0.5 * ties) / dist.size)
        jack = self._jackknife()
        dev = jack.mean() - jack
        scale = 6.0 * float(np.sum(dev ** 2)) ** 1.5
        accel = float(np.sum(dev ** 3)) / max(scale, EPS)

    below + 0.5 * ties is the mid-rank position of the observed statistic inside its own bootstrap distribution; z0 > 0 means the replicates sit mostly below θ̂, i.e. the statistic is biased upward. dev is the jackknife's deviation from its own mean, and accel is its standardised third moment — the skewness of the statistic under leave-one-out. max(scale, EPS) guards the constant statistic, where dev is all zeros and 0/0 would make the interval nan.

    The quantile remapping is three lines, and the guard is not cosmetic:

            for j, p in enumerate((alpha, 1.0 - alpha)):
            z = _norm_ppf(p)
            denom = 1.0 - accel * (z0 + z)
            out[j] = 0.5 if abs(denom) < EPS else _norm_cdf(z0 + (z0 + z) / denom)

    1 − a(z₀ + z) can cross zero when the acceleration is large (a very skewed statistic), and the remapped level then flips past the middle of the distribution. Falling back to 0.5 keeps the interval finite and, at that point, as uninformative as the data warrants. _norm_ppf is Acklam's rational approximation with a Newton step, so the file needs no SciPy; the demo's BCa interval matches scipy.stats.bootstrap(method="BCa") to six decimals.

    The main loop is one branch for the Bayesian variant and one for everything else:

            for b in range(self.n_resamples):
            if self.variant == "bayesian_bootstrap":
                dist[b] = float(statistic(self._data, rng.dirichlet(np.ones(self.n_))))
            else:
                dist[b] = self._take(self._indices(rng))

    rng.dirichlet(np.ones(n)) is the posterior draw and the statistic receives the weights directly, so the Bayesian variant never materialises a resample — that is why its interface takes statistic(data, weights) while the others take statistic(resample). Keeping the same dist array for both means the quantile code and se_ are shared.

    The observed statistic is computed once in _setup, and the Bayesian case needs a different call:

            if self.variant == "bayesian_bootstrap":
            self.theta_ = float(statistic(self._data, np.full(n, 1.0 / n)))
        else:
            self.theta_ = float(statistic(self._data))

    np.full(n, 1/n) is the uniform weight vector, which is the Bayesian bootstrap's point estimate. Without this branch z₀ would be computed against a statistic that is nan or an error, and every BCa-style correction downstream would silently degenerate.

    The interval itself is a quantile read, which is the entire difference between the variants:

            if self.variant == "bca":
            self.ci_ = self._bca_quantiles()
        else:
            alpha = (1.0 - self.level) / 2.0
            self.ci_ = np.quantile(dist, [alpha, 1.0 - alpha])

    np.quantile with [α/2, 1−α/2] is the percentile interval; the BCa branch passes adjusted levels into the same call. self.width_ is stored because the demo compares widths constantly and recomputing ci_[1] - ci_[0] at every call site invites a sign error.

    Complexity #

    Step Time Memory
    One replicate O(n + T) for resampling plus statistic cost T O(n)
    percentile / paired B·(n + T) O(B)
    block_bootstrap B·(n + T) + O(n log n) block assembly O(B)
    bayesian_bootstrap B·(n + T) for the Dirichlet draw O(B + n)
    bca (B + n)·(n + T) — the jackknife adds n fits O(B + n)
    Indexing (paired) O(n·m) for m paired vectors O(n·m)

    B = 2000 is the usual default: the Monte Carlo error of a quantile estimate falls as B^{-1/2}, so 2000 replicates give interval endpoints stable to about 2% of their width, which is finer than the statistical error. BCa costs one extra pass of n statistic evaluations — negligible for a mean, decisive for a model refit, where each T is minutes. Memory is O(B) for the distribution, which is why the class stores it: plotting the bootstrap distribution is often more informative than the interval.

    Failure modes #

    1. Non-smooth statistics. The bootstrap works for smooth functionals. The maximum, the mode, the number of distinct values and the argmax of a likelihood are not smooth: the resampled statistic can be identical across many replicates, the distribution is lumpy, and the percentile interval is wrong. Use a smoothed bootstrap or a different tool.
    2. Heavy tails. For a distribution with infinite variance (Pareto with α < 2), the bootstrap distribution of the mean has no stable spread and the interval is meaningless. Check the tail before trusting the number; no resampling scheme invents moments that do not exist.
    3. Dependent rows. The iid bootstrap on autocorrelated or clustered data is overconfident by √((1+ρ)/(1−ρ)) — the demo's 3.5×. Blocks or a cluster bootstrap are the fix, and the block length is a real tuning decision.
    4. B too small. With B = 200 the 2.5% quantile is estimated from the 5th smallest replicate; it moves by percent-level amounts between seeds. Use B ≥ 1000 for intervals, more for tail quantiles.
    5. Bootstrapping the wrong thing. Resampling the two error vectors independently when the models were evaluated on the same rows throws away the pairing and can turn a significant difference into "no effect". The demo's 5× width ratio is the size of the mistake.
    6. Percentile interval on a biased estimator. The percentile interval is centred on θ̂, so if θ̂ is biased the interval is too — it bounds the wrong quantity confidently. The bootstrap can estimate the bias (θ̄* − θ̂) and correct it, but that is a separate step, not something the interval does.
    7. Small n. Undercoverage grows as n falls: 0.915 at n = 50 for a skewed statistic. BCa helps but does not eliminate it. Below n ≈ 30, prefer exact or likelihood-based intervals where they exist.
    8. Statistic near a boundary. A variance estimated as near-zero, or a proportion near 0 or 1, gives a bootstrap distribution piled against the boundary and a one-sided interval. Transform first (log, logit) and back-transform the endpoints.
    9. Silent dependence between the statistic and n. Bootstrapping a statistic that depends on the sample size (an AIC, a penalised likelihood) resamples at the wrong size for part of its definition. Compute the statistic on the resample, not a precomputed table of per-row values, unless you have verified the two agree.
    10. Weights ignored. For the Bayesian variant the statistic must actually use the weights. Passing np.mean there throws them away and silently reports the point estimate B times, giving a zero-width interval.

    Applications #

    • Confidence intervals for any metric. Accuracy, F1, AUC, median error, the 95th percentile latency — no distributional assumption, one implementation. This is the default way to put error bars on a reported number.
    • Model comparison on a test set. Paired bootstrap over the test rows gives an interval on the difference in error, which is the quantity a decision actually depends on.
    • A/B tests with awkward metrics. Revenue per user, session length, conversion — heavily skewed and zero-inflated, where a t-test's assumptions fail and the bootstrap merely needs a resample.
    • Time-series and sequential data. Block or stationary bootstrap for forecast error, autocorrelated residuals, and any metric computed over an ordered stream.
    • Small-sample inference. Where asymptotic intervals are unreliable and the data are too few for a nonparametric test, the bootstrap often still works.
    • Estimator bias and stability. θ̄* − θ̂ estimates the bias, and the spread of the replicates says how much the estimate depends on which rows were sampled.
    • Simulation calibration. Bootstrap a metric on synthetic data with a known truth to check that your reported interval covers it — the demo's 200-replication check, applied to whatever you are about to ship.

    References #

    • Efron, Bootstrap Methods: Another Look at the Jackknife, 1979, Annals of Statistics — the bootstrap, and the jackknife connection that BCa's acceleration term still uses.
    • Efron & Tibshirani, An Introduction to the Bootstrap, 1993 — the standard reference for the percentile and BCa intervals and for the transformation-respecting argument.
    • DiCiccio & Efron, Bootstrap Confidence Intervals, 1996, Statistical Science — the taxonomy of intervals and the O(n^{-1}) accuracy of BCa.
    • Davison & Hinkley, Bootstrap Methods and Their Application, 1997 — worked derivations, including the paired and block schemes.
    • Rubin, The Bayesian Bootstrap, 1981, Annals of Statistics — Dirichlet weights and the posterior interpretation.
    • Künsch, The Jackknife and the Bootstrap for General Stationary Observations, 1989, Annals of Statistics — the block bootstrap and its consistency for stationary series.
    • Politis & Romano, The Stationary Bootstrap, 1994, JASA — random block lengths and guidance on choosing the block size.
    • Hall, Theoretical Comparison of Bootstrap Confidence Intervals, 1988, Annals of Statistics — why BCa is second-order accurate and the percentile interval is not.
