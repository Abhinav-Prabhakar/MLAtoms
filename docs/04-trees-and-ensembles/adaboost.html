    04 · Trees & ensembles/tier 1/6 variants/source

    AdaBoost

    Intuition #

    Boosting answers a simple question: what should the next weak learner look at? The answer is whatever the current ensemble gets wrong. AdaBoost turns that into a reweighting scheme — after each learner, upweight the rows it misclassified, fit the next learner on the new weights, and combine everything in a weighted vote. No gradient is computed explicitly; the weights are the gradient.

    The surprising part is that reweighting is equivalent to gradient descent on the exponential loss exp(−yF(x)). That is why AdaBoost is the ancestor of every gradient-boosted tree: gradient_boosting.py is the same loop with the loss written down and the tree fitted to its negative gradient.

    The six variants are the historical answers to "what does a weak learner return?". discrete returns a hard ±1 label. real returns the log-odds of its leaf, which is a confidence and converges faster. gentle returns the weighted regression fit directly, with no log and no α, which is the most numerically stable. samme and samme_r are the multiclass extensions, and stagewise makes the gradient-descent view explicit by line-searching the step.

    Variants #

    Variant Task Weak learner output Step Weight update
    discrete binary hard ±1 α = ½ln((1−err)/err) ∝ exp(−yαh)
    real binary ½ln(p/(1−p)) per leaf none (α=1) ∝ exp(−yh)
    gentle binary 2p−1 per leaf none (α=1) ∝ exp(−yh)
    samme K classes hard class vote ln((1−err)/err) + ln(K−1) ∝ exp(α·1[wrong])
    samme_r K classes (K−1)(log p_k − mean log p) none ∝ p_true^{−(K−1)/K}
    stagewise binary 2p−1, line-searched γ γ from Newton ∝ exp(−yγh)

    discrete is the original Freund–Schapire algorithm and the one in every textbook. real and gentle are Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani's confidence-rated refinements: the leaf probability is more informative than the leaf label, and using it removes the need for a separate α. samme generalises the α to K classes by adding ln(K−1); without that term a weak learner at chance level (err = 1−1/K) would get a positive weight. samme_r replaces the hard vote with per-class log-probabilities, which is the multiclass analogue of real.

    stagewise is the odd one out: it is the same exponential loss, but the step is chosen by a line search over γ instead of the closed-form α, and learning_rate multiplies it. It is the bridge to gradient_boosting.py — replace the exponential loss with any differentiable loss, fit the tree to the negative gradient, and the loop is identical.

    Derivation #

    Exponential loss and the additive model. With y ∈ {−1, +1} and F(x) = Σ_t α_t h_t(x), AdaBoost minimises

    \[L(F) = \sum_i \exp\!\big(-y_i F(x_i)\big)\]

    One stage at a time, holding the previous F fixed:

    \[(\alpha, h) = \arg\min_{\alpha,h} \sum_i w_i \exp(-\alpha y_i h(x_i)), \qquad w_i = \exp(-y_i F_{m-1}(x_i))\]

    Discrete case. For a hard h ∈ {−1,+1}, split the sum by whether h is right:

    \[\sum_i w_i e^{-\alpha y_i h_i} = e^{-\alpha} W_{\text{correct}} + e^{\alpha} W_{\text{wrong}} = e^{-\alpha}(W - W_{\text{err}}) + e^{\alpha} W_{\text{err}}\]

    with W = Σw and W_err = Σ w·1[h wrong]. Setting the derivative to zero gives

    \[\alpha^\* = \frac12 \ln\frac{W - W_{\text{err}}}{W_{\text{err}}} = \frac12\ln\frac{1-\text{err}}{\text{err}}\]

    This is why err ≥ 0.5 is a stop condition: α ≤ 0 means the learner is worse than chance, and flipping its sign would be a different learner. The best hard h at each stage is the one minimising W_err, which is exactly what a weighted classification tree finds — the weights enter the impurity, not the split search.

    The normaliser. After the update, the total weight is

    \[Z_t = (1-\text{err}_t)e^{-\alpha_t} + \text{err}_t e^{\alpha_t} = 2\sqrt{\text{err}_t(1-\text{err}_t)}\]

    so the normalised weights are w_i ← w_i e^{−α y h}/Z_t, and the exponential loss of the final model is exactly

    \[\frac1n\sum_i e^{-y_i F(x_i)} = \prod_{t=1}^T Z_t\]

    The demo checks this identity to nine digits (0.475614 == 0.475614), which is a much stronger test than checking that the loss decreased. Since Z_t ≤ 1, the training error obeys

    \[\frac1n\#\{y_i \ne \mathrm{sign}F(x_i)\} \le \prod_t Z_t = \exp\!\Big(-2\sum_t \gamma_t^2\Big), \qquad \gamma_t = \tfrac12 - \text{err}_t\]

    so a weak learner with a constant edge γ drives the training error to zero exponentially fast — the margin explanation of why AdaBoost does not overfit as quickly as its training error suggests.

    Real AdaBoost. Instead of a hard label, let the leaf return h_j = ½ln(W_+^j/W_-^j) for leaf j, where W_±^j are the weighted class masses in that leaf. This is the minimiser of the same objective when h is allowed to be any real number within a leaf, and it is the log-odds of the leaf's class probability. The α disappears because the log-odds already carries the confidence: a pure leaf gets a large step, a 50/50 leaf gets none. Friedman's analysis shows the stagewise fit converges faster than discrete and produces better-calibrated scores, which the demo reflects in the lower exponential loss (0.292 vs 0.476 at the same 80 stumps, with test accuracies 0.927 vs 0.942 — the hard vote is not worse, just less confident).

    Gentle AdaBoost. Replace the log-odds with the weighted least-squares fit f_j = Σ_{i∈j} w_i y_i / Σ_{i∈j} w_i, and add it directly. This is a Newton step for the exponential loss: the second derivative of e^{−yF} is e^{−yF}, so the local quadratic has the same weight in numerator and denominator, and the minimiser is the weighted mean of y. No log, no division by err — hence "gentle": it cannot blow up when a leaf is nearly pure, and it is the recommended default in Friedman's comparison. The demo's depth-3 comparison (0.372 vs 0.632 at stage 6) shows the other axis: stronger weak learners converge in fewer stages, at the cost of variance.

    SAMME. For K classes, the weak learner returns a hard class h(x) ∈ {1,…,K} and the model is F_k(x) = Σ α_t 1[h_t(x)=k], predicted by argmax_k F_k. The stage objective becomes

    \[\sum_i w_i \exp\!\big(-\alpha\, \mathbb{1}[y_i = h(x_i)]\big)\]

    because the multiclass exponential loss is exp(−α) for a correct vote and 1 for every wrong class. Minimising gives

    \[\alpha^\* = \ln\frac{1-\text{err}}{\text{err}} + \ln(K-1)\]

    The extra ln(K−1) is exactly the correction that makes random guessing (err = 1−1/K) give α = 0. That is why SAMME's stop condition is err ≥ 1−1/K, not err ≥ 1/2.

    SAMME.R. Use the leaf's class probabilities p_k(x) and set

    \[h_k(x) = (K-1)\left(\log p_k(x) - \frac1K\sum_j \log p_j(x)\right)\]

    so the K outputs sum to zero and only their differences matter. The weight update is

    \[w_i \leftarrow w_i \, p_{y_i}(x_i)^{-(K-1)/K}\]

    which is the multiclass counterpart of exp(−α y h): a row whose true class has low probability gets a large weight, and the exponent (K−1)/K is the factor that makes the update consistent with the K-class exponential loss. samme_r needs far fewer stages than samme for the same accuracy, which the demo shows as 0.983 vs 0.975 with 40 vs 60 trees.

    Stagewise. Treat F as a vector and take a gradient step in function space: F ← F + γh where h is fitted to −∂L/∂F = y·exp(−yF) under the current weights. Choosing γ by minimising Σ w_i exp(−γ y_i h_i) is a one-dimensional Newton problem:

    \[\gamma \leftarrow \gamma + \frac{\sum_i w_i s_i e^{-\gamma s_i}}{\sum_i w_i s_i^2 e^{-\gamma s_i}}, \qquad s_i = y_i h_i\]

    which is what _line_search does. With γ fixed to 1 and h a hard label, this reduces to discrete; with h a weighted mean, to gentle. learning_rate < 1 shrinks the step, which is the same shrinkage knob as in gradient boosting — the demo's lr=0.3 run takes smaller steps (0.369 on average) and reaches 0.943 accuracy in 150 stages, while lr=1.0 gets 0.932 in 25.

    Code walkthrough #

    The weak learner is a weighted tree, and one line in the split search is the whole difference from an unweighted tree:

        counts = np.zeros((n, k))
    counts[np.arange(n), y] = w
    parent = 1.0 - float(((counts.sum(axis=0) / wsum) ** 2).sum())

    counts holds weighted class mass rather than row counts. The parent impurity is the gini of the weighted distribution, so a row with weight 0.1 contributes a tenth as much as a row with weight 1. Everything downstream — the cumulative sums, the child impurities, the gain — is the same arithmetic as CART, which is why the tree code is short.

    min_samples_leaf is the exception, and it is the subtle one:

            wl = lc.sum(axis=1)
        wr = rc.sum(axis=1)
        # min_samples_leaf counts rows, not weights.
        n_l = np.cumsum(np.ones(n))[cut]
        n_r = n - n_l
        ok = (n_l >= min_samples_leaf) & (n_r >= min_samples_leaf)

    Weight sums shrink as boosting proceeds (they are normalised to 1), so a leaf limit applied to wl would reject every split after a few stages and the ensemble would stop learning. Row counts are tracked separately and never renormalised. Getting this wrong is the classic way to write an AdaBoost that looks correct and trains one tree.

    Leaves store weighted class frequencies, which is what every variant reads:

    def _leaf(y: np.ndarray, w: np.ndarray, k: int) -> dict:
    proba = np.bincount(y, weights=w, minlength=k).astype(float)
    total = proba.sum()
    return {
        "leaf": True,
        "n": y.size,
        "proba": proba / total if total > 0 else np.full(k, 1.0 / k),
    }

    np.bincount(..., weights=w) is the weighted histogram; normalising by total makes proba a distribution even when the incoming weights do not sum to 1. discrete uses proba[:, 1] > 0.5, real uses its log-odds, gentle and stagewise use 2p−1, and both multiclass variants use the whole vector. One leaf representation serves six algorithms.

    The discrete branch is the textbook formula, and its two guards are not optional:

                if self.variant == "discrete":
                pred = (p[:, 1] > 0.5).astype(float)
                err = float((w * (pred != yi)).sum())
                err = float(np.clip(err, 1e-10, 1.0 - 1e-10))
                if err >= 0.5:
                    break
                alpha = 0.5 * np.log((1.0 - err) / err)
                h = 2.0 * pred - 1.0
                F += alpha * h
                self.alphas_.append(float(alpha))

    err is a weighted error, not a mean of booleans. The clip prevents log(0) on perfectly separable data, and the err >= 0.5 break stops the ensemble instead of adding a negatively weighted learner. Break happens before the tree is appended, so estimators_, alphas_ and errors_ stay aligned.

    real replaces the hard label with the leaf log-odds:

                elif self.variant == "real":
                p1 = np.clip(p[:, 1], 1e-10, 1.0 - 1e-10)
                h = 0.5 * np.log(p1 / (1.0 - p1))
                F += h
                self.alphas_.append(1.0)

    alpha is still recorded as 1 so the prediction loop is uniform, but the step size is inside h. The clip matters here too: a pure leaf would otherwise produce an infinite confidence and a saturated F after one stage.

    Weights are recomputed from F instead of being multiplied in place:

                if self.variant not in ("samme", "samme_r"):
                # exp(-yF) underflows/overflows on separable data; the clip keeps
                # the weights finite without changing the argmin.
                w = np.exp(-y_signed * np.clip(F, -50.0, 50.0))
                w /= w.sum()

    This is algebraically identical to w *= exp(−α y h) and numerically better: the product of many small factors would underflow, and recomputing from the margin keeps the relative weights accurate. The clip is a guard for separable data, where F grows without bound; the exponential loss is flat enough there that the clipped weights pick the same splits.

    Multiclass hard votes add one class score at a time:

                elif self.variant == "samme":
                pred = np.argmax(p, axis=1)
                err = float((w * (pred != yi)).sum())
                err = float(np.clip(err, 1e-10, 1.0 - 1e-10))
                alpha = np.log((1.0 - err) / err) + np.log(k - 1.0)
                if err >= 1.0 - 1.0 / k:
                    break
                F[np.arange(n), pred] += alpha

    F[np.arange(n), pred] += alpha is a scatter-add: row i's score for its predicted class increases. argmax of those scores is the vote, so the update is the multiclass analogue of sign. The ln(K−1) term is what makes α zero at chance level; omit it and SAMME keeps boosting a learner that knows nothing.

    samme_r uses the log-probabilities directly, centred across classes:

                else:                                     # samme_r
                pk = np.clip(p, 1e-10, 1.0)
                logp = np.log(pk)
                h = (k - 1.0) * (logp - logp.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True))
                F += h
                self.alphas_.append(1.0)
                err = float((w * (np.argmax(p, axis=1) != yi)).sum())
                w = w * pk[np.arange(n), yi] ** (-(k - 1.0) / k)
                w /= w.sum()

    logp.mean(axis=1) is the per-row mean over classes, so h sums to zero across classes and only the relative log-probabilities enter. The weight update indexes the true class's probability with pk[np.arange(n), yi] — a fixed k=… index would upweight every row by the same class's confidence and destroy the reweighting.

    stagewise is the only variant whose step is computed numerically:

        @staticmethod
    def _line_search(y_signed: np.ndarray, h: np.ndarray, w: np.ndarray) -> float:
        """Minimise sum w exp(-gamma * y * h) over gamma >= 0 by Newton steps."""
        s = y_signed * h
        gamma = 1.0
        for _ in range(20):
            e = np.exp(-gamma * s)
            f1 = -float((w * s * e).sum())
            f2 = float((w * s * s * e).sum())
            if f2 <= EPS:
                return 0.0
            step = f1 / f2
            gamma = float(np.clip(gamma - step, 0.0, 5.0))
            if abs(step) < 1e-9:
                break
        return gamma

    s = y·h is the signed agreement, so a leaf that agrees with the label contributes a positive exponent. f2 is the curvature and is always positive unless every s is zero; the EPS guard returns a zero step rather than dividing by it. The clip to [0, 5] keeps the line search in a sensible range — a negative γ would fit the learner backwards, which is discrete's failure mode generalised.

    Prediction replays the stored (tree, alpha) pairs, and the discrete branch rebuilds the hard label from the stored probability:

                for tree, alpha in zip(self.estimators_, self.alphas_, strict=True):
                p = _tree_proba(tree, X, k)
                if self.variant == "discrete":
                    F += alpha * (2.0 * (p[:, 1] > 0.5) - 1.0)

    Storing probabilities rather than hard labels in the tree is what lets one tree serve all six variants; the variant decides how to read them at prediction time. strict=True on the zip turns an accidental mismatch between trees and alphas into an error instead of silently dropping stages.

    Complexity #

    Quantity Cost
    One weighted tree (stump) O(d n log n) for the sort + O(d n) prefix sums
    One weighted tree, depth h O(d n h log n)
    T stages T × the tree cost
    Prediction, one row O(T · depth)
    Memory O(T · nodes)

    The weights add no asymptotic cost: they replace counts in the cumulative sums. The sort dominates, and it is repeated at every node. AdaBoost is sequential like gradient boosting — the next tree depends on the current weights — so the only parallelism is inside a tree's split search. Training a few hundred stumps on 400 rows is milliseconds, which is why AdaBoost is still a reasonable first baseline on small tabular problems.

    Failure modes #

    1. Weak learners at chance. discrete stops when err ≥ 0.5. A stump on XOR is exactly at chance, so boosting stumps cannot learn XOR — the demo uses a circle for this reason. The fix is a deeper weak learner or a feature that carries signal.
    2. Label noise. AdaBoost upweights misclassified rows, and a mislabelled row is permanently misclassified, so its weight grows without bound. After enough stages the ensemble fits the noise. The demo's 5% noise is survivable; 20% is not. Regularise with a depth cap and early stopping.
    3. Unbounded margins on separable data. err → 0 makes α → ∞ and F grows without limit. The clip in the weight update keeps the code finite, but the probabilities saturate. Use gentle or real with shrinkage, or stop on a validation curve.
    4. α sign after clipping. Clipping err to 1e-10 on a perfect learner gives α ≈ 11.5, which is finite but large; the next stage sees a nearly uniform weight and stops. Do not read the number of stages as convergence — read errors_.
    5. min_samples_leaf on weights. As noted above, a row-count limit must not be applied to normalised weights, or every deep split becomes invalid. This is the most common implementation bug in hand-written AdaBoost.
    6. Multiclass base rate. samme with err = 1−1/K gives α = 0, but the implementation must also stop: continuing adds zero-weight trees and wastes stages. The break tests the same threshold the α formula uses.
    7. samme_r with zero probabilities. log(0) is −inf; the clip at 1e-10 caps the confidence at about 23, which is large enough to dominate the ensemble. Calibrate the weak learner (it is a weighted tree, so its leaves are smoothed by min_samples_leaf) or clip harder.
    8. Stagewise step size. The line search assumes h is fixed while γ varies. With a deep tree, h is already a fitted function and a large γ moves far from the linearisation point; keep learning_rate small (0.1–0.3) and use more stages.
    9. Probabilities are not calibrated. sigmoid(2F) is a convenient monotone map, not a fitted calibration. The demo checks that probabilities are ordered and sum to one, not that they match frequencies; use isotonic or Platt scaling if the probability itself is the product.

    Applications #

    • Face detection. Viola–Jones is AdaBoost over Haar-like features with a cascade; the reweighting is what selects the few hundred features that matter out of tens of thousands.
    • Small tabular classification. A few hundred stumps give a strong, interpretable baseline (each stump is a threshold on one feature) with no hyperparameter tuning beyond the stage count.
    • Feature selection. The stump chosen at each stage is the most useful single threshold given the current weights; the sequence of chosen features is a cheap importance ranking, and it is the mechanism behind Viola–Jones' cascade.
    • Class-imbalanced detection. The weight update naturally focuses on hard positives, which is useful for rare-event detection — but see failure mode 2, since label noise and rare positives look the same to the algorithm.
    • Teaching boosting. AdaBoost is the cleanest derivation of the exponential-loss view; gradient_boosting.py is the same loop with a pluggable loss, and reading the two side by side is the fastest way to understand modern GBDTs.
    • Multiclass baselines with weak learners. SAMME works with any classifier that accepts sample weights, so it is a wrapper for turning a binary or multiclass stump into a competitive ensemble.
