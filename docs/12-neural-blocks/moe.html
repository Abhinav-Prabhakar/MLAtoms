    Mixture of experts — sparse capacity without sparse gradients

    Intuition #

    A mixture of experts replaces one wide feed-forward network with \(E\) narrow ones and a small router that sends each token to a few of them. The capacity is sparse — a token touches \(k\) experts, not \(E\) — but the parameter gradients are not: every expert owns a full array, and the experts that were not selected simply receive zeros. That combination is the whole engineering story. The model has \(E\) times more parameters but only \(k\) times the arithmetic, and the arrays stay dense so the usual optimiser needs no special case.

    The trade is a new failure surface. The router makes a discrete choice, and a discrete choice has no derivative almost everywhere. A top-1 gate is the constant one, so the task loss sends no gradient to the router at all; only an auxiliary loss can train it. The demo measures this: with every token routed to expert 0, the gradients of experts 1–3 are zero and the router's gradient is zero, while the auxiliary loss still has a usable slope.

    Capacity is the second half. To keep a batch's expert computation bounded, each expert accepts at most \(C\) tokens; the overflow is dropped and its output is zero. The variants divide along these two axes — how many experts per token, who chooses them, and what happens when an expert is full.

    Variants #

    variant token chooses experts kept capacity aux loss
    top1 argmax 1 no no
    topk top-\(k\) softmax \(k\) no no
    expert_choice experts choose tokens variable yes no
    switch argmax 1 no yes
    soft_moe all, weighted \(E\) no optional
    capacity_factor top-\(k\) \(k\) yes no
    load_balance_loss top-\(k\) \(k\) no yes
    shared_expert top-\(k\) plus a shared expert \(k+1\) no no

    The demo uses six input channels, eight hidden units, four experts and twenty-four tokens.

    top1 is the simplest useful mixture: one expert per token, output equal to that expert's output exactly. It has no router gradient from the task loss.

    topk keeps the \(k\) largest logits and renormalises their softmax, so the gates sum to one. With \(k=1\) it is top1; with \(k=E\) it is a dense mixture.

    expert_choice flips the direction: each expert selects its \(C\) favourite tokens. Every expert is full by construction, so load is perfectly balanced, but a token may be chosen zero, one or several times.

    switch is top-1 routing plus the load-balance auxiliary loss. It is the recipe that made sparse mixtures trainable at scale; the capacity mechanism is separated into capacity_factor here so the two effects can be measured independently.

    soft_moe weights every expert by its router probability, with no hard assignment. It has no arithmetic saving but no discrete choice either, so the gradient reaches every expert.

    capacity_factor is top-\(k\) routing with a hard quota \(C=\lceil \alpha n/E\rceil\) per expert; assignments beyond the quota are dropped.

    load_balance_loss is top-\(k\) routing with the auxiliary term, without dropping.

    shared_expert adds one always-on expert to the top-\(k\) mixture, the DeepSeekMoE arrangement: a dense path for common features and sparse experts for the rest.

    Derivation #

    The mixture #

    For a token \(x\) with selected set \(S(x)\) and gates \(g_e\),

    \[y=\sum_{e\in S(x)} g_e\,E_e(x), \qquad E_e(x)=\mathrm{gelu}(xW_1^e+b_1^e)W_2^e+b_2^e . \qquad (1)\]

    The router produces logits and probabilities,

    \[z=xW_r+b_r,\qquad p=\mathrm{softmax}(z),\qquad S=\mathrm{TopK}(z),\qquad g=\mathrm{softmax}(z_S), \qquad (2)\]

    so \(g_j=p_j/\sum_{l\in S}p_l\) on the selected set. The token-wise structure is why the layer is easy to batch: every operation is a matrix product over rows, and no row mixes with another except through the router's normalisation.

    With \(k=1\) the selected set has one element and \(g=1\) identically. That is the key observation behind the Switch auxiliary loss: since \(y=E_{\arg\max z}(x)\), the derivative with respect to \(z\) is zero wherever the argmax is locally constant, so the task loss cannot train the router at all.

    Capacity #

    \[C=\left\lceil \alpha\,\frac{n}{E}\right\rceil, \qquad (3)\]

    where \(n\) is the number of tokens and \(\alpha\) the capacity factor. Each expert accepts the first \(C\) assignments in token order and drops the rest; a dropped slot contributes nothing, so a top-1 token can disappear entirely. The demo sends all 24 tokens to expert 0 with \(\alpha=0.5\) and \(E=4\), so \(C=3\): three outputs survive, twenty-one are exactly zero.

    Load balancing #

    Let \(c_e\) be the number of assignments expert \(e\) received before dropping, \(f_e=c_e/(nk)\) the fraction of all assignments, and \(P_e=\frac1n\sum_i p_{ie}\) the mean router probability. The Switch auxiliary loss is

    \[L_{\mathrm{aux}} =E\sum_{e=1}^{E} f_e P_e . \qquad (4)\]

    At uniform routing \(f_e=1/E\) and \(P_e=1/E\), so \(L_{\mathrm{aux}}=1\). At a collapse onto one expert \(f=(1,0,\dots)\) and \(P\approx(1,0,\dots)\), so \(L_{\mathrm{aux}}=E\). Hence

    \[1\le L_{\mathrm{aux}}\le E, \qquad (5)\]

    with equality at the two extremes. Dividing \(c_e\) by \(nk\) rather than \(n\) is what keeps the lower bound at one when \(k>1\); the original Switch statement is the \(k=1\) case.

    The gradient passes through \(P\) only, because \(f\) is a hard count:

    \[\frac{\partial L_{\mathrm{aux}}}{\partial p_{ie}}=\frac{E f_e}{n}, \qquad \frac{\partial L_{\mathrm{aux}}}{\partial z_{ie}} =p_{ie}\Bigl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial p_{ie}} -\sum_l p_{il}\frac{\partial L}{\partial p_{il}}\Bigr). \qquad (6)\]

    The second factor is the softmax Jacobian. The demo checks (6) against central differences to \(1.4\times10^{-10}\) for switch and \(1.8\times10^{-10}\) for load_balance_loss.

    Gradients through the gate #

    For top-\(k\), let \(d_j=\partial L/\partial g_j\) be the derivative arriving at the selected gates. The softmax over the selected logits gives

    \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_{S_j}} =g_j\Bigl(d_j-\sum_{l\in S}g_l d_l\Bigr), \qquad (7)\]

    and the unselected logits receive zero from this path. The expert gradient is the usual MLP backward pass multiplied by the gate:

    \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial E_e(x)}=g_e\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial y}. \qquad (8)\]

    For \(k=1\), (7) is \(1\cdot(d-d)=0\), which is the zero router gradient the demo measures. For \(k>1\) the gates move with the logits and the router does learn from the task, though the auxiliary term is still what keeps usage even.

    Expert choice #

    Each expert ranks all tokens by its own logit and takes the top \(C\). Let \(m_i=\sum_{e\in S_i}p_{ie}\) be the total probability mass a token received. The gate is the renormalised probability,

    \[g_{ie}=\frac{p_{ie}}{m_i}\,\mathbf 1[e\in S_i], \qquad (9)\]

    and the derivative with respect to the full probability vector is

    \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial p_{if}} =\frac{1}{m_i}\Bigl(d_{if}-\sum_{e\in S_i}d_{ie}g_{ie}\Bigr) \quad\text{for }f\in S_i, \qquad (10)\]

    with zero outside \(S_i\); the softmax Jacobian of (6) then maps it to the logits. Note that \(m_i\) itself depends on \(p\), which is why (10) has the correction term rather than a plain division. The demo's finite differences agree to \(2.4\times10^{-10}\).

    Because each expert takes exactly \(C\) tokens, the counts are balanced without any loss term. The price is collisions: with 24 tokens and \(E=4\), \(C=6\) gives 24 assignment slots for 24 tokens, and the demo finds that 10 tokens are chosen by no expert at all. Their output is zero.

    Soft routing #

    With no selection, \(S_i\) is every expert and \(g=p\):

    \[y_i=\sum_{e=1}^{E}p_{ie}E_e(x_i), \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial p_{ie}} =\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_i}\cdot E_e(x_i). \qquad (11)\]

    Every expert contributes to every token, so no capacity is needed and no assignment can be wasted. The auxiliary loss still applies, but now \(f_e=P_e\), so (4) becomes \(E\sum_e P_e^2\), which is minimised by uniform mean probability. Its gradient replaces the constant \(E f_e/n\) in (6) with \(2E P_e/n\); the demo checks that case to \(3.6\times10^{-10}\).

    Shared expert #

    \[y=\mathrm{Shared}(x)+\sum_{e\in S(x)}g_eE_e(x). \qquad (12)\]

    The shared branch sees every token, so it always receives gradient and acts as a dense residual path. Its parameters are one more expert's worth. The demo subtracts a plain topk model with the same seed and finds the difference is exactly the shared output, then perturbs the router and confirms the shared branch does not move.

    Parameter and arithmetic counts #

    With input width \(d\), hidden width \(h\) and \(E\) experts,

    \[P_{\mathrm{expert}}=2dh+h+d,\qquad P_{\mathrm{router}}=dE+E,\qquad P_{\mathrm{total}}=EP_{\mathrm{expert}}+P_{\mathrm{router}} . \qquad (13)\]

    The small demo model has \(d=4\), \(h=5\), \(E=3\), so each expert owns \(40+5+4=49\) parameters and the whole mixture owns \(3\cdot49+15=162\). A forward pass costs \(k\) expert MLPs per token instead of \(E\); a dense soft mixture costs all \(E\). The router costs \(O(dE)\) per token.

    Code walkthrough #

    The auxiliary loss is the normalized product of hard usage and soft probability, and its lower bound is one exactly when both are uniform:

    def _load_balance(probability: np.ndarray, fractions: np.ndarray) -> float:
    """Switch-style auxiliary loss, minimal at uniform routing."""
    experts = probability.shape[1]
    return float(experts * np.sum(fractions * probability.mean(axis=0)))

    The top-\(k\) router is three lines: rank, gather, renormalise. Nothing is computed for an expert that no token selected, which is where the arithmetic saving lives:

                indices = np.argsort(-logits, axis=1)[:, :width]
            selected = np.take_along_axis(logits, indices, axis=1)
            gates = _softmax(selected)

    Capacity is a greedy fill in token order, so a token that arrives late can be dropped even when its gate is large. This is deliberate: it makes the output independent of the gate magnitudes and keeps the batch order meaningful:

        def _keep(self, n: int, width: int, indices: np.ndarray, capacity: int):
        """Keep at most `capacity` assignments per expert, in token order."""
        used = np.zeros(self.experts, dtype=int)
        kept = np.zeros((n, width), dtype=bool)
        for row in range(n):
            for slot in range(width):
                expert = int(indices[row, slot])
                if used[expert] < capacity:
                    kept[row, slot] = True
                    used[expert] += 1
        return kept, used

    An expert is a one-hidden-layer GELU network. It is deliberately identical in every variant, so the differences in the file are routing differences:

    def _expert_forward(parameters: dict[str, np.ndarray], name: str,
                    x: np.ndarray):
    pre = x @ parameters[f"{name}.W1"] + parameters[f"{name}.b1"]
    hidden = _gelu(pre)
    out = hidden @ parameters[f"{name}.W2"] + parameters[f"{name}.b2"]
    return out, (x, pre, hidden)

    Expert choice inverts the direction of selection. Each expert takes its own top-\(C\) tokens, and the gate is the probability renormalised over whichever experts happened to choose that token:

                capacity = self._capacity(n)
            chosen = np.zeros((n, self.experts), dtype=bool)
            for expert in range(self.experts):
                order = np.argsort(-logits[:, expert])
                chosen[order[:capacity], expert] = True
            mass = (probability * chosen).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
            gates = (probability * chosen) / np.maximum(mass, 1e-12)

    Soft routing is the dense limit: every expert is evaluated and the probability is the weight. There is no branch, no capacity and no zero:

                for expert in range(self.experts):
                out, cache = _expert_forward(p, f"expert{expert}", x)
                output += probability[:, expert, None] * out
                caches[expert] = (rows, None, cache, out)

    The backward pass through a softmax is the same identity in every variant. For top-\(k\) the selected gate derivative is scattered back into the full logit matrix, and the unselected logits get nothing from this path:

                d_selected = gates * (d_gate - (gates * d_gate).sum(
                axis=1, keepdims=True))
            rows = np.arange(n)
            for slot in range(indices.shape[1]):
                np.add.at(dz, (rows, indices[:, slot]), d_selected[:, slot])

    Expert choice adds the correction for the token-dependent normaliser from (10). Without the second term the gradient of a gate would act as if the other gates were fixed, and the finite-difference check would fail by a visible margin:

                correction = (d_gate * gates).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
            d_probability = np.where(
                info["chosen"],
                (d_gate - correction) / np.maximum(mass, 1e-12), 0.0)

    The auxiliary gradient is analytic rather than autograd: differentiate (4), apply the softmax Jacobian, and hand the result to the same optimiser update as the task gradient:

            if self.variant == "soft_moe":
            d_probability = 2.0 * experts * probability.mean(
                axis=0)[None, :] / n
        else:
            d_probability = experts * info["fractions"][None, :] / n
        dz = probability * (d_probability
                            - (probability * d_probability).sum(
                                axis=1, keepdims=True))

    The shared expert is added after the routed sum, so the backward pass sends the same upstream gradient down both branches. No gate multiplies it:

            if self.variant == "shared_expert":
            shared, shared_cache = _expert_forward(p, "shared", x)
            output += shared
            info.update(shared=shared, shared_cache=shared_cache)

    Complexity #

    For \(n\) tokens, width \(d\), hidden width \(h\), \(E\) experts and \(k\) selected experts:

    • Router: \(O(ndE)\) time for the logits, and \(O(nE)\) for the sort or top-\(k\) pass. The router is tiny next to the experts.
    • Experts: \(O(nkdh)\) time and \(O(kdh)\) working memory per token. A dense mixture is \(E\) times the expert cost; soft_moe is exactly that.
    • Parameters: \(E(2dh+h+d)+dE+E\), independent of \(n\) and \(k\). Sparse capacity buys parameters, not a smaller state.
    • Capacity: the greedy fill is \(O(nk)\) and the quota is \(O(\alpha n/E)\) per expert. In this dense implementation dropped tokens are still evaluated and then multiplied by zero, so capacity bounds the output but not the wall-clock work; a real kernel skips the dropped rows.
    • Expert choice: an \(O(n\log n)\) sort per expert, then \(O(E n)\) to build the assignment matrix; the gates are one masked row-normalisation.
    • The demo: eleven sections over at most 24 tokens and 4 experts, plus a few thousand finite-difference forward passes on a 7-token model. It is deterministic and runs in well under a second.

    Failure modes #

    A top-1 router gets no task gradient. The gate is the constant one, so \(y\) is piecewise constant in \(z\); the derivative is zero except at ties. The demo confirms both the unselected experts' gradients and the router gradient are exactly zero. Switch's auxiliary loss is not a nicety: it is the only signal the router receives. A straight-through estimator is the other common patch.

    Dropped tokens are silently lost. With capacity \(\alpha n/E\) and a router that favours one expert, most of a batch can vanish. A top-1 token that is dropped produces a zero row, and the loss then trains on the surviving tokens only. Capacity factors above one and the auxiliary loss both reduce this, at the cost of more compute or a weaker router.

    Expert choice collides. Every expert is full, but tokens are not covered. At 24 tokens, 4 experts and capacity 6, the demo finds 10 tokens chosen by no expert. Raising the capacity factor lowers the collision rate, but the total assignment slots must exceed the token count for coverage, and even then it is not guaranteed.

    Batch composition changes the answer. Capacity and expert choice both depend on the whole batch. A token's output can change when other tokens join or leave, so per-example inference and training disagree. This is a real correctness hazard for serving, not just a numerical detail.

    Dead experts never revive. An expert that is never selected gets zero gradient forever. The auxiliary loss discourages collapse but cannot guarantee that every expert is used, and a hard top-1 router can stay stuck in a self-reinforcing assignment.

    Partial top-\(k\) dropping changes the scale. If one of two selected slots is dropped, the remaining gate is not renormalised, so the output shrinks. Switch's top-1 has the cleaner behaviour: the whole token is dropped. A renormalising variant would keep the scale but change the gradient.

    The shared expert can dominate. The shared branch is a dense path with a guaranteed gradient, and a large shared branch lets the routed experts idle. The DeepSeek recipe uses a small shared expert and a separate balance term; the demo shows the shared output unchanged when the router moves, which is exactly why it is a stable but potentially lazy path.

    Soft routing saves no arithmetic. Every expert runs for every token, so the parameter count is large and the FLOP count is the dense one. It is a way to scale parameters and specialisation, not a way to scale compute.

    The auxiliary loss balances probabilities more easily than assignments. Equation (4) contains \(P\), which is differentiable, and \(f\), which is a hard count. Gradient descent moves \(P\) smoothly but \(f\) only when an argmax flips; the demo needs 600 steps to even out the load. A very sharp router makes \(f\) change in large jumps.

    A gate can be large and still be dropped. Capacity fills in token order, not gate order, so a high-confidence token late in the batch can lose its slot to a low-confidence one that arrived earlier. This keeps the batching simple and the result reproducible, but it is not the max-weight assignment.

    The dense implementation wastes work on dropped rows. forward evaluates every selected expert and multiplies the dropped slots by zero. The output is correct, but the arithmetic saving of the mixture is not realised; a production kernel gathers the kept rows first.

    Applications #

    Switch Transformer, GShard and GLaM made top-1 and top-2 routing with capacity the standard recipe for scaling language models. Mixtral's decoder layers are top-2 mixtures with eight experts per block. DeepSeekMoE adds a shared expert and fine-grained expert segmentation, and its auxiliary-loss-free balancing replaces the term in (4) with a bias update on the router.

    Expert Choice is used where perfect expert balance matters more than token coverage — vision and speech towers with a fixed per-expert batch. Soft MoE targets the same setting with a fully differentiable assignment, and the Perceiver's latent cross-attention is the same idea without the expert ensemble.

    Outside language, V-MoE routes image patches and shows the capacity trade at inference time; multi-task mixtures route by task or by token. The shared-expert pattern also appears in adapters and conditional computation, where a dense path carries the common signal and small branches carry the specialisation.
