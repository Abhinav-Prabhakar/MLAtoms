    Pooling — nine ways to throw away spatial resolution

    Intuition #

    A pooling layer replaces every window of a feature map with one number. It is the cheapest way to make a representation smaller, more tolerant of small shifts, and — in the global forms — independent of the input's spatial size.

    The variants differ only in which number. The maximum keeps the strongest response in the window and throws the rest away. The mean keeps a summary of everything and dilutes the peak. The generalised mean sits between them and is the same function at \(p=1\) and, in the limit, at \(p=\infty\). Attention pooling learns the weights instead of fixing them. Stochastic pooling samples a location, which is a regulariser with a strange-looking but simple expectation. The three global variants are the same operations with the window covering the whole map, and adaptive is the mean or max on a window grid whose sizes are computed from the input rather than fixed.

    The demo measures one property per family. Max and mean agree with brute-force windows on a \(2\times5\times6\times3\) input. adaptive turns seven samples into three with the intervals \([0,3)\), \([2,5)\), \([4,7)\) — overlapping at every seam — and seven into seven as the identity. Attention pooling, trained by gradient descent on a single scalar, moves its output from \(0.924\) to \(4.0000\), the location of the spike, and the learned weight sits on position 6. The generalised mean is monotone in \(p\): geometric \(0.7304\), mean \(0.8713\), rms \(0.9889\), and \(p=64\) reaches \(1.6750\) against a true maximum of \(1.7433\). Stochastic pooling's 4000 draws land on the four inputs with shares \([0.105, 0.240, 0.455, 0.200]\) against exact probabilities \([0.100, 0.250, 0.450, 0.200]\). And the gradient routes exactly where the forward pass read: max sends the whole upstream mass to the argmax, mean splits it evenly, and both conserve the total.

    Variants #

    variant output window learned
    max window maximum fixed no
    avg window mean fixed no
    global_max one value per channel whole map no
    global_avg one value per channel whole map no
    adaptive fixed spatial size floor/ceil grid no
    attention_pool weighted average whole map yes
    gem generalised mean fixed power
    lp_pool unnormalised power sum fixed power
    stochastic one sampled activation fixed power

    max is the default in vision. It keeps the strongest activation in each window, so a feature detector fires if its pattern appears anywhere in the window. The cost is that the gradient goes only to the argmax: a max-pooled layer is a sparse router, not an averaging one.

    avg is the older choice, and it is a box filter divided by its area. It passes gradient to every input in the window, which makes it better behaved in very deep networks, at the price of diluting sparse, high-contrast features.

    global_max and global_avg pool over every spatial position and return one value per channel. Global average pooling is what replaced the fully connected classifier in modern convnets: it has no parameters and no fixed input size.

    adaptive takes the output size and derives the windows. It is how a network accepts images of different resolutions while keeping a fixed-size feature vector.

    attention_pool learns a score per position and takes a softmax-weighted average. It is the set-pooling layer of multiple-instance learning: with a sharp enough temperature it approaches max pooling, and with a flat one it approaches the mean.

    gem is \((\frac1K\sum x_i^p)^{1/p}\). At \(p=1\) it is exactly the mean, at \(p\to\infty\) it is the max, at \(p\to0\) it is the geometric mean, and it is monotone in \(p\) throughout. It interpolates the two classical choices with one hyperparameter.

    lp_pool is \((\sum x_i^p)^{1/p}\) — the same root without the \(1/K\). It differs from gem by exactly \(K^{1/p}\), which the demo checks numerically.

    stochastic samples one location per window with probability proportional to \(x_i^p\). It is a regulariser in the style of dropout: at training time it injects noise, and at test time the expectation is used.

    Derivation #

    Windows #

    Write the window at output position \(\mathbf o\) as

    \[W_{\mathbf o} =\bigl\{\mathbf o\odot s+\mathbf k \;:\; 0\le k_i

    with stride \(s\) and window shape \(\mathbf k\). Every non-adaptive variant is a reduction over \(W_{\mathbf o}\):

    \[y_{b,\mathbf o,c} =\rho\bigl(x_{b,\mathbf p,c}:\mathbf p\in W_{\mathbf o}\bigr). \qquad (2)\]

    The output size is \(\lfloor (n_i-k_i)/s_i\rfloor+1\) per axis. The demo's \(5\times6\) map with a \(2\times2\) window at stride 2 gives \(2\times3\); the last row and column are dropped because the window does not fit, which is the standard behaviour and a frequent source of off-by-one bugs.

    Max and mean #

    \[\rho_{\max}=\max_{\mathbf p\in W}x_{\mathbf p}, \qquad \rho_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{\mathbf p\in W}x_{\mathbf p}, \qquad K=\prod_i k_i . \qquad (3)\]

    The mean is a convolution with a box kernel scaled by \(1/K\),

    \[\rho_{\mathrm{avg}}(x) =\frac{1}{K}\,\mathrm{conv}\bigl(x,\mathbf 1_{\mathbf k}\bigr), \qquad (4)\]

    so it inherits the border behaviour of whatever padding the convolution uses. The max has no such linear form; it is a nonlinear filter, and that is the source of both its tolerance to shifts and its sparse gradient.

    Global pooling #

    \[\rho_{\mathrm{gmax}}(x)_c=\max_{b,\mathbf p}x_{b,\mathbf p,c}, \qquad \rho_{\mathrm{gavg}}(x)_c=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{b,\mathbf p}x_{b,\mathbf p,c}, \qquad (5)\]

    with \(N\) the number of spatial positions. The batch axis is not reduced: the demo returns shape \((2,3)\) from a batch of two with three channels.

    Adaptive windows #

    For input length \(n\) and target length \(m\),

    \[a_i=\left\lfloor\frac{i\,n}{m}\right\rfloor, \qquad b_i=\left\lceil\frac{(i+1)\,n}{m}\right\rceil, \qquad y_i=\rho\bigl(x_{a_i:b_i}\bigr). \qquad (6)\]

    For \(n=7\), \(m=3\) this gives \((0,3)\), \((2,5)\), \((4,7)\): the windows overlap by one and every input is covered. For \(n=m\) it gives \((i,i+1)\), the identity. The overlap is the price of exact coverage with a fixed output size; it also means neighbouring outputs are correlated, which is why adaptive pooling is a poor choice when the outputs feed an independence assumption.

    Attention pooling #

    With a learned vector \(w\) and temperature \(\tau\),

    \[u_p=\frac{w^{\top}x_p}{\tau}, \qquad \alpha_p=\frac{e^{u_p}}{\sum_q e^{u_q}}, \qquad y=\sum_p \alpha_p x_p . \qquad (7)\]

    The temperature sets how sharply the weights concentrate. The demo's table has values \(4.0\) at position 6, \(1.0\) at 3, \(0.5\) at 8 and zeros elsewhere. At \(\tau=0.05\) and a learned \(w=0.01\) the initial output is \(0.924\) — a weighted average pulled toward the spike but still mixing in the zeros. Gradient descent on \(w\) against the target \(4.0\) raises \(w\), sharpens the softmax, and drives the output to the peak. The temperature is not learned here, but it is the hyperparameter that decides whether the layer behaves like a mean or a max.

    Generalised mean #

    \[M_p=\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum_i x_i^p\right)^{1/p}. \qquad (8)\]

    \(p=1\) is the arithmetic mean. As \(p\to\infty\) the largest term dominates and \(M_p\to\max_i x_i\); as \(p\to-\infty\) it tends to the minimum. At \(p=0\) the limit is the geometric mean,

    \[M_0=\left(\prod_i x_i\right)^{1/K} =\exp\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum_i\log x_i\right), \qquad (9)\]

    which is why the implementation special-cases \(p=0\) rather than computing \(1/0\). All of this needs \(x_i>0\) for the log form and \(x_i\ge0\) in general: a negative entry with a fractional power is undefined, and the code raises rather than returning a NaN that would propagate silently.

    Monotonicity in \(p\) follows from Jensen's inequality applied to the convex function \(t\mapsto t^p\); the demo asserts it on the concrete values.

    LP pooling #

    \[L_p=\left(\sum_i x_i^p\right)^{1/p} =K^{1/p}M_p . \qquad (10)\]

    The factor \(K^{1/p}\) is the only difference. At \(p=2\) and \(K=16\) it is a factor of \(4\), which the demo multiplies back out and compares to machine precision. PyTorch's LPPool exposes this unnormalised form because it composes with other scale factors; the generalised mean is the version with a meaningful absolute scale.

    Stochastic pooling #

    Choose an index \(i\) with probability

    \[P(i)=\frac{x_i^p}{\sum_j x_j^p}, \qquad \mathbb E[x_I]=\frac{\sum_i x_i^{p+1}}{\sum_i x_i^p}. \qquad (11)\]

    The expectation is a ratio of consecutive moments, not the mean: for the demo's four values with \(p=1\) it is \((0.2^2+0.5^2+0.9^2+0.4^2)/(0.2+0.5+0.9+0.4)=1.26/2.0=0.63\), between the mean \(0.5\) and the max \(0.9\). At inference one either uses this expectation or averages many samples; using a single sample at test time is a bug that costs accuracy quietly.

    Gradients #

    For max, the derivative is a selector; for mean, it is a constant:

    \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_i} =g\cdot\mathbb 1\bigl[i=\arg\max_j x_j\bigr], \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial x_i}=\frac{g}{K}. \qquad (12)\]

    Ties are the subtle case. The demo splits the gradient evenly among tied maxima, which is one of several conventions; taking the first index is another. Either is defensible, but the choice has to match between forward and backward, or the gradient check fails only on the rare tied batch.

    For the generalised mean, differentiating (8) gives

    \[\frac{\partial M_p}{\partial x_i} =\frac{1}{K}\,M_p^{1-p}x_i^{p-1}, \qquad (13)\]

    which reduces to \(1/K\) at \(p=1\) as it must, and concentrates on the largest entry as \(p\) grows. Stochastic pooling has no pathwise derivative through the sampled index; it is trained either with REINFORCE or, more commonly, treated as a noise layer whose gradient is taken as if the chosen value were the mean.

    Code walkthrough #

    Every non-adaptive variant reads windows from one view:

        dims = len(size)
    view = np.lib.stride_tricks.sliding_window_view(
        x, size, axis=tuple(range(1, dims + 1)))
    steps = (slice(None),) + tuple(slice(None, None, step) for step in stride)
    return view[steps]

    sliding_window_view appends the window axes after the channel axis, so the result is (batch, *output, channels, *window) and the reduction is over the trailing dims axes. The view is read-only and copies nothing; the stride is applied by subsampling the output axes, not by moving the window. Getting that order wrong produces windows that overlap in the wrong direction, which still runs and still pools — just not the windows you asked for.

    The reduction is one branch per family:

        if how == "gem":
        if np.any(windows < 0.0):
            raise ValueError("gem needs non-negative inputs")
        if power == 0.0:
            if np.any(windows <= 0.0):
                raise ValueError("the geometric mean needs positive inputs")
            return np.exp(np.log(windows).mean(axis=axes))
        moment = np.mean(windows ** power, axis=axes)
        return np.power(moment, 1.0 / power)

    The guards are not decoration: 0.0 ** -1 is inf, (-1.0) ** 0.5 is nan, and log(0) is -inf. Each of those propagates through a whole network before anyone notices, so the entry points refuse them.

    Adaptive windows are computed per output index:

    def _bounds(size: int, out: int, index: int) -> tuple[int, int]:
    """The torch adaptive rule: floor at the start, ceiling at the end."""
    start = index * size // out
    end = -(-(index + 1) * size // out)
    return start, max(end, start + 1)

    The max(end, start + 1) is defensive: for whole-number inputs the ceiling is already at least start + 1, but stating the invariant where the window is built beats relying on it. When the target is larger than the input the windows repeat inputs, which is the defined behaviour of adaptive upsampling.

        for index in np.ndindex(*target):
        window = tuple(slice(*_bounds(x.shape[1 + axis], target[axis], start))
                       for axis, start in enumerate(index))
        piece = x[(slice(None), *window)]
        out[(slice(None), *index)] = piece.max(axis=tuple(range(1, dims + 1))) \
            if reduce == "max" else piece.mean(axis=tuple(range(1, dims + 1)))
    return out

    The slice(*_bounds(...)) is (6) written directly. The reduction axes are 1..dims, which leaves batch and channels untouched, so the output keeps its channel dimension even when the spatial size collapses to one.

    Attention pooling is a softmax over flattened positions:

        flat = x.reshape(x.shape[0], -1, x.shape[-1])
    scores = (flat @ weight) / temperature
    scores -= scores.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    weights = np.exp(scores)
    weights /= weights.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    pooled = np.einsum("bn,bnc->bc", weights, flat)

    The shift by the row maximum is the standard stabiliser: softmax is invariant to adding a constant to every score, and subtracting the largest keeps the exponentials below one. Without it, a temperature of \(0.01\) overflows within one epoch.

    Stochastic pooling builds a cumulative distribution and draws once per output:

        cumulative = np.cumsum(weights / total, axis=-1)
    draw = rng.random(cumulative.shape[:-1] + (1,))
    chosen = np.minimum((cumulative < draw).sum(axis=-1), flat.shape[-1] - 1)
    return np.take_along_axis(flat, chosen[..., None], axis=-1)[..., 0]

    (cumulative < draw).sum() is inverse-transform sampling without a Python loop: the number of bins below the uniform draw is the chosen index. The minimum guards against a floating-point draw of exactly 1.0, which would otherwise index one past the end.

    The gradient router is the forward pass read backwards:

            if mode == "mean":
            gradient[(slice(None), *window)] += (flows / count)[(slice(None),
                                                                 *([None] * dims))]
            continue
        best = piece.max(axis=tuple(range(1, dims + 1)), keepdims=True)
        hits = piece == best
        hits = hits / hits.sum(axis=tuple(range(1, dims + 1)), keepdims=True)
        gradient[(slice(None), *window)] += \
            hits * flows[(slice(None), *([None] * dims))]

    For mean, the upstream value is divided by the window size and broadcast over the window. For max, hits is the indicator of (12), normalised so ties share the gradient instead of each receiving the full amount. The window slices overlap, so this must be +=, exactly as in the convolution adjoint.

    The dispatcher is mostly shape bookkeeping:

        if variant in ("global_max", "global_avg"):
        axes = tuple(range(1, dims + 1))
        return x.max(axis=axes) if variant == "global_max" else x.mean(axis=axes)

    Global pooling is not the fixed-window path with a large window: it has no window at all, so it must not pay the cost of materialising one. The same distinction lets it accept any spatial size without a stride calculation.

    Complexity #

    For batch \(B\), \(D\) spatial axes, \(N\) output positions, \(K\) window volume and \(C\) channels:

    • Arithmetic: \(O(B\,N\,K\,C)\) comparisons for max and additions for mean, \(O(B\,N\,K\,C)\) again for the backward pass. Pooling is cheaper than the convolution that precedes it by a factor of the kernel volume, because it has no channel mixing.
    • Memory: the window view is free, but the reduction reads \(B\,N\,K\,C\) elements. A \(2\times2\) window at stride 2 reads each input once; overlapping windows read some inputs several times.
    • Global pooling: \(O(B\,N\,C)\) and no temporary, since numpy reduces in place along the spatial axes.
    • Attention pooling: \(O(B\,N\,C)\) for the scores plus \(O(B\,N)\) for the softmax and \(O(B\,N\,C)\) for the weighted sum — linear in positions, not quadratic, because there is no pairwise attention.
    • Adaptive pooling: \(O(B\,N\,K\,C)\) with a Python loop over outputs, so the constant is worse than the fixed-window path.
    • Stochastic pooling: one cumulative sum and one uniform draw per output, the same order as mean pooling plus \(O(B\,N\,K\,C)\) for the normalisation.

    Failure modes #

    Max pooling has a sparse, biased gradient. Only the argmax of each window receives gradient, so a unit that never wins its window never learns. In deep networks this shows up as dead filters, and it is the reason average pooling and strided convolution are common alternatives.

    Ties are a convention, not a fact. Two equal maxima have no unique gradient. Splitting evenly, taking the first, and taking all of them are all used in practice; the demo splits. A mismatch between the forward selector and the backward router produces a gradient that looks almost right and fails a finite-difference check only on tied inputs.

    Adaptive pooling correlates neighbouring outputs. The windows overlap by construction, so the outputs are not independent samples of the input. Feeding them to a model that assumes independence — a naive Bayes head, or a per-position loss — silently overcounts the shared region.

    Pooling loses localisation. A global average is invariant to where a feature is; that is the point, and it is also why a classifier with global pooling cannot report a bounding box. Global max keeps a little more, attention pooling keeps the weights, and neither is a detector.

    Stochastic pooling is stochastic at test time too. A single draw has high variance, and using it at inference makes predictions non-reproducible. Either average many draws or use the analytic expectation of (11); the demo's frequency check is exactly the evidence that sampling matches the intended distribution.

    Generalised mean is undefined off the positive orthant. Negative activations with a fractional power produce NaNs, and \(p=0\) needs strictly positive inputs. ReLU outputs are safe; post-normalisation features with negative values are not, so GEM is usually placed after a ReLU.

    Large \(p\) overflows. \(x^p\) for \(p=64\) and \(x=10\) is \(10^{64}\), close to the edge of float64; \(p=1000\) overflows outright. Implementations either work in logs or cap \(p\); this one caps nothing and relies on the caller's scale.

    A window that does not divide the input drops the tail. The output size formula floors, so a \(7\)-wide map with a \(2\)-wide window at stride 2 returns three windows and silently ignores the seventh column. Use ceil_mode padding, or an adaptive target, if the tail matters.

    Pooling is not shift invariant, only shift tolerant. A shift smaller than the window changes the pooled values; a shift by a multiple of the stride permutes the outputs. Calling max pooling "translation invariant" is the most common overstatement in the literature.

    Applications #

    Pooling is the downsampling stage of every convolutional classifier. Max pooling after a block of convolutions enlarges the receptive field and keeps the strongest evidence; average pooling smooths and is gentler on the gradient. Modern architectures often replace both with a strided convolution, which learns the downsample instead of fixing it, but the pooling variants remain the interpretable baseline.

    Global average pooling is the standard classifier head. It removes the large fully connected layer, makes the model accept arbitrary input sizes, and acts as a strong regulariser — the ResNet family's parameter count depends on it.

    Adaptive pooling is what lets one network serve several resolutions. It is also the pooling used in detection and segmentation heads, where the output grid is fixed by the task rather than by the input.

    Attention pooling is the multiple-instance-learning layer: a bag of patches, one label for the bag, and a learned weight per patch. It is used in histopathology, whole-slide imaging, and any set-valued problem where the important element is unknown.

    Generalised mean pooling is the retrieval standard. GeM with a learned \(p\) outperforms both max and average on image retrieval benchmarks, because the right amount of peakiness depends on the dataset and can be learned.

    Stochastic pooling is a training-time regulariser with a long tail of use in small vision models. It is also a clean example of a layer whose expectation is tractable but whose samples are not differentiable, which is the setting that motivates reparameterisation tricks elsewhere.
