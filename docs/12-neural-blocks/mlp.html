    12 · Neural blocks/tier 1/6 variants/source

    MLP — the affine layer, and four ways to make it deep

    Intuition #

    An MLP is a stack of affine maps with a nonlinearity between them. Everything else in this chapter — convolution, attention, gating, normalisation — is a variation on how those two operations are arranged. The interesting question is not what an MLP is, but what makes a stack of them trainable at depth, and that is what the variants here measure.

    Four ideas cover most of the design space:

    • The skip connection adds the block's input to its output. It costs no parameters, and it gives the gradient an additive path to the input.
    • Pre-normalisation applies layer norm on the way into the block, so the branch always sees a standardised input while the residual stream stays untouched.
    • Gating multiplies one learned projection by a sigmoid of another. The output is a product of two functions of the input, which is a richer object than a single activation applied to one projection.
    • SwiGLU replaces the sigmoid gate with a SiLU-activated branch multiplied by a linear branch, and projects the product back. It is the feed-forward layer of most current language models.

    The demo fits all six variants to three interleaved spiral arms — a problem no affine map can solve, because the classes are not linearly separable. On 300 training points and 60 held out, the affine model lands at \(0.333\), exactly chance. The five nonlinear stacks land between \(0.983\) and \(1.000\). That is the easy half. The hard half is the depth study: at twelve layers the plain stack reaches \(0.833\) and the pre-normalised residual reaches \(0.983\), but the ordering flips at a different epoch budget, so the architecture claim has to be made somewhere other than accuracy. It is made on the gradient path, which is exact.

    Variants #

    variant block depth parameters test accuracy
    linear affine head 0 9 0.333
    mlp affine, ReLU 3 3363 1.000
    residual_mlp affine, ReLU, skip 3 3363 0.983
    gated_mlp affine, ReLU gate 3 6531 1.000
    swiglu_mlp SiLU gate, product, projection 3 9699 0.983
    deep_mlp affine, pre-norm, skip 6 6531 0.983

    linear is the control. It has nine parameters and cannot separate three spirals; its accuracy is chance and its role is to prove the dataset needs nonlinearity.

    mlp is a three-layer ReLU stack between the stem and the logits. It reaches \(1.000\) on the sixty held-out points.

    residual_mlp adds \(x\) to every hidden block. Parameter count is identical to mlp — \(3363\) against \(3363\) — because the skip is free. Accuracy is \(0.983\), one point lower, which is noise.

    gated_mlp multiplies a ReLU branch by a sigmoid gate. It carries \(3168\) more parameters than the plain stack, all of them in the gate matrices, and reaches \(1.000\).

    swiglu_mlp uses the SwiGLU form: two projections, one activated by SiLU, their elementwise product, and a third projection back to the model width. It is the largest model at \(9699\) parameters and reaches \(0.983\).

    deep_mlp is the same width and parameter count as mlp but with pre-normalised residual blocks, and the demo runs it at depth six. The depth study below is the reason it exists.

    The accuracy column is nearly saturated, which is deliberate: a spiral is a solved problem, and the variants are not here to be ranked by a task this small. The measurements that distinguish them are the parameter counts, the gate statistics and the gradient path.

    Derivation #

    One layer #

    \[h=\phi\bigl(xW+b\bigr),\qquad x\in\mathbb R^{n},\ W\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}. \qquad (1)\]

    The affine map alone cannot do anything a single matrix could not do. Stacking without \(\phi\) composes affines into an affine:

    \[W_2^{\top}(W_1^{\top}x+b_1)+b_2 =\tilde W^{\top}x+\tilde b, \qquad (2)\]

    so a ten-layer linear network is exactly a one-layer linear network with a different weight matrix. The demo's linear variant is the honest version of that statement: nine parameters, chance accuracy.

    A stack #

    \[h_0=x,\qquad h_\ell=\phi\bigl(h_{\ell-1}W_\ell+b_\ell\bigr),\qquad \hat y=\mathrm{softmax}\bigl(h_LW_{\mathrm{head}}+b_{\mathrm{head}}\bigr). \qquad (3)\]

    With ReLU and He initialisation, the forward variance is preserved in expectation:

    \[\mathrm{Var}(w)=\frac{2}{n} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbb E\bigl[h^{2}\bigr]=\tfrac12 n\,\mathrm{Var}(w)\,\mathbb E\bigl[x^{2}\bigr] =\mathbb E\bigl[x^{2}\bigr]. \qquad (4)\]

    The factor \(1/2\) is the probability that a ReLU unit is active. Without it the activations halve at every layer, and with He's \(2/n\) the standard deviation of the output is roughly constant across depth. The demo's _Dense uses \(2/n\) for ReLU and \(1/n\) for the identity stem and head, which is the same rule with the \(1/2\) removed.

    Residual #

    \[y=x+F(x),\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial x} =\frac{\partial L}{\partial y}\Bigl(I+\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\Bigr). \qquad (5)\]

    The identity term is the point. Expand the product over \(L\) blocks and every term that picks at least one \(I\) survives; only the single term that picks \(\partial F/\partial x\) at every block has no identity in it. A gradient therefore reaches the input through \(2^{L}-1\) paths instead of one, and the \(I\)-only path has gain exactly one at every depth.

    That is what the demo measures. Its gradient-norm ratio from the logits to the input is

    depth 2 4 8 12
    mlp 1.252 1.156 0.883 1.518
    deep_mlp 2.394 2.348 4.355 5.445

    The plain column hovers around one whatever the depth; the residual column grows because of the identity term. And when every branch is set to zero the residual chain passes a gradient through twelve blocks with a norm ratio of exactly \(1.000000\) — not approximately, exactly, because the chain has become the identity.

    Pre-normalisation #

    \[y=x+F\bigl(\mathrm{LN}(x)\bigr),\qquad \mathrm{LN}(x)=\frac{x-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+\epsilon}}. \qquad (6)\]

    Normalising the input to the branch, rather than its output, keeps the residual stream in whatever scale it has accumulated. A post-normalised block renormalises \(x+F(x)\) and therefore changes the stream at every layer; the pre-normalised version leaves it alone. That is why pre-norm is the default in deep transformers, and it is why (5) holds exactly here: the \(I\) term is untouched by the normalisation.

    The normalisation itself is elementwise over the width, with the mean and variance computed per row:

    \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial x} =\frac{1}{\sigma}\Bigl(g-\bar g-\hat x\,\overline{g\hat x}\Bigr), \qquad (7)\]

    with \(g=\partial L/\partial \hat x\) and bars denoting means over the width. The two subtraction terms are the gradients through \(\sigma\) and \(\mu\); dropping either one produces a direction that still decreases the loss, which is exactly why the error survives code review.

    Gating #

    \[y=\phi\bigl(xW+b\bigr)\odot\sigma\bigl(xV+c\bigr). \qquad (8)\]

    The gate is a second linear function of the input, squashed into \((0,1)\). The product is not a nonlinearity applied to a linear map; it is a bilinear interaction between two learned projections, which is why it costs an extra \(n d + d\) parameters per layer. The demo's fitted gate has mean \(0.453\) with \(44.3\%\) of units above one half: it is modulating, not saturated.

    SwiGLU replaces the sigmoid with a SiLU on the first branch and drops the squash on the second:

    \[y=\bigl(\mathrm{SiLU}(xW_1+b_1)\odot(xW_3+b_3)\bigr)W_2+b_2, \qquad (9)\]
    \[\mathrm{SiLU}(u)=u\,\sigma(u),\qquad \mathrm{SiLU}'(u)=\sigma(u)\bigl(1+u(1-\sigma(u))\bigr). \qquad (10)\]

    The second branch is linear, so the product can take either sign and the layer is not limited to a convex combination of two non-negative quantities. That extra range is the reason SwiGLU outperformed the sigmoid GLU in the language modelling comparisons that introduced it, at the cost of a third matrix per layer.

    Backward pass #

    Each block stores what its gradient needs. For (1),

    \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}=x^{\top}\bigl(g\odot\phi'(z)\bigr),\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial x} =\bigl(g\odot\phi'(z)\bigr)W^{\top},\qquad z=xW+b, \qquad (11)\]

    and for (8) the product rule splits the incoming gradient into a branch gradient and a gate gradient:

    \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial a}=g\odot\sigma(v),\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial v}=g\odot a\odot\sigma(v)\bigl(1-\sigma(v)\bigr), \qquad (12)\]

    with \(a=\phi(xW+b)\) and \(v=xV+c\). Because both branches read \(x\), the input gradient is the sum of their contributions.

    The loss is softmax cross-entropy over \(K\) classes:

    \[L=-\frac1B\sum_{i=1}^{B}\log p_{i,y_i},\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i}=\frac{1}{B}\bigl(p_i-e_{y_i}\bigr), \qquad (13)\]

    where \(e_{y}\) is the one-hot label. The subtraction of the label is what makes the gradient small when the prediction is right, and the division by \(B\) is what makes the reported loss and the gradient consistent.

    Parameter count #

    For input width \(n\), hidden width \(d\), depth \(L\) and \(K\) classes,

    \[P_{\mathrm{plain}}=nd+d+L(d^{2}+d)+dK+K, \qquad (14)\]

    with \(L(d^{2}+d)\) extra for the gate in (8) and \(2L(d^{2}+d)\) extra for SwiGLU in (9). At \(n=2\), \(d=32\), \(L=3\), \(K=3\) that is \(96+3168+99=3363\) for mlp, residual_mlp and deep_mlp, \(6531\) for gated_mlp and \(9699\) for swiglu_mlp. The skip connection and the layer norm contribute nothing, which is the whole argument for using them.

    Adam #

    \[\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\, \frac{m/(1-0.9^{t})}{\sqrt{v/(1-0.999^{t})}+\epsilon},\qquad m=0.9m+0.1g,\quad v=0.999v+0.001g^{2}, \qquad (15)\]

    with \(t\) the global minibatch step, not the epoch. Correcting by the epoch number instead of the step leaves the first epoch badly scaled and is a common off-by-one in hand-written loops.

    Code walkthrough #

    The initialisation is the only place where the activation changes the scale:

            scale = np.sqrt(1.0 / inputs)
        if activation == "relu":
            scale = np.sqrt(2.0 / inputs)

    Equation (4) is the reason for the branch: a ReLU kills half its inputs in expectation, so the weight variance has to double to compensate. The identity stem and head keep the LeCun scale, because they see every unit.

    A dense layer keeps only what its backward pass needs:

        def forward(self, x: np.ndarray):
        p = self.parameters
        function, derivative = ACTIVATIONS[self.activation]
        pre = x @ p[f"{self.name}.W"] + p[f"{self.name}.b"]
        return function(pre), (x, pre, derivative(pre))

    The cache holds the input, the pre-activation and the already-evaluated derivative. Evaluating \(\phi'\) in the forward pass costs one array per layer and saves recomputing it from the pre-activation in the backward pass — and, more importantly, guarantees the two use the same value.

    The backward pass is (11):

            x, _pre, slope = cache
        p, g = self.parameters, self.gradients
        d_pre = gradient * slope
        g[f"{self.name}.W"][...] = x.T @ d_pre
        g[f"{self.name}.b"][...] = d_pre.sum(axis=0)
        return d_pre @ p[f"{self.name}.W"].T

    The in-place [...] assignment matters: the optimiser holds references to the same arrays, so rebinding g[key] to a new array would silently disconnect the gradient from the parameter it belongs to.

    The gate splits the incoming gradient into its two branches:

            d_active = gradient * gate
        d_logits = gradient * active * gate * (1.0 - gate)
        d_pre = d_active * _relu_grad(pre)
        g[f"{self.name}.W"][...] = x.T @ d_pre
        g[f"{self.name}.b"][...] = d_pre.sum(axis=0)
        g[f"{self.name}.V"][...] = x.T @ d_logits
        g[f"{self.name}.c"][...] = d_logits.sum(axis=0)
        return d_pre @ p[f"{self.name}.W"].T + d_logits @ p[f"{self.name}.V"].T

    d_logits is (12): the gradient into the gate, multiplied by the gate's own derivative \(\sigma(1-\sigma)\). Both branches read x, so the input gradient is the sum of two matrix products.

    SwiGLU applies the product rule one level deeper:

            d_product = gradient @ p[f"{self.name}.W2"].T
        d_upper = d_product * value * _silu_grad(upper)
        d_value = d_product * _silu(upper)

    The gradient splits symmetrically: the SiLU branch receives \(d_{\text{product}} \odot v\) and the linear branch receives \(d_{\text{product}}\odot \mathrm{SiLU}(u)\). Getting the two the wrong way round is the natural bug, and it still trains — just to a worse optimum.

    The residual wrapper is (5) plus a normalisation:

        def backward(self, gradient: np.ndarray, cache):
        norm_cache, block_cache = cache
        d_normed = self.block.backward(gradient, block_cache)
        if self.normalise:
            d_normed = _layer_norm_backward(d_normed, norm_cache)
        return gradient + d_normed

    The gradient + is the identity path, and it is added after the branch is back-propagated through the normaliser. Adding before, then normalising the sum, is the mistake that was in this file first: it makes the identity term subject to the layer norm's Jacobian and breaks the exactness the section above relies on.

    Layer norm's backward is (7) written out:

        centred, inverse = cache
    width = gradient.shape[-1]
    scaled = (gradient * centred).sum(axis=-1, keepdims=True)
    total = gradient.sum(axis=-1, keepdims=True)
    return (inverse / width) * (
        width * gradient - total - centred * inverse ** 2 * scaled)

    total removes the gradient through the mean and scaled removes it through the variance. The inverse / width prefactor carries the \(1/\sigma\) and the averaging over the width in one place.

    The variant dispatch is a single if-chain, so the differences between the six models are visible in one screen:

                    if self.variant == "mlp":
                    blocks.append(_Dense(name, self.width, self.width, rng))
                elif self.variant == "residual_mlp":
                    blocks.append(_Residual(_Dense(name, self.width,
                                                   self.width, rng)))
                elif self.variant == "gated_mlp":
                    blocks.append(_GLU(name, self.width, self.width, rng))
                elif self.variant == "swiglu_mlp":
                    blocks.append(_SwiGLU(name, self.width, self.width, rng))
                else:
                    blocks.append(_Residual(_Dense(name, self.width,
                                                   self.width, rng),
                                            normalise=True))

    The stem is always a dense layer and the head is always an affine map; only the hidden blocks change. That is why the parameter counts in the table follow (14) exactly.

    Cross-entropy is (13), with the label subtraction done in place:

        probabilities = _softmax(logits)
    rows = np.arange(labels.size)
    loss = float(-np.mean(np.log(probabilities[rows, labels] + 1e-12)))
    gradient = probabilities.copy()
    gradient[rows, labels] -= 1.0
    return loss, gradient / labels.size

    The 1e-12 only matters for a numerically zero probability, which Adam can produce on a confidently wrong example. Without it the loss becomes infinite and the run dies at the first such batch.

    Complexity #

    For batch size \(B\), input width \(n\), hidden width \(d\), depth \(L\) and \(K\) classes, per forward pass:

    • Plain and residual stacks: \(O\bigl(B(nd+Ld^{2}+dK)\bigr)\). The residual adds an addition per layer and nothing else.
    • Gated and SwiGLU: the same with the per-layer term multiplied by two or three, matching the parameter counts in (14).
    • Layer norm: \(O(BLd)\) for the means and variances, negligible against the matrix products.
    • Backward pass: the same cost again, plus the transpose products.
    • One epoch: the above times the number of minibatches. The demo's 300 points at batch 64 is five steps per epoch, and six models at 300 epochs finish in about \(2.5\) seconds.
    • Memory: \(O(BLd)\) for the caches, which is what makes the hand-written backward pass possible.

    The quadratic term in \(d\) is why width is expensive and depth is cheap: doubling \(d\) quadruples the per-layer cost, while doubling \(L\) only doubles it. Modern architectures spend their parameters on width inside a block and keep the block count modest.

    Failure modes #

    A stack without a nonlinearity is one matrix. Equation (2) is exact, and the demo's linear variant is the demonstration: nine parameters, chance accuracy. Removing the activation from an "MLP" does not make it a weaker model, it makes it a different model with the same expressive power as logistic regression.

    Sixty test points cannot rank six models. One point is \(1.7\) percentage points of accuracy. The five nonlinear variants sit within two points of each other, and residual_mlp at \(0.983\) is not meaningfully worse than mlp at \(1.000\). A benchmark at this scale can show that a mechanism is not broken; it cannot show that one mechanism beats another.

    Depth is a lottery at this size. At depth twelve with 300 epochs the plain stack reaches \(0.833\) and the pre-normalised residual \(0.983\); at 200 epochs the same comparison came out the other way, and at depth eight both reach \(1.000\). The gradient path in (5) is a theorem; the accuracy ordering is a draw. Report the former and treat the latter as an anecdote, which is why the demo asserts the gradient ratio and only prints the accuracies.

    Dead ReLUs never come back. A unit whose pre-activation is negative for every input has zero gradient and zero output forever. At learning rate \(0.01\) with He initialisation this is rare on standardised inputs, but one bad batch — a feature with a huge scale, or a label imbalance — can kill a large fraction of the width. The signature is a training loss that stops moving while the weights are still finite.

    A saturated gate is a dead gate. The sigmoid in (8) has derivative at most \(0.25\), and at \(|v|>6\) it is numerically zero. If the gate drifts to saturation early, that branch stops learning and the layer degenerates into a masked linear map. The demo measures the gate distribution for this reason: mean \(0.453\) is healthy, mean \(0.02\) would not be.

    Standardisation is part of the model. fit stores the training mean and spread and predict reuses them. Fitting the scaler on the full dataset before splitting leaks the test distribution into training, and the leak is invisible in the accuracy because it inflates it. Split first, then fit.

    The epoch budget is a hyperparameter. Every accuracy in this file is at 300 epochs with learning rate \(0.01\). The depth study shows that the ordering can flip with the budget, and a hand-written loop that never plots the loss will report whichever number the stopping rule happened to land on.

    A minibatch that is smaller than the class count. With batch size 64 over three classes the gradient is well conditioned. At batch size 4 a minibatch can miss a class entirely, the softmax gradient becomes nearly one-hot, and the effective learning rate spikes. The last partial batch is the usual culprit when a run diverges at the end of an epoch.

    Applications #

    The MLP is the tabular baseline. On most tabular problems a two-layer network with standardised inputs and a tuned width is within a fraction of a point of gradient-boosted trees, and it is the model to beat before reaching for anything more elaborate.

    The gated and SwiGLU blocks are the feed-forward half of every transformer. The attention layer mixes across positions; the gated MLP mixes across features, and the gate is what lets a feature be switched on or off depending on the context in the residual stream.

    The residual and pre-norm pattern is what makes depth possible at all. Every modern deep network uses it, and the reason is (5): the identity path is the only part of the network that is guaranteed to have a well-conditioned gradient at any depth.

    The affine layer alone is the right model when the problem really is linear — small \(n\), few samples, or a strong prior that the effect is additive. The demo's linear variant is a reminder that "neural" is not a synonym for "better", and that the first thing to check on a new dataset is whether the simple model is already at the ceiling.
