    12 · Neural blocks/tier 1/10 variants/source

    Convolution — one engine, ten variants, and its exact adjoint

    Intuition #

    A convolution is a dot product applied at every position of a signal, with the same weights reused at each position. That weight sharing is the whole idea. It buys two things at once: the parameter count stops depending on the input length, and the operation commutes with translation, so a feature learned at one offset is recognised at every other offset.

    Everything in this file is one scan. The kernel slides over the input, a kernel-sized window is flattened to a vector, and that vector is multiplied by a matrix of flattened kernels. The variants are switches on that scan:

    • how many spatial axes are being scanned — one, two or three;
    • how the kernel steps over them — stride, dilation, and where it is allowed to read past the border;
    • how the channels are wired — dense, grouped, depthwise, or depthwise then pointwise;
    • whether the scan runs forwards or backwards — transposed is the exact adjoint of the forward scan, and it is what the backward pass calls.

    The demo establishes each switch with a property rather than a picture. A delta into a kernel returns the kernel's taps. A box kernel reproduces numpy.convolve. The transpose satisfies \(\langle \mathrm{conv}(x), y\rangle = \langle x, \mathrm{conv}^{\top}(y)\rangle\) to machine precision — both sides came out at \(15.220855\). Grouped convolutions equal concatenated smaller ones. Causal padding provably cannot see the future: perturbing steps 20 onward leaves every earlier output bit-identical, and the change first appears exactly at step 20. Circular padding commutes with rolling. And the hand-written backward pass matches finite differences on all 21 probes to \(1.49\times10^{-9}\).

    Variants #

    variant what it changes kernel shape
    conv1d one spatial axis (out, in, k)
    conv2d two spatial axes (out, in, kh, kw)
    conv3d three spatial axes (out, in, kd, kh, kw)
    dilated spaces kernel taps apart same as the rank
    transposed runs the adjoint scan (out, in, *k)
    depthwise one kernel per channel (in, 1, *k)
    separable depthwise, then pointwise a pair of the two
    group independent channel blocks (out, in/g, *k)
    causal pads only the past (out, in, k)
    circular_pad wraps instead of zeroing same as the rank

    conv1d, conv2d, conv3d are the same function with a rank check. The engine already works in \(n\) dimensions, so these exist to make the caller's intent checkable: a 2-D kernel handed to conv1d is a bug, not a broadcast.

    dilated multiplies the tap spacing by \(d\). A width-3 kernel with \(d=2\) sees five input positions and still has three weights. It refuses \(d=1\), because that is the plain case and silently accepting it hides a configuration mistake.

    transposed is not an inverse. It is the adjoint of the valid forward convolution: it scatters each output value back onto the inputs that produced it. That is exactly the operation the backward pass needs, and the demo checks the defining inner-product identity.

    depthwise gives every input channel its own kernel and no mixing. It is the grouped case with \(G=C_{\mathrm{in}}\), and it refuses a kernel whose output count does not equal the channel count.

    separable is depthwise followed by a \(1\times1\) convolution, which is the MobileNet block. At the demo's shapes a dense layer needs \(5\cdot6\cdot3=90\) weights and the split form needs \(6\cdot3 + 5\cdot6=48\), or \(0.53\) of the cost.

    group splits the channels into \(G\) blocks that never talk to each other. The demo verifies the definition literally: three groups of two channels equal three separate two-channel convolutions, concatenated.

    causal pads \(d(k-1)\) zeros on the left and none on the right, so the output at step \(t\) reads only inputs at steps \(\le t\). It is one-dimensional by construction, since "the past" is not a meaningful direction in an image.

    circular_pad wraps the border, which makes the scan commute with cyclic rolls. That is the right choice for genuinely periodic signals — angles, phase, a torus — and the wrong one for an image, where the top edge would be blended into the bottom.

    Derivation #

    The scan #

    For input \(x\in\mathbb R^{B\times n_1\times\cdots\times n_D\times C}\), kernel \(w\in\mathbb R^{O\times C\times k_1\times\cdots\times k_D}\), stride \(s\) and dilation \(d\),

    \[y_{b,o,\mathbf{p}} =\sum_{c}\sum_{\mathbf{k}} w_{o,c,\mathbf{k}}\; x_{b,\,c,\,\mathbf{p}\odot s+\mathbf{k}\odot d}+b_o . \qquad (1)\]

    The sum over \(\mathbf k\) runs over the kernel's taps and \(\odot\) is elementwise. This is cross-correlation, not the textbook convolution with a flipped kernel. Every deep learning framework calls it convolution anyway, and the distinction only matters when you compare against a signal-processing library or read a proof about commutativity.

    Output size #

    With \(p_{\mathrm b}\) zeros before and \(p_{\mathrm a}\) after on some axis,

    \[n_{\mathrm{out}} =\left\lfloor \frac{n+p_{\mathrm b}+p_{\mathrm a}-d(k-1)-1}{s} \right\rfloor+1 . \qquad (2)\]

    The quantity \(d(k-1)\) is the kernel's reach, the distance from its first tap to its last. The demo's nine-by-nine input with a \(3\times3\) kernel at \(d=2\) has reach four, so the valid output is \(9-4=5\) wide, and the same padding prepends two and appends two to keep the length at nine.

    Padding #

    The four modes are one table:

    mode before after length at \(s=1\)
    valid 0 0 \(n-d(k-1)\)
    same \(\lfloor r/2\rfloor\) \(r-\lfloor r/2\rfloor\) \(n\)
    causal \(r\) 0 \(n\)
    full \(r\) \(r\) \(n+r\)

    with \(r=d(k-1)\). same splits the reach as evenly as it can, which is exact for odd kernels and off by one for even ones — an even kernel cannot be centred on a sample, so the output is shifted half a sample. That asymmetry is a real property of the padding, not a rounding bug, and it is why vision models almost always use odd kernels.

    The adjoint #

    Let \(A\) be the linear map \(x\mapsto \mathrm{conv}(x,w)\). Its adjoint \(A^{\top}\) is defined by

    \[\langle Ax,\,y\rangle=\langle x,\,A^{\top}y\rangle \quad\text{for all }x,y . \qquad (3)\]

    Written out, the adjoint scatters each output value onto the inputs it read:

    \[\bigl(A^{\top}y\bigr)_{c,\mathbf{q}} =\sum_{o}\sum_{\mathbf{k}} w_{o,c,\mathbf{k}}\; y_{o,\,(\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{k}\odot d)/s}, \qquad (4)\]

    where a term is present only when every coordinate of \((\mathbf q-\mathbf k\odot d)\) is divisible by \(s\) and lands in range. The demo computes both sides of (3) for random arrays and gets the same float twice. This identity is the reason a convolution layer needs no separate backward implementation: the gradient with respect to the input is the adjoint.

    Weight gradient #

    For upstream gradient \(g=\partial L/\partial y\),

    \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{o,c,\mathbf{k}}} =\sum_{b}\sum_{\mathbf{p}} g_{b,o,\mathbf{p}}\; x_{b,\,c,\,\mathbf{p}\odot s+\mathbf{k}\odot d}, \qquad (5)\]

    which is the forward scan again, with the upstream gradient in place of the kernel and the input's windows in place of the input. The implementation materialises the windows once and contracts them against \(g\) with a single tensordot.

    Groups #

    Split the \(C\) input channels and \(O\) output channels into \(G\) blocks of \(C/G\) and \(O/G\). Each output block reads only its own input block, so

    \[P_{\mathrm{group}} =G\cdot\frac{O}{G}\cdot\frac{C}{G}\cdot\prod_i k_i =\frac{OC}{G}\prod_i k_i . \qquad (6)\]

    \(G=1\) is the dense case and \(G=C=O\) is depthwise. The demo's three groups of two channels over six inputs and six outputs give \(6\cdot2\cdot3=36\) weights against \(6\cdot6\cdot3=108\) dense, and match the three independent scans exactly.

    Separable #

    Depthwise convolution followed by a pointwise one is

    \[P_{\mathrm{sep}} =C\prod_i k_i \;+\; CO . \qquad (7)\]

    The first term mixes space, the second mixes channels, and the factorisation assumes the two are separable — an assumption about the data, not a theorem. At kernel \(3\), \(C=6\), \(O=5\) that is \(18+30=48\) against \(90\). The saving grows with the kernel: at \(k=7\) a dense layer costs \(49CO\) and the split one \(7C+CO\), which tends to \(1/7\) of the cost once \(C\) is much larger than \(k\).

    Dilation and receptive field #

    A stack of \(L\) convolutions with kernel \(k_\ell\) and dilation \(d_\ell\) sees

    \[R_L = 1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L} d_\ell\,(k_\ell-1) \qquad (8)\]

    input positions. With \(k=2\) and \(d=1,2,4,\dots,2^{L-1}\) the reach is \(2^{L}\), so \(L\) layers cover \(2^L\) steps with \(2L\) weights per channel. That exponential reach at linear parameter cost is why dilated causal convolutions replaced recurrent networks for long audio.

    A dilated kernel with \(k\) taps has the same support as a dense kernel of width \(d(k-1)+1\) with zeros between the taps. It is a sparse large kernel, not a compressed one: the missing positions are not interpolated, they are skipped.

    Cyclic and causal structure #

    Circular padding makes the input an element of the group \(\mathbb Z_{n_1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb Z_{n_D}\). The scan then commutes with translation on that group,

    \[T_\tau\bigl(\mathrm{conv}(x)\bigr) =\mathrm{conv}\bigl(T_\tau x\bigr), \qquad (9)\]

    which the demo checks by rolling the input four steps and confirming the output rolls four steps with no seam. Zero padding has no such identity: a shifted input produces a differently-clipped output.

    Causal padding gives the one-sided analogue. With \(p_{\mathrm b}=d(k-1)\) and \(p_{\mathrm a}=0\), the output at \(t\) satisfies

    \[y_t = f\bigl(x_{t-d(k-1)},\dots,x_t\bigr), \qquad (10)\]

    so no future input can influence it. Stacking such layers preserves the property, which is what makes a temporal convolutional network usable for forecasting at all.

    Transposed convolution and stride #

    The adjoint of a stride-\(s\) scan is a fractionally strided convolution: each output sample of the adjoint corresponds to a position of the original grid, and the \(s-1\) positions in between receive nothing from that sample. In practice this is implemented by inserting \(s-1\) zeros between the inputs and then running a stride-1 scan — the same scatter as (4), written differently. It is an upsampling operator with learned weights, not an inverse: applying \(\mathrm{conv}^{\top}\) after \(\mathrm{conv}\) smooths and mixes, and does not recover the original signal.

    Code walkthrough #

    Padding is a single table driven by the kernel's reach:

        reach = dilation * (kernel - 1)
    if mode == "valid":
        return 0, 0
    if mode == "same":
        before = reach // 2
        return before, reach - before
    if mode == "causal":
        return reach, 0
    if mode == "full":
        return reach, reach

    The floor division in same is the even-kernel asymmetry from the table above. Writing reach // 2 on both sides would lose a column for odd reach; the second term is reach - before for exactly that reason.

    The window extraction is the honest, slow version of what a GPU does:

        for index in np.ndindex(*shape):
        window = tuple(slice(start * stride[axis],
                             start * stride[axis]
                             + dilation[axis] * (kernel[axis] - 1) + 1,
                             dilation[axis])
                       for axis, start in enumerate(index))
        windows[(slice(None), *index)] = np.moveaxis(
            x[(slice(None), *window)], -1, 1)

    A slice with step dilation is the dilated tap pattern, and stride multiplies the starting offset. The moveaxis puts channels before the kernel axes so that a window's memory layout matches the flattened kernel. The loop over output positions is why this file is a reference implementation and not a fast one.

    The scan itself is one contraction over three of the four index groups:

        flat = windows.reshape(*windows.shape[:dims + 1], channels, width)
    blocks = flat.reshape(*flat.shape[:dims + 1], groups, channels // groups,
                          width)
    kernels = weight.reshape(groups, weight.shape[0] // groups,
                             channels // groups, width)
    out = np.einsum("...gck,gock->...go", blocks, kernels)
    return out.reshape(*out.shape[:dims + 1], weight.shape[0])

    Grouping is a reshape, not a loop: both the windows and the kernels are split along a new g axis, and einsum refuses to sum across it. That is why the grouped, depthwise and dense cases are the same code path with a different groups value. The ... covers batch and every output position at once.

    The adjoint is a scatter-add:

            piece = np.einsum("bo,oc...->bc...", y[(slice(None), *index)], weight)
        out[(slice(None), *window)] += piece.transpose(0, *spread, 1)

    piece is the contribution of one output position to the input channels, placed at the window's offsets. The overlapping windows mean many positions add into the same input cell, which is why this is += and why the buffer is zero-initialised. Getting the transpose axes wrong here is the classic bug: the output is (batch, out_channels), the kernel is (out, in, *k), and the result must be (batch, in_channels, *k_window).

    The weight gradient reuses the same windows:

        windows = _patches(x, kernel, stride, dilation)
    flat = windows.reshape(*windows.shape[:dims + 1], x.shape[-1], -1)
    axes = list(range(dims + 1))
    d_weight = np.tensordot(flat, upstream, axes=(axes, axes))
    d_weight = d_weight.transpose(2, 0, 1)
    d_input = transpose(upstream, weight, stride=stride, dilation=dilation)

    tensordot sums over batch and every output position, leaving (channels, kernel_volume, out_channels), and the final transpose puts it back into the kernel's layout. d_input is literally the call to transpose: equation (4) is the backward pass, so there is nothing to re-derive.

    The depthwise guard is where the variant earns its name:

            channels = x.shape[-1]
        if np.shape(weight)[0] != channels or np.shape(weight)[1] != 1:
            raise ValueError("depthwise needs one kernel per input channel")
        return convolve(x, weight, stride=stride, dilation=dilation,
                        groups=channels, padding=padding)

    One kernel per channel means an output-channel count equal to the input-channel count and an input-channel count of one inside each group. Both conditions are checked, because groups=channels alone would accept a mismatched kernel and then reshape it into something silently wrong.

    Separable is two calls, and this is the whole implementation:

            depth, point = weight
        mixed = convolve(x, depth, stride=stride, dilation=dilation,
                         groups=x.shape[-1], padding=padding)
        return convolve(mixed, point, padding="same")

    The unpacking doubles as the type check: a single array cannot be unpacked into two, so passing a dense kernel to separable fails before any arithmetic. Stride and dilation belong to the depthwise stage; the pointwise stage is a \(1\times1\) scan and has no space to stride through.

    Causal padding is one line in the padding table, but it needs a rank guard:

        if variant == "causal":
        if x.ndim != 3:
            raise ValueError("causal is a one-dimensional padding mode")
        return convolve(x, weight, stride=stride, dilation=dilation,
                        groups=groups, padding="causal")

    Without the guard, a 2-D call would silently pad only the first spatial axis and produce a filter that sees half the future. The demo's check is behavioural rather than structural: it adds \(100\) to every step from 20 onward and asserts that outputs 0 through 19 are bit-identical, then that the first difference lands at step 20.

    Circular padding is a flag on the padding helper:

    def _pad(x: np.ndarray, pads, wrap: bool) -> np.ndarray:
    widths = [(0, 0), *pads, (0, 0)]
    if wrap:
        return np.pad(x, widths, mode="wrap")
    return np.pad(x, widths)

    mode="wrap" repeats the signal cyclically, which is exactly the group structure of (9). The batch and channel axes get zero-width pads so the wrap applies only to space; using np.pad(..., mode="wrap") without the explicit widths would wrap the batch dimension too.

    Complexity #

    For batch \(B\), \(D\) spatial axes, \(C\) input channels, \(O\) output channels, kernel volume \(K=\prod_i k_i\) and output volume \(N=\prod_i n_{\mathrm{out},i}\):

    • Arithmetic: \(O(B\,N\,O\,C\,K/G)\) multiply-adds, the same count for the backward pass. This is the number a hardware spec quotes.
    • Window materialisation: \(O(B\,N\,C\,K)\) floats of temporary storage. For a \(3\times3\) kernel and 256 channels that is nine times the input, which is why real implementations tile the scan instead of materialising it.
    • Python overhead: the demo loops over \(N\) output positions, so the wall clock is dominated by the interpreter rather than the arithmetic. A vectorised as_strided view removes the loop but not the memory cost.
    • Grouped and depthwise: the arithmetic divides by \(G\), but the materialised windows do not, so the memory saving is smaller than the FLOP saving. This is the usual reason depthwise layers are memory-bound on accelerators.
    • Parameter count: \(OCK/G + O\) with bias, from (6); the demo's separable example is \(48\) against \(90\).
    • Receptive field: \(1+\sum_\ell d_\ell(k_\ell-1)\) from (8), independent of channel count.

    Failure modes #

    same is not centred for even kernels. With \(k=2\) the reach is one, so before=0 and after=1: the output is shifted half a sample relative to the input. Stacking several such layers drifts the feature map, and the drift is easy to mistake for a data alignment bug.

    Dilation wider than the input is an error, not a zero. The engine raises when the padded input is shorter than the kernel's reach. Some frameworks silently return an empty or zero output, which turns a configuration mistake into a model that trains to chance.

    Stride destroys shift equivariance. A stride-\(s\) scan samples the input at \(s\) phases, so a one-pixel shift of the input is not a one-pixel shift of the output unless the shift is a multiple of \(s\). This is aliasing, and it is why detection models often use stride 1 in the stem and pool afterwards.

    The transpose is not an inverse. transpose(convolve(x, w), w) is a correlation of a correlation: it smooths and mixes channels. It does not recover x, and a decoder built on the assumption that it does will produce blurry reconstructions that no amount of training fixes.

    Circular padding wraps the wrong thing. On a periodic signal it is exact; on an image it blends the top row into the bottom. The demo's roll identity holds precisely because the data is treated as cyclic, so it is a check of the implementation, not evidence that wrapping is right for your problem.

    Zero padding creates a border artefact. The output near an edge is computed from fewer real inputs than the interior, so the effective function changes with position. With full padding the artefact moves outside the original support, which is why full is the right mode for analysis and same for stacks.

    Grouped convolutions permute channels. Group \(g\) of the output reads only group \(g\) of the input, so a channel permutation applied to the input is not undone by the layer. Weight initialisation and channel order therefore matter more than in the dense case, and a shuffled-channel bug can survive several layers before it shows.

    The demo's kernel has no bias. convolve is the linear map only. A real layer adds \(b_o\) per output channel, which is \(O\) parameters and one broadcast; leaving it out keeps the adjoint identity clean, since a bias would not be part of the linear operator.

    Applications #

    Convolution is the vision backbone. Every image classifier from LeNet through ResNet is a stack of these scans with a nonlinearity and a normalisation between them, and the variants here are the standard blocks: \(3\times3\) dense for accuracy, depthwise separable for mobile budgets, grouped for ResNeXt-style width, dilated for segmentation without losing resolution.

    The causal form is the time-series workhorse. WaveNet and the temporal convolutional network replace recurrence with a stack of dilated causal convolutions, which parallelise over time and have a fixed receptive field instead of a vanishing gradient.

    The transposed form is the decoder half of every autoencoder, GAN generator and segmentation network. It is also the operation inside gradient-based attribution methods, where the adjoint of the forward pass is exactly what is needed to map a gradient at the output back onto the input.

    Circular padding shows up wherever the signal really is periodic: angle regression, phase estimation, crystallography, and any model over a torus. Separable and depthwise convolutions are the default on phones, and grouped convolutions are how mixture-of-experts ideas first entered vision.
