    Dropout family — noise that is scaled to disappear at inference

    Intuition #

    Dropout multiplies activations by random noise during training and does nothing at inference. The noise forces a unit to work without its neighbours, which breaks up co-adaptation; the scaling is what makes the two regimes agree. With inverted dropout the surviving units are divided by the keep probability, so the expected activation is unchanged and inference needs no correction. Every variant in this file shares that contract:

    \[\text{training: } y=x\odot\text{noise},\qquad \text{inference: } y=x . \qquad (1)\]

    What changes is the shape of the noise. Independent elementwise masks are the default; spatial masks drop whole feature maps; variational noise is fixed across time; dropout1d drops timesteps; drop-path drops a residual branch; stochastic depth makes the drop rate grow with depth; alpha dropout replaces the mask with a shift-and-scale that preserves the first two moments of a standardized input. The demo measures each contract on synthetic data rather than trusting it.

    The demo also shows the distinction that matters most in practice. Inverted dropout leaves the mean alone but inflates the variance to \(p/(1-p)\) times the activation; alpha dropout leaves both mean and variance alone, at the cost of assuming the input is already standardized. On the same \(50000\times8\) standard-normal batch, standard dropout has variance 1.3288 and alpha dropout 0.9965.

    Variants #

    variant mask or noise shared over rate
    standard Bernoulli, elementwise nothing rate
    spatial Bernoulli per channel spatial axes, per sample rate
    gaussian Gaussian, mean one nothing sigma
    variational Gaussian, mean one time, per sample and feature sigma
    dropout1d Bernoulli per timestep features, per sample rate
    droppath Bernoulli per sample the whole branch rate
    stochastic_depth Bernoulli per sample the whole branch depth schedule
    alpha_dropout affine shift and scale nothing rate

    standard is the original: a fresh Bernoulli mask for every element, with survivors scaled by \(1/(1-r)\).

    spatial draws one mask per sample and channel and broadcasts it over the spatial axes, so a feature map is either kept whole or zeroed whole. It is the usual regulariser for convolutional trunks.

    gaussian multiplies by \(1+\sigma\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\). It never produces an exact zero and has no keep probability, but its mean is still one.

    variational uses the same Gaussian noise at every time step of a sequence, one noise per sample and feature. That is the fixed-noise reading of variational dropout for recurrent nets, and it is what makes the regulariser consistent along the sequence.

    dropout1d drops entire time steps: one Bernoulli mask per sample and step, shared across features. It removes a frame rather than a unit, which suits sequence and audio models. (PyTorch's Dropout1d drops channels instead; that is spatial in this file.)

    droppath applies the mask to a whole residual branch: the output is the residual plus either the scaled branch or nothing. It is the residual-network regulariser from the stochastic-depth paper.

    stochastic_depth is drop-path with a schedule. At depth \(\ell\) of \(L\) blocks the rate is \(r_\ell=r_0(\ell+1)/L\), so early blocks are almost always kept and late blocks are regularised hardest.

    alpha_dropout replaces dropped values by a constant and rescales the kept ones so that a zero-mean unit-variance input keeps mean zero and variance one. It is designed for SELU networks, whose self-normalizing property needs exactly those moments.

    Derivation #

    Standard dropout #

    Let \(K\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)\) with \(p=1-r\), independent per element. Inverted dropout is

    \[y=x\,\frac{K}{p}, \qquad \mathbb E[y]=x, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(y)=x^2\Bigl(\frac1p-1\Bigr)=x^2\frac{r}{1-r}. \qquad (2)\]

    The mean identity is exact. The variance grows without bound as \(p\to0\), which is why a rate near one destroys the signal even though the expectation is formally preserved. For a random input \(X\) independent of \(K\),

    \[\mathrm{Var}(y)=\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X)}{p} +\mathbb E[X]^2\Bigl(\frac1p-1\Bigr), \qquad (3)\]

    so a mean-zero activation has its variance inflated by \(1/p\). In the demo, \(x=1\) and \(r=0.25\) give a measured mean of 0.9983 and variance 0.3345 against the predicted \(1/3\).

    Spatial dropout #

    For an image tensor of shape \((n,h,w,c)\) the mask has shape \((n,1,1,c)\):

    \[m_{n:\,:\,c}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p), \qquad y_{nhwc}=x_{nhwc}\frac{m_{n:\,:\,c}}{p}. \qquad (4)\]

    Every pixel of a surviving channel is scaled by the same factor, so the regulariser removes a feature detector rather than a location. The demo keeps 16 of 24 maps and checks that each is either all zero or exactly \(x/0.5\) everywhere.

    Gaussian and variational noise #

    Independent Gaussian dropout uses

    \[y=x\,(1+\sigma\varepsilon), \qquad \mathbb E[y]=x, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(y)=x^2\sigma^2 . \qquad (5)\]

    The variational variant draws one \(\varepsilon\) per sample and feature and broadcasts it over the middle axes:

    \[\varepsilon_{n:\,:\,c}\sim\mathcal N(0,1), \qquad y_{n t c}=x_{n t c}(1+\sigma\varepsilon_{n:\,:\,c}). \qquad (6)\]

    The same unit is therefore noisy in the same direction at every step, which is the behaviour a recurrent model needs; fresh noise per step would average out along the sequence. The demo uses five sequences of seven steps and six features and checks that the ratio \(y/x\) is constant along time.

    Dropout1d #

    With one mask per sample and time step,

    \[m_{nt}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p), \qquad y_{ntc}=x_{ntc}\frac{m_{nt}}{p}. \qquad (7)\]

    This drops a frame from every feature at once. The demo drops 16 of 35 steps and verifies that the zero pattern is constant along the feature axis.

    Drop path and stochastic depth #

    A residual branch is either kept or skipped per sample:

    \[y=r+x\,\frac{K}{p}, \qquad K\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p). \qquad (8)\]

    Dropping the branch leaves the identity path untouched, which is why the gradient of a dropped sample is exactly zero and the residual still carries information. With a schedule \(r_\ell=r_0(\ell+1)/L\) at depth \(\ell=0,\dots,L-1\) the rate at the last block is \(r_0\) and the first is \(r_0/L\). The demo uses \(r_0=0.5\) and \(L=6\), giving rates

    \[0.083,\ 0.167,\ 0.250,\ 0.333,\ 0.417,\ 0.500, \qquad (9)\]

    and measured drop fractions 0.066 to 0.494 over 2000 samples. The measured value tracks the nominal rate because each sample is one Bernoulli draw.

    Alpha dropout #

    Let \(\alpha'=-\alpha\lambda\) be the SELU dropout constant, with \(\alpha=1.6733\) and \(\lambda=1.0507\). The layer is

    \[y=a\bigl(xK+\alpha'(1-K)\bigr)+b, \qquad K\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p), \qquad (10)\]

    with

    \[a=\bigl(p(1+r\alpha'^2)\bigr)^{-1/2}, \qquad b=-a\,\alpha'\,r . \qquad (11)\]

    For an input with mean zero and variance one, the shift cancels the mean of the dropped branch:

    \[\mathbb E[y]=a\,p\,\mathbb E[x]+a\,\alpha' r+b=0. \qquad (12)\]

    For the variance, write \(c=\mathbb E[y]=0\). Since \(\mathbb E[(xK+\alpha'(1-K))^2]=p+r\alpha'^2\) and \(\mathbb E[xK+\alpha'(1-K)]=\alpha' r\),

    \[\mathrm{Var}(y) =a^2\bigl(p+r\alpha'^2\bigr)+2ab\,\alpha'r+b^2 =a^2p\bigl(1+r\alpha'^2\bigr)=1, \qquad (13)\]

    using \(b=-a\alpha'r\). The demo checks (13) numerically and in closed form, and finds mean \(-0.0016\) and variance 0.9965 on a standard-normal batch. If the input has nonzero mean the identity no longer holds: the output mean is \(ap\mu\), not \(\mu\). That is not a bug; SELU networks are standardized before every layer.

    Inference #

    Every non-path variant returns \(x\) unchanged when training=False, and the path variants return \(r+x\). There is no keep-probability correction at inference because the inverted scaling already put it in training. A rate of zero is the identity in both regimes, which the demo checks for all eight variants.

    Code walkthrough #

    Standard and alpha dropout share the mask but not the arithmetic. Alpha's learning signal is the affine map kept * a, so its backward pass differs by one constant:

                mask = self.rng.random(x.shape) < keep
            kept = mask.astype(float)
            if self.variant == "alpha_dropout":
                alpha_p = -self.alpha * self.scale
                a = (keep * (1.0 + (1.0 - keep) * alpha_p ** 2)) ** -0.5
                b = -a * alpha_p * (1.0 - keep)
                output = x * kept * a + alpha_p * (1.0 - kept) * a + b
                scale = kept * a
                cache.update(scale=scale, a=a, b=b, alpha_p=alpha_p)
                return output, cache
            scale = kept / keep
            cache["scale"] = scale
            return x * scale, cache

    The broadcast shapes are the whole trick behind the structured variants. One helper for channel masks and one for time masks cover spatial, variational and dropout1d without a second implementation:

        def _channel_shape(self, x: np.ndarray) -> tuple[int, ...]:
        """One mask entry per sample and channel, broadcast over space."""
        return (x.shape[0],) + (1,) * (x.ndim - 2) + (x.shape[-1],)

    Spatial dropout is the channel helper plus a Bernoulli draw. Note that the mask is generated on the small shape, not on the full tensor, so the memory cost is one number per feature map:

            if self.variant == "spatial":
            if x.ndim < 2:
                raise ValueError("spatial needs a sample and channel axis")
            shape = self._channel_shape(x)
            mask = self.rng.random(shape) < keep
            scale = mask.astype(float) / keep
            cache["scale"] = scale
            return x * scale, cache

    Gaussian and variational dropout share the noise formula; only the shape of the draw differs. The cache keeps the noise because the backward pass is linear in it:

            if self.variant == "gaussian":
            noise = 1.0 + self.sigma * self.rng.standard_normal(x.shape)
            cache["noise"] = noise
            return x * noise, cache

            if self.variant == "variational":
            if x.ndim < 2:
                raise ValueError("variational needs a sample and feature axis")
            shape = self._channel_shape(x)
            noise = 1.0 + self.sigma * self.rng.standard_normal(shape)
            cache["noise"] = noise
            return x * noise, cache

    Dropout1d uses the other helper and a fresh Bernoulli mask per step:

            if self.variant == "dropout1d":
            if x.ndim < 2:
                raise ValueError("dropout1d needs a sample and time axis")
            shape = self._time_shape(x)
            mask = self.rng.random(shape) < keep
            scale = mask.astype(float) / keep
            cache["scale"] = scale
            return x * scale, cache

    The path variants are the same mask at sample granularity, added back to the residual. A dropped sample returns the residual exactly, because multiplying by a zero mask gives an exact zero before the addition:

            shape = self._sample_shape(x)
        mask = self.rng.random(shape) < keep
        scale = mask.astype(float) / keep
        cache.update(scale=scale, rate=1.0 - keep)
        return residual + x * scale, cache

    Stochastic depth's only extra machinery is the schedule. It is computed at forward time from the depth, so the same object can be reused at every block:

        def _rate_at(self, depth: int | None) -> float:
        if self.variant != "stochastic_depth":
            return self.rate
        level = self.depth if depth is None else depth
        if level < 0 or level >= self.max_depth:
            raise ValueError("depth must be in [0, max_depth)")
        return self.rate * (level + 1) / self.max_depth

    Backward is one multiply by the cached mask or noise, and the identity at inference:

        def backward(self, gradient: np.ndarray, cache) -> np.ndarray:
        gradient = np.asarray(gradient, dtype=float)
        if cache.get("eval"):
            return gradient
        if "noise" in cache:
            return gradient * cache["noise"]
        return gradient * cache["scale"]

    Complexity #

    For \(n\) elements:

    • Standard, gaussian, alpha: one random draw and one or two elementwise operations per element, \(O(n)\) time and one mask's worth of cache.
    • Spatial: \(O(n/c)\) draws for \(c\) channels and an \(O(n)\) broadcast multiply. The mask is one number per sample and channel.
    • Variational and dropout1d: the same broadcast pattern over time rather than space, with a mask one axis smaller than the tensor.
    • Path variants: one draw per sample and one multiply of the branch, so the cost is \(O(n)\) in the branch and free in the residual.
    • Parameters: zero. The only state is the random generator, which is why seeding is part of reproducibility.
    • The demo: masks over at most 40000 elements and one 50000-row moment check; every section is deterministic under its seed and the whole file runs in a fraction of a second.

    Failure modes #

    Train and inference diverge if the scaling is left out. Without the \(1/p\) factor, training produces activations \(p\) times too small, and inference is then a sudden rescaling. Inverted dropout is the fix, not a detail.

    High rates destroy the signal. The expectation is preserved for any \(p>0\), but the variance in (3) grows like \(1/p\). A rate of 0.9 turns a well-conditioned layer into noise with ten times the variance; the guard refuses \(r=1\) because the scale would be infinite.

    Dropping before batch norm leaks the training distribution. If dropout is applied before a batch-norm layer, the batch statistics are computed on the noisy tensor while inference sees the clean one. The usual placement is after the norm and before the activation, or on the residual branch rather than the identity path.

    Spatial masks are easy to broadcast into nothing. A mask shaped \((n,c)\) on a tensor \((n,h,w,c)\) either fails or broadcasts along the wrong axis. The helper builds \((n,1,1,c)\) explicitly; the demo asserts that the zero pattern is constant across space.

    Variational noise that is redrawn per step is just Gaussian dropout. The point of the variational variant is the shared draw. If the mask changes at every timestep the regulariser averages out along the sequence and the recurrent interpretation is lost.

    A path variant without a residual is a zero layer. Dropping the branch then leaves nothing, and the gradient of a dropped sample is zero. The constructor requires residual, and the demo checks that the surviving samples are exactly \(r+2x\) at rate 0.5.

    Depth indexing is off by one. A schedule written as \(\ell/L\) instead of \((\ell+1)/L\) never reaches the maximum rate, and one written as \(\ell/(L-1)\) divides by zero at \(L=1\). The guard keeps depth inside [0, max_depth) and the demo prints the six rates.

    Alpha dropout assumes standardized inputs. It preserves zero mean and unit variance, not an arbitrary mean. Applying it after a layer with a large bias shifts the output by \(ap\mu\) and breaks the self-normalizing argument. It also uses the SELU constants, so pairing it with ReLU is a different (and unjustified) regulariser.

    Alpha's dropped value is a constant, not zero. The dropped branch becomes \(\alpha'a+b\), which is nonzero; code that assumes zeros will misread the sparsity. The demo checks that every dropped entry equals that constant.

    Gaussian noise has no sparsity. It regularises by variance, not by removing units, so it does not give the sparse-activation effect of a Bernoulli mask. Large \(\sigma\) also compounds multiplicatively through depth.

    Dropout in evaluation inflates the variance. Forgetting to switch to inference mode leaves the model noisy and, for spatial or path variants, randomly zeroes whole feature maps. The demo's training=False checks are the contract every caller depends on.

    Randomness must be seeded per run, not per layer. Two layers seeded with the same value share a mask, which correlates their noise. The demo seeds each model independently and relies on that for its printed counts.

    Applications #

    Standard dropout is still used on transformer attention weights and feed-forward activations, and on the classifier head of almost every vision model. Spatial dropout is common in convolutional trunks where whole filters should be regularised; dropout1d and variational dropout target sequence and recurrent models.

    Drop-path and stochastic depth are standard in residual networks and vision transformers, where dropping a whole block is cheaper than dropping units and matches the identity-path argument. Alpha dropout belongs with SELU and self-normalizing networks, and Gaussian dropout appears in variational inference and as a smooth alternative to Bernoulli masks.
