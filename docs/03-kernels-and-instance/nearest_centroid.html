    Nearest centroid — the classifier you can fit with an average

    Intuition #

    Take the average of each class and send a new point to the nearest average. That is the whole model, it costs one pass over the training set, and it stores \(K\) vectors instead of \(n\):

           x₂
        │        class 1
        │       ·  ·  ·
        │      ·  ·⊕·  ·        ⊕ = class mean, c₁
        │       ·  ·  ·
        │            ╲
        │             ╲  the boundary is the perpendicular bisector
        │              ╲
        │   ·  ·  ·     ╲
        │  ·  ·⊕·  ·     ╲      ⊕ = class mean, c₀
        │   ·  ·  ·       ╲
        └──────────────────────── x₁

    The decision boundary is a straight line, always, because the rule compares two squared distances and their difference is linear in \(x\). It is therefore the cheapest linear classifier there is — cheaper than logistic regression and cheaper than a linear SVM, with no iterations and no margin parameter.

    The rule is not a heuristic. If every class is a Gaussian with the same covariance matrix, the Bayes-optimal classifier is a linear function of the distance to the mean, and when that covariance is the identity the optimal rule is exactly "nearest mean". Nearest centroid is linear discriminant analysis with the covariance assumed away, which explains both why it is so cheap and where it fails: a class that is a banana, or two blobs, has a mean that is nowhere near its own points.

    The three other variants are the repairs, and each repairs a different lie:

    • shrunken repairs the lie that every feature is informative. It standardises each feature by its within-class spread and soft-thresholds the class means towards the overall mean, so a feature whose class means are within noise is set to the same value in every class and stops voting. This is the nearest shrunken centroid of Tibshirani and coauthors, and it is why the method worked on gene expression data with twenty thousand columns and sixty rows.
    • nearest_subclass repairs the lie that each class is one blob. It replaces a class by a small k-means mixture, so a class made of two clusters gets two centroids and the average stops lying.
    • cosine repairs the lie that distance is the right comparison. When the length of a vector is noise — document length, exposure time, embedding norm — the angle is the signal and the Euclidean distance is not.

    The demo is a sequence of failure and repair. On three separated blobs the plain rule is right 92.8 % of the time and a 1-nearest-neighbour baseline is at 90.5 %, because one estimated mean has less variance than four hundred stored points. On a class made of two blobs the plain rule falls to 0.502 — a coin flip, and the centroids are both at the origin — while two subclasses reach 1.000. On 600 features with 30 training points, shrinkage lifts held-out accuracy from 0.929 to 0.993 while the retained set falls from 600 features to 11, and then, once the threshold passes the signal, the model collapses to the majority class, which is the correct behaviour of a rule that has been told every feature is noise. On documents of random length, Euclidean distance gets 0.890 and cosine gets 1.000.

    Variants #

    variant replaces parameter fixes
    standard — — the baseline
    shrunken the mean by a thresholded mean shrinkage uninformative features
    nearest_subclass one mean by \(k\) means n_subclasses multimodal classes
    cosine the distance by the angle — magnitude as noise 
    graph TD
  A[class mean c_k] --> B[standard: argmin distance]
  A --> C[shrunken: threshold each feature]
  A --> D[nearest_subclass: k-means per class]
  A --> E[cosine: argmax angle]
  C --> F[feature selection]
  D --> G[mixture of centroids]
  E --> H[length-invariant match]

    standard is the rule in the title: one mean per class, squared Euclidean distance, no parameters. Its decision_function returns the negative squared distance per class, so larger is better and predict is an argmax.

    shrunken is the nearest shrunken centroid. It estimates a pooled within-class standard deviation per feature, converts each class mean into a standardised deviation from the overall mean, soft-thresholds that deviation at shrinkage, and reconstructs the centroid. The classifier then scores with the standardised distance and the class log-prior. Zero shrinkage reproduces the plain rule on standardised data; infinite shrinkage sends every centroid to the overall mean and the model to the majority class.

    nearest_subclass runs k-means inside each class and keeps the resulting centres with a pointer back to their class. Scoring takes the best subclass per class. With n_subclasses=1 and a fixed seed it is the plain rule; with two it is a piecewise-linear boundary that can carve out a class with two modes.

    cosine normalises both the point and the centroid and scores with the dot product, which is the cosine of the angle between them. The centroid is still the arithmetic mean of the class, which is a subtle choice: the mean of unnormalised vectors is the direction of their sum, and that is exactly the Rocchio relevance centroid from information retrieval.

    Derivation #

    The rule #

    Let class \(k\) have \(n_k\) points and mean

    \[c_k=\frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{j:\,y_j=k}x_j , \qquad (1)\]

    and predict the class whose mean is closest, \(\hat y(x)=\arg\min_k\|x-c_k\|^2\). Expanding the square, \(\|x-c_k\|^2=\|x\|^2-2x^{\top}c_k+\|c_k\|^2\), and the \(\|x\|^2\) term is the same for every class, so the rule is

    \[\hat y(x)=\arg\max_k\left(x^{\top}c_k-\tfrac12\|c_k\|^2\right). \qquad (2)\]

    The boundary between two classes is where the two scores are equal, which is a hyperplane with normal \(c_i-c_j\): nearest centroid is a linear classifier whose weights are differences of class means.

    Why it is LDA without the covariance #

    Let class \(k\) be Gaussian with mean \(\mu_k\) and covariance \(\Sigma\), the same for every class, and prior \(\pi_k\). The discriminant is

    \[\log p(x\mid k)+\log\pi_k =-\tfrac12(x-\mu_k)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu_k)+\log\pi_k+\mathrm{const} . \qquad (3)\]

    The quadratic term in \(x\) does not depend on \(k\), so the comparison reduces to

    \[\hat y(x)=\arg\max_k\left(\mu_k^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}x -\tfrac12\mu_k^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\mu_k+\log\pi_k\right), \qquad (4)\]

    which is LDA. Now write \(W=\Sigma^{-1/2}\) and complete the square:

    \[\mu_k^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}x-\tfrac12\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\mu_k\|^2 =\tfrac12\|Wx\|^2-\tfrac12\|Wx-W\mu_k\|^2 . \qquad (5)\]

    The first term is common to all classes, so the optimal rule is exactly nearest-centroid in whitened space: transform every point by \(W\) and take the nearest mean. Nearest centroid is LDA with the whitening step skipped, which is optimal when \(\Sigma\) is a multiple of the identity and otherwise merely cheap.

    With unequal priors the rule gains the term \(\log\pi_k\), and with a per-feature standard deviation \(s_i\) it becomes the standardised distance the shrunken variant uses. The demo's closed-form LDA agrees with the whitened nearest centroid on every held-out point, which is a direct check of (5).

    Shrinkage #

    The nearest shrunken centroid starts from a pooled within-class standard deviation per feature,

    \[s_i=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j:\,y_j=k} \left(x_{ji}-\bar x_{ki}\right)^2}, \qquad (6)\]

    and the overall mean \(\bar x_i\). The class mean is turned into a standardised deviation from the overall mean, scaled by how much a mean of \(n_k\) points can wander:

    \[d_{ik}=\frac{\bar x_{ki}-\bar x_i}{m_k\,s_i}, \qquad m_k=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_k}-\frac{1}{n}} . \qquad (7)\]

    The factor \(m_k\) is the standard error of the difference between the class mean and the overall mean, so \(d_{ik}\) is a \(t\)-like statistic: how many standard errors the class sits away from the pool. Soft-thresholding it,

    \[d'_{ik}=\mathrm{sign}(d_{ik})\,\max\left(|d_{ik}|-\Delta,\,0\right), \qquad (8)\]

    sets to zero every feature whose class deviation is within \(\Delta\) standard errors, and the shrunken centroid is the reconstruction

    \[\bar x'_{ik}=\bar x_i+m_k\,s_i\,d'_{ik} . \qquad (9)\]

    Classification uses the standardised distance and the log-prior,

    \[\hat y(x)=\arg\min_k\left(\sum_i\frac{(x_i-\bar x'_{ik})^2}{s_i^2} -2\log\pi_k\right), \qquad (10)\]

    which is (4) with a diagonal covariance and the shrunken means. The variant is therefore a feature selector and a classifier in one: a feature whose \(d'_{ik}\) is zero in every class contributes the same value to every score and cannot change the answer. The demo measures the two ends of that trade: at \(\Delta=3\) the model keeps 11 of 600 features and 11 of the 12 informative ones, and accuracy peaks; at \(\Delta=10\) it keeps nothing, every centroid equals the overall mean, and the answer is the majority class.

    Subclasses #

    For a class with several modes, replace one centroid by \(J\) of them and solve

    \[\min_{\{c_{kj}\}}\sum_{k}\sum_{j:\,y_j=k}\min_{m}\|x_j-c_{km}\|^2 , \qquad (11)\]

    which is \(K\) independent k-means problems, each solved by Lloyd's algorithm from a k-means++ start. The decision rule becomes

    \[\hat y(x)=\arg\min_k\min_m\|x-c_{km}\|^2 , \qquad (12)\]

    still piecewise linear, but with as many pieces as there are subclasses. This is a crude Gaussian mixture classifier: the mixture weights and covariances are dropped, and only the centres are kept.

    Cosine #

    For the angle, score

    \[\hat y(x)=\arg\max_k\frac{x^{\top}c_k}{\|x\|\,\|c_k\|}, \qquad (13)\]

    which ignores \(\|x\|\) entirely. It is the right rule when a point's direction is its content and its length is an accident of how it was measured, and it costs one normalisation per row.

    Code walkthrough #

    The distance, without a loop #

        squared = (np.sum(X ** 2, axis=1)[:, None]
               + np.sum(C ** 2, axis=1)[None, :]
               - 2.0 * X @ C.T)
    return np.maximum(squared, 0.0)

    The expansion of \(\|x-c\|^2\) as three matrix operations: the row norms of the points, the row norms of the centroids, and the cross term. It is the standard identity, and the maximum is not decoration — for nearly identical vectors the subtraction leaves a small negative number, and a negative squared distance would sort below zero and win an argmax it should not.

    Shrinkage in ten lines #

            for group in groups:
            centred = points[group] - points[group].mean(axis=0)
            total += np.sum(centred ** 2, axis=0)
        spread = np.sqrt(total / max(rows - len(groups), 1))

    The pooled within-class variance of (6), accumulated one class at a time: each class is centred on its own mean, so the class means themselves do not inflate the spread. The denominator is the residual degrees of freedom, \(n-K\).

            for group in groups:
            scale = np.sqrt(1.0 / group.size - 1.0 / rows)
            deviation = (points[group].mean(axis=0) - overall) / (scale * spread)
            keep = np.sign(deviation) * np.maximum(
                np.abs(deviation) - self.shrinkage, 0.0)
            centroids.append(overall + scale * spread * keep)

    (7), (8) and (9) in order. The scale is \(m_k\), which is where the method differs from naive thresholding of the means: a class with ten points is allowed to sit further from the pool before its deviation counts as real than a class with a thousand. np.sign times maximum is the soft threshold, and the reconstruction multiplies back by exactly the factor that standardised it, so a feature with \(d'=d\) comes back unchanged.

    Scoring the shrunken model #

            if self.variant == "shrunken":
            standardised = (points[:, None, :]
                            - self.centroids_[None, :, :]) / self.scales_
            return -np.sum(standardised ** 2, axis=2) + 2.0 * np.log(self.priors_)

    (10) with the sign flipped so that larger is still better. The broadcasting builds an \((m, K, d)\) array of differences — one temporary of size \(m\cdot K\cdot d\) — and reduces it in one pass. The log-prior is the \(-\log\pi_k\) of (10) moved to the left. The standard and nearest_subclass variants do not use priors, which is a deliberate asymmetry: they are the unregularised baseline, and adding a prior to them would hide the effect the demo is trying to measure.

    Subclass scoring #

            scores = np.full((points.shape[0], self.classes_.size), -np.inf)
        for column, owner in enumerate(self.centroid_classes_):
            scores[:, owner] = np.maximum(scores[:, owner], distance[:, column])
        return scores

    (12): each subclass scores its own class, and the class takes the best of its subclasses. -inf is the identity for maximum, so a class with no centroid cannot accidentally win — impossible here, but the initialisation says what the reduction means. The cost is one pass over the subclass columns after the distance matrix, not a second distance matrix.

    Cosine #

            if self.variant == "cosine":
            norms = np.linalg.norm(points, axis=1, keepdims=True)
            norms[norms == 0.0] = 1.0
            unit = points / norms
            target = self.centroids_ / np.linalg.norm(
                self.centroids_, axis=1, keepdims=True)
            return unit @ target.T

    (13), with one guard: a zero vector has no angle, so its norm is replaced by one and the point is scored against the origin. Without the guard the division propagates nan into the argmax, and nan comparisons are false, so the prediction becomes the first class — a silent, data-dependent bug rather than an error. The centroids are normalised at predict time rather than stored normalised, because centroids_ is part of the public state and should be the means, not a rescaled version of them.

    Complexity #

    Let \(n\) be the training count, \(d\) the features, \(K\) the classes, \(J\) the subclasses per class, \(I\) the k-means iterations, and \(m\) the test count.

    operation time memory
    fit, standard \(O(nd)\) \(O(Kd)\)
    fit, shrunken \(O(nd+Kd)\) \(O(Kd+d)\)
    fit, nearest_subclass \(O(nKJI)\) \(O(KJd)\)
    fit, cosine \(O(nd)\) \(O(Kd)\)
    predict on \(m\) points \(O(mKd)\) \(O(mK)\)
    model size — \(O(Kd)\)

    Fitting is a single pass to accumulate sums, and prediction is one matrix product. That is the entire appeal: no Gram matrix, no iterations, no hyperparameters except the two the repairs introduce. The k-means inside nearest_subclass is the only part that iterates, and it is \(I\approx50\) passes over one class at a time, so it stays linear in \(n\) with a constant that depends on \(J\).

    The model is also small in the way that matters on a device: 600 features and three classes is 1 800 numbers plus the scales, against \(n\cdot d\) for a nearest-neighbour rule and \(n\) support vectors for an SVM. The demo's 1-NN baseline has to keep every training point to be beaten by a model with one mean per class.

    Failure modes #

    A class that is not one blob. The mean of two separated clusters sits between them, where no sample lives. The demo's XOR geometry puts both class means within 0.1 of the origin and the plain rule at 0.502; two subclasses take it to 1.000. Any time the within-class scatter is larger than the between-class separation, suspect this before tuning anything.

    Features on different scales. A feature measured in thousands dominates one measured in fractions, and the nearest mean is effectively chosen by the loudest column. Standardise first, or use shrunken, which divides by the within-class spread and is scale-invariant by construction. The plain variant has no such protection, and it is the most common reason nearest centroid loses to logistic regression on tabular data.

    High dimensions and small samples. With \(d\) comparable to \(n\), each class mean is estimated from a handful of points per coordinate, and the estimate's variance is \(O(d/n)\). Shrinkage is the fix, and the demo shows both sides: the optimum at \(\Delta=3\) keeps 11 features and scores 0.993; \(\Delta=4\) keeps 9 and drops to 0.969, because it has started to delete signal. The threshold is a hyperparameter and needs a validation split — the training accuracy is monotone in it and useless for choosing.

    Magnitude that is not content. A long document and a short one on the same topic are far apart in Euclidean distance and identical in angle. The demo's short documents of the long class sit closer to the wrong centroid than to the right one, and Euclidean distance scores 0.890. cosine fixes it, but only if length really is noise; for measurements where magnitude carries information, cosine throws that information away.

    Unequal class sizes and no priors. standard and nearest_subclass ignore \(n_k\), so a rare class with a noisy mean can lose to a common one that happens to be nearby. shrunken is the only variant that adds \(\log\pi_k\). If the priors matter, the fix is a line of code, but it has to be a deliberate one.

    A single class, or a class with no points. Both are refused at fit. One class has nothing to compare against and the classifier would return that class for everything; the code raises rather than doing that quietly.

    Ties. Two equidistant means resolve to the lower class index because argmax returns the first maximum. With continuous features this is measure zero; with integer or categorical features it is common, and the tie-break is documented by the code rather than chosen by the data.

    Reading decision_function as a probability. The scores are negative squared distances, so they are unbounded below and have no calibration. A score of \(-0.3\) and a score of \(-30\) both mean "closest so far". Only the ranking within one fitted model is meaningful, exactly as with the one-class decision values.

    Applications #

    Text classification. The Rocchio classifier — centroid per category, cosine similarity — is the classic relevance-feedback method in information retrieval and remains a strong baseline for topic tagging. It needs no training loop, and it degrades gracefully as categories are added.

    Gene expression and biomarkers. PAM (prediction analysis for microarrays) is the shrunken variant applied to expression data, where \(d\) is in the thousands and \(n\) in the dozens. It was designed for exactly the regime in which the plain rule fails, and the shrunken centroids double as a gene panel.

    Speaker and writer identification. A centroid per speaker in MFCC or embedding space, scored by cosine, is a standard text-independent baseline and the fallback when an i-vector or neural system is unavailable.

    Prototype networks and few-shot learning. A class mean of embedded examples is the nearest-centroid rule in a learned metric, and prototypical networks are its modern form: the embedding replaces the raw features and the rest of the algorithm is unchanged.

    Retrieval over embeddings. For a fixed set of categories, one vector per category is an approximate index that fits in cache and answers in one matrix product, which is often the right trade against an exact nearest-neighbour search.

    Anomaly triage. The distance to the nearest class centroid is a crude novelty score: a point far from every mean is worth a second look even if the argmax is confident. It is the cheapest version of the one-class idea in the previous atom.

    A baseline that is hard to beat. On small, noisy, roughly Gaussian data the variance of the estimate matters more than its bias, and one mean per class beats \(k\)-NN — the demo's 0.928 against 0.905 at 400 points. Reporting nearest centroid in a comparison is a service to the reader.
