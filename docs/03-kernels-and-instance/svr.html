    Support vector regression — a tube, a hinge and a dual

    Intuition #

    Support vector regression takes the support-vector machine and turns the street into a tube. In classification the model must put every point on the right side of a margin. In regression there are no sides, so the model is allowed to be wrong by up to \(\epsilon\) everywhere — inside a band of half-width \(\epsilon\) around the target, the loss is exactly zero:

          y
      │        ·        ·
      │   ────────────────  f(x) + ε
      │      ·    ·         ·
      │   ·  ──────────────  f(x)
      │        ·   ·   ·    ·
      │   ────────────────  f(x) − ε
      │  ·                     ·
      └────────────────────────── x
        free    free    a point
        (inside (on the  outside the tube
        the tube) tube)  pays |y − f| − ε

    Outside the tube the cost is linear, not squared. That single change is what makes the solution sparse. With a squared loss every point pulls on the fitted curve; with an \(\epsilon\)-insensitive loss a point that the tube already covers pulls on nothing, and its weight is exactly zero. The model is the handful of points the tube cannot contain — the support vectors — and everything else could be deleted from the training set without changing the prediction.

    The second consequence is that the fit is written in terms of kernel values between training points, so a linear model, a polynomial and an RBF model are the same code with a different Gram matrix. The third is that the parameters are not solved for directly: the problem is a box-constrained quadratic program in one coefficient per training point, and the whole atom is a small solver for that program plus the bookkeeping around it.

    Two parameterisations come out of the same solver. Fix the tube \(\epsilon\) and bound the coefficients by \(C\); or fix the budget \(\nu\) as a fraction of points allowed outside the tube and let the solver find \(\epsilon\). The second one is easier to reason about because \(\nu\) is a number between zero and one: at \(\nu=0.5\) with \(C=1\) on forty points the model uses twenty support vectors and twenty points fall outside the tube.

    Variants #

    variant what it changes default kernel
    epsilon_svr the tube is fixed, the box is \(C\) RBF
    nu_svr the support fraction is fixed, the tube is found RBF
    linear a straight-line model, \(K(x,z)=x^{\top}z\) linear
    rbf a bump per training point, \(K=e^{-\gamma|x-z|^2}\) RBF
    smo the classic largest-violation working set RBF 
    graph TD

  A --> C[kernel: polynomial]
  A --> D[kernel: RBF]
  A --> E[kernel: sigmoid]
  A --> F[working set: largest violation]
  A --> G[working set: second order]
  A --> H[parameterise by nu]

    epsilon_svr is the base model: minimise the tube loss with a box bound on the dual coefficients and a hard tube of width \(\epsilon\). Everything else in the file is a variation on it.

    nu_svr replaces \(C\) and \(\epsilon\) with a single \(\nu\) in \((0,1]\) that bounds the fraction of margin errors from above and the fraction of support vectors from below. The solver bisects on \(\epsilon\) until the total weight hits \(C\nu\).

    linear sets the kernel to the dot product. The model is then literally a line, and the dual is a program over one direction — the Gram matrix has rank one in one dimension.

    rbf sets the kernel to a Gaussian bump centred on each support vector. The prediction is a weighted sum of bumps, which is why it can bend.

    smo keeps the model and changes only the rule that picks the pair of coefficients to update. The default rule is second order (Fan, Chen and Lin); this variant is the classic rule that takes the largest KKT violation. Both stop on the same tolerance and reach the same answer.

    Derivation #

    The loss #

    The \(\epsilon\)-insensitive loss is

    \[L_\epsilon(r)= \begin{cases} 0 & |r|\le\epsilon,\\ |r|-\epsilon & |r|>\epsilon, \end{cases} \qquad (1)\]

    with \(r=y-f(x)\). It is the hinge loss of classification with two hinges instead of one: a dead zone in the middle and a linear penalty on each side. Squaring it gives the Huber loss; not squaring it is what keeps the support set finite.

    The primal #

    With slack variables for the two sides of the tube, the problem is

    \[\min_{w,b,\xi,\xi^*} \tfrac12\|w\|^2+C\sum_i(\xi_i+\xi_i^*) \qquad (2)\]

    subject to

    \[y_i-w^{\top}\phi(x_i)-b\le\epsilon+\xi_i,\qquad w^{\top}\phi(x_i)+b-y_i\le\epsilon+\xi_i^*, \qquad \xi_i,\xi_i^*\ge0. \qquad (3)\]

    The first constraint is the upper hinge, the second the lower one. The regulariser \(\tfrac12\|w\|^2\) is what makes the solution a combination of a few training points rather than an interpolant, and \(C\) trades it against the tube violations.

    The dual #

    Introduce multipliers \(\alpha_i,\alpha_i^*\ge0\) for the two constraints and \(\eta_i,\eta_i^*\ge0\) for the slack. Stationarity in \(w\) gives the expansion

    \[w=\sum_i(\alpha_i-\alpha_i^*)\,\phi(x_i), \qquad (4)\]

    stationarity in \(b\) gives the balance \(\sum_i(\alpha_i-\alpha_i^*)=0\), and stationarity in the slacks gives \(C=\alpha_i+\eta_i\). Substituting back leaves

    \[\min_{\alpha,\alpha^*}\ \tfrac12\delta^{\top}K\delta -\ y^{\top}\delta+\epsilon\,\|\delta\|_1 \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \mathbf{1}^{\top}\delta=0,\ \ 0\le\alpha_i,\alpha_i^*\le C, \qquad (5)\]

    where \(\delta=\alpha-\alpha^*\) and \(K_{ij}=K(x_i,x_j)\). Only the difference \(\delta\) appears, so the two multipliers are one variable with a sign: a point outside the tube above gets \(\alpha_i>0\), a point outside below gets \(\alpha_i^*>0\), and a point inside gets both zero.

    The prediction is the expansion (4) evaluated at a new point:

    \[f(x)=\sum_i\delta_i\,K(x_i,x)+\bar b. \qquad (6)\]

    What the KKT conditions say #

    The box constraints and the tube give a three-way classification, and the demo checks it point by point. With \(r_i=y_i-w^{\top}\phi(x_i)\) and \(\bar b\) the intercept:

    dual coefficient position of the point condition
    \(0<\alpha_i on the upper edge of the tube \(r_i-\bar b=\epsilon\)
    \(0<\alpha_i^* on the lower edge \(r_i-\bar b=-\epsilon\)
    \(\alpha_i=\alpha_i^*=0\) strictly inside \(\lvert r_i-\bar b\rvert\le\epsilon\)
    \(\alpha_i=C\) above the tube \(r_i-\bar b\ge\epsilon\)
    \(\alpha_i^*=C\) below the tube \(r_i-\bar b\le-\epsilon\)

    The first two rows are the useful ones: a coefficient strictly inside the box is a point sitting exactly on the tube edge, and it is those points that pin the intercept. If none exist the intercept is only determined up to an interval, and the code takes its midpoint.

    The solver #

    Write \(\beta=(\alpha,\alpha^*)\) and stack the problem. With \(s_i=+1\) for the first block and \(s_i=-1\) for the second, the constraint is \(s^{\top}\beta=0\) and the objective matrix is \(Q_{ij}=s_is_jK_{i\bmod n,\,j\bmod n}\), so the program is the standard box-constrained form

    \[\min_\beta\ \tfrac12\beta^{\top}Q\beta+p^{\top}\beta \quad\text{s.t.}\quad s^{\top}\beta=0,\ \ 0\le\beta_i\le C, \qquad p=(\epsilon-y,\ \epsilon+y). \qquad (7)\]

    A pair step moves two coordinates so that the equality survives. If \(s_i=s_j\) the step is \(\beta_i\leftarrow\beta_i-t\), \(\beta_j\leftarrow\beta_j+t\); if \(s_i\ne s_j\) both move by \(+t\). The exact minimiser along the direction is the Newton step, which for the first case is

    \[t^\star=\frac{G_i-G_j}{K_{ii}+K_{jj}-2K_{ij}}, \qquad (8)\]

    and the second case has \(-G_i-G_j\) in the numerator. The denominator is \(\|\phi(x_i)-\phi(x_j)\|^2\) — the same curvature the SVM uses — and it is positive unless the two points coincide. The step is then clipped to the box, so a pair that cannot move freely stops at whichever coordinate hits a bound first.

    The working set is what separates a usable solver from a slow one. The default rule fixes \(i\) as the most violated coordinate in the "can increase" set and picks \(j\) from the "can decrease" set by maximising the predicted decrease

    \[\text{gain}(j)=\frac{\big(\text{violation}_j\big)^2}{\|\phi_i-\phi_j\|^2}, \qquad (9)\]

    which is the exact reduction in the objective for the pair. The smo variant instead picks the largest violation without dividing by the curvature. Both are valid; on the demo's problems the second-order rule needs 1490 steps where the classic one needs 3009, and at \(\epsilon=0\) the ordering reverses.

    The intercept #

    For a free support vector the KKT condition is an equation, so each one gives a candidate

    \[\bar b=r_i-\mathrm{sign}(\delta_i)\,\epsilon , \qquad (10)\]

    and the code averages the candidates. When every coefficient sits on the box there is no equation and the conditions become inequalities, which define an interval

    \[\max_{\delta_j=-C}\ (r_j+\epsilon)\ \le\ \bar b \ \le\ \min_{\delta_i=+C}\ (r_i-\epsilon), \qquad (11)\]

    widened by the inside points, whose condition is \(|r_i-\bar b|\le\epsilon\). The code intersects all of them and returns the midpoint. Getting this wrong is invisible on problems that have free support vectors and shows up as a large KKT residual on the ones that do not.

    The nu parameterisation #

    The \(\nu\) form replaces the tube with a budget. Its dual is

    \[\min_{\delta}\ \tfrac12\delta^{\top}K\delta-y^{\top}\delta \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \mathbf{1}^{\top}\delta=0,\ \ \|\delta\|_1\le C\nu,\ \ |\delta_i|\le C/n, \qquad (12)\]

    and \(\epsilon\) becomes the multiplier of the norm constraint. Two facts follow directly from the budget. Every nonzero coefficient has \(|\delta_i|\ge\) the smallest positive weight, and the points outside the tube are exactly those at the box, so

    \[\#\{\text{support vectors}\}\ \ge\ \nu n,\qquad \#\{\text{margin errors}\}\ \le\ \nu n . \qquad (13)\]

    The code does not solve (12) directly. Instead it solves (5) for a sequence of \(\epsilon\) and bisects, using the fact that \(\|\delta(\epsilon)\|_1\) is non-increasing in \(\epsilon\): a wider tube explains more points for free, so fewer coefficients leave zero. The measured table in the demo runs from \(\epsilon=3.17\) at \(\nu=0.1\) down to \(\epsilon=0.27\) at \(\nu=0.8\), with 4 and 32 support vectors.

    Kernels #

    Nothing in the derivation above needed the features, only their inner products. Four are provided:

    \[K(x,z)=x^{\top}z,\qquad K(x,z)=(\gamma x^{\top}z+c_0)^d,\qquad K(x,z)=e^{-\gamma\|x-z\|^2},\qquad K(x,z)=\tanh(\gamma x^{\top}z+c_0). \qquad (14)\]

    The RBF kernel is computed from the identity \(\|x-z\|^2=\|x\|^2+\|z\|^2-2x^{\top}z\), which is one matrix product and two broadcasts rather than a loop over pairs. On the demo's sine wave the linear kernel reaches a held-out RMSE of \(0.4786\) and the RBF kernel \(0.0865\), with ten support vectors instead of thirty-eight.

    Code walkthrough #

    The Gram matrix #

        if kind == "rbf":
        squared = (np.sum(X ** 2, axis=1)[:, None]
                   + np.sum(Z ** 2, axis=1)[None, :] - 2.0 * X @ Z.T)
        return np.exp(-gamma * np.maximum(squared, 0.0))

    The expansion is the whole kernel. maximum(squared, 0.0) is not cosmetic: for near-identical points the subtraction can produce a tiny negative number, and exp of a small positive number is larger than one — a Gram entry above one is harmless, but it breaks the identity \(\mathrm{diag}(K)=1\) that the demo's assertions rely on.

    Selecting the pair #

                score = -sign * gradient
            i = int(np.argmax(np.where(up, score, -np.inf)))
            head = i % rows
            gap = np.maximum(diagonal[head] + diagonal
                             - 2.0 * gram[head, index % rows], 1e-12)
            violation = score[i] - score
            candidate = low & (index != i) & (violation > self.tol)
            if not candidate.any():
                break
            if second_order:
                j = int(np.argmax(np.where(candidate, violation ** 2 / gap,
                                           -np.inf)))
            else:
                j = int(np.argmax(np.where(candidate, violation, -np.inf)))

    score = -sign * gradient is the quantity whose maximum over the "up" set and minimum over the "low" set defines a violating pair. The index != i term is essential rather than defensive: a coordinate strictly inside the box belongs to both sets, so without it the same coordinate can be chosen as both members of the pair, the gain is zero, and the loop stops on a solution that is not optimal. gap is the curvature of (8) for every candidate partner at once, which is what makes the second-order rule a single vectorised expression.

    The step and the update #

                agree = sign[i] == sign[j]
            slope = gap[j]
            if agree:
                step = (gradient[i] - gradient[j]) / slope
                low_step = max(-beta[j], beta[i] - box)
                high_step = min(beta[i], box - beta[j])
            else:
                step = (-gradient[i] - gradient[j]) / slope
                low_step = max(-beta[i], -beta[j])
                high_step = min(box - beta[i], box - beta[j])
            step = float(np.clip(step, low_step, high_step))

    Two cases, one for each way of keeping \(s^{\top}\beta\) fixed. The bounds are computed from both coordinates, so the step lands exactly on a box face when the unconstrained Newton step would leave the feasible set. slope = gap[j] is the curvature between the chosen pair — the bound of \(10^{-12}\) from the selection step is what keeps a duplicate point from dividing by zero.

                if agree:
                beta[i] -= step
                beta[j] += step
                left, right = -step * sign[i], step * sign[j]
            else:
                beta[i] += step
                beta[j] += step
                left, right = step * sign[i], step * sign[j]
            delta[head] += left
            delta[tail] += right
            change = left * gram[:, head] + right * gram[:, tail]
            fitted += change
            gradient[:rows] += change
            gradient[rows:] -= change

    delta is kept in update form rather than recomputed from beta, so the solver never has to subtract two nearly equal numbers. The gradient update is two rank-one columns of the Gram matrix, because a pair step only touches two training points — that is why one iteration costs \(O(n)\) and not \(O(n^2)\).

    The bisection #

            zero = self._solve(gram, targets, box, 0.0)
        if float(np.abs(zero[0]).sum()) <= target:
            self.epsilon_ = 0.0
            return zero
        state = zero
        for _ in range(60):
            middle = 0.5 * (low + high)
            state = self._solve(gram, targets, box, middle)
            mass = float(np.abs(state[0]).sum())
            if mass > target:
                low = middle
            else:
                high = middle
            if high - low <= 1e-12:
                break
        self.epsilon_ = high
        return self._solve(gram, targets, box, high)

    The early exit handles a \(\nu\) small enough that the tube-free solution already fits the budget — then \(\epsilon=0\) is optimal and there is nothing to bisect. The direction of the update is the monotonicity: too much weight means the tube is too narrow, so \(\epsilon\) moves up. The box is \(C/n\) rather than \(C\), which is what turns the norm budget into a count in (13).

    The intercept #

            delta = alpha - alpha_star
        top = delta >= box - 1e-8
        bottom = delta <= -box + 1e-8
        middle = ~top & ~bottom
        lower = np.concatenate([residual[bottom] + epsilon,
                                residual[middle] - epsilon])
        upper = np.concatenate([residual[top] - epsilon,
                                residual[middle] + epsilon])
        if lower.size and upper.size:
            return 0.5 * float(lower.max() + upper.min())

    This is (11) with the three cases of the KKT table collapsed into two arrays. The bottom points give lower bounds on \(\bar b\) and the top points upper bounds; the middle points, including the free support vectors, give both. Taking the midpoint is what keeps the residual on either side of the interval balanced, so the reported KKT violation is half the width instead of the whole width when the interval is not a point.

    The KKT residual #

            for value, error in zip(self.delta_, residual, strict=True):
            free = 1e-8 < abs(value) < scale - 1e-8
            if free:
                worst = max(worst, abs(error - (self.b_ + np.sign(value) * epsilon)))
            elif abs(value) <= 1e-8:
                worst = max(worst, max(0.0, abs(error - self.b_) - epsilon))
            elif value >= scale - 1e-8:
                worst = max(worst, max(0.0, (self.b_ + epsilon) - error))
            else:
                worst = max(worst, max(0.0, error - (self.b_ - epsilon)))

    error is the residual without the intercept, so every condition compares against self.b_ shifted by \(\pm\epsilon\). The four branches are the four rows of the table, and scale is \(C\) or \(C/n\) depending on the parameterisation. A free coefficient is checked as an equality and the others as one-sided inequalities, which is exactly why the demo can assert \(10^{-14}\) on one variant and \(10^{-9}\) on the other: the nu solver stops on a tolerance in \(\epsilon\), not in the coefficients.

    Complexity #

    Let \(n\) be the training count, \(d\) the features, and \(T\) the number of pair updates.

    operation time memory
    Gram matrix \(O(n^2d)\) \(O(n^2)\)
    one pair update \(O(n)\) \(O(1)\)
    one bisection solve \(O(Tn)\) \(O(n)\)
    the whole nu fit \(O(Tn\log(1/\tau))\) \(O(n)\)
    prediction on \(m\) points \(O(m\,n_{\text{sv}}\,d)\) \(O(n_{\text{sv}})\)

    The Gram matrix is the dominant cost in \(n\) and is computed once. Each pair update touches two columns, so the solver is linear in \(n\) per step, and the step count is the only thing the working-set rule changes. Prediction only touches the support vectors, which is why the sparse expansion is worth having at serving time: the demo's RBF model uses ten of fifty points.

    The bisection multiplies the solve cost by about forty: the bracket starts at the spread of the targets and stops when it is \(10^{-12}\) wide, which is \(\log_2\) of the ratio — around forty halvings. That is the price of the clean parameterisation. A production implementation carries \(\epsilon\) as a variable in the dual instead.

    Failure modes #

    A tube so wide that nothing is outside it. With \(\epsilon\) larger than the spread of the residuals every coefficient is zero, the prediction is the mean of the targets, and the model has no support vectors. The demo's \(\epsilon=0.6\) row keeps four, which is where the boundary is. Nothing errors; the model just stops being a model.

    A tube so narrow that everything is a support vector. At \(\epsilon=0\) every noisy point is a margin error, all forty coefficients are nonzero, and the "sparse" method costs as much as a dense one. That is not a bug — it is the definition — but it is the reason \(\epsilon\) is the first parameter to tune.

    Assuming a plane is enough. On the demo's sine wave the linear kernel reaches a held-out error of \(0.4786\) while the RBF kernel reaches \(0.0865\). Both converged, both satisfy the KKT conditions, and one of them is useless. Choosing the kernel is not part of the optimisation.

    An ill-conditioned Gram matrix. A degree-three polynomial kernel on inputs of magnitude three produces entries in the thousands and a condition number above \(10^{19}\); the solver then needs thousands of updates to converge and may stop on max_iter with a visible KKT residual. The demo uses \(\gamma=0.05\) for that kernel, which is the kernel-scale version of feature scaling.

    Overflow in the kernel scale. \(\gamma\) too large makes the RBF matrix the identity: every training point only sees itself, the fit interpolates the training set and predicts zero everywhere else. \(\gamma\) too small makes it rank one, which is the linear kernel in disguise. The default \(1/d\) is a starting point, not a rule.

    A bug that only appears when no coefficient is free. The intercept in (11) degenerates when every support vector sits on the box. Averaging the two groups separately rather than intersecting their constraints produces a plausible number, a small training error and a KKT residual of order the gap between the two bounds — here \(0.17\) before the fix and \(10^{-9}\) after.

    Solving a different program than the one documented. Flipping the sign of the tube term, or treating \(\alpha\) and \(\alpha^*\) as independent without the balance constraint, still yields a convex program and still converges. The only symptom is that the residuals of the free points are at \(-\epsilon\) instead of \(+\epsilon\), which is why the demo checks the sign rather than the magnitude.

    Reading sparsity as importance. A nonzero coefficient means the point is outside the tube, not that it is informative. Points in a noisy region are all outside it; the support set of an \(\epsilon\)-SVR is a set of violations, and outliers dominate it.

    Using the training KKT residual as a generalisation estimate. The residual is a solver diagnostic and it is zero at the optimum of any well-posed problem, including one that has memorised the data. The demo reports held-out RMSE separately for exactly this reason.

    Applications #

    Time-series and sensor calibration. A tube around the fitted value is a natural model of instrument tolerance, and the sparse expansion means the predictor is a short list of past readings rather than a dense model.

    Robust regression. The linear loss outside the tube bounds the influence of a single outlier, unlike least squares; the support set tells you which points were allowed to pull, which is often the interesting output.

    Load and demand forecasting. SVR was a standard baseline for short-horizon load prediction before gradient boosting, and the \(\epsilon\)-tube matches the error tolerance the operator actually cares about.

    Chemometrics and spectral calibration. Near-infrared spectra are wide and highly collinear, and a kernel SVR on a few hundred wavelengths with a sparse support set became the standard chemometric tool for concentration prediction.

    Financial volatility. The asymmetric version with two different tube widths fits the asymmetry of returns, and the budget parameterisation controls the fraction of days the model refuses to explain.

    Surrogate models for simulation. A small SVR trained on a design of experiments is a cheap stand-in for a solver, and the \(\epsilon\) tube keeps the surrogate from chasing numerical noise in the simulator.

    Anomaly scoring. The residual of a fitted SVR, thresholded, is a one-class detector in disguise: points far outside the tube at inference are the ones the training distribution does not cover.

    Teaching duality. The atom is small enough to check by hand: a primal with slacks, a dual with box constraints, a KKT table, and a solver whose every step can be verified against strong duality. That is why it survives in courses long after it stopped being the state of the art.
