    03 · Kernels & instance-based/tier 3/5 variants/source

    Metric learning — learn the distance instead of assuming it

    Intuition #

    Every method in this chapter takes a distance as given. kNN measures Euclidean distance, the RBF network measures it after a bandwidth, the SVM measures it in a kernel's feature space. But a feature vector is an argument about relevance, and Euclidean distance is the claim that every coordinate matters equally. When one axis is a measurement in metres and the next is a count of children, that claim is false before the data arrives.

    Metric learning replaces the claim with a parameter. The model is a positive semidefinite matrix \(M\), and the distance is

    \[d_M(x,z)^2=(x-z)^{\top}M(x-z),\]

    or, equivalently, a linear map \(L\) with \(M=L^{\top}L\) and

    \[d_M(x,z)=\lVert L(x-z)\rVert_2 .\]

    The map is the useful form: it sends the data into a new space where ordinary Euclidean distance is the right one. Supervision is the only thing that can choose it, and the labels say which pairs should land close and which should land far:

          raw space                       learned space
   ┌────────────────┐              ┌──────────────┐
   │  ●    ●        │              │  ● ●         │
   │      noise ────┼──► L ──►     │ ● ●          │
   │  ●    ●        │              │         ○ ○  │
   └────────────────┘              │          ○   │
                                   └──────────────┘

    The picture is a rotation plus a stretch, but the stretch is learned from pairs, not from the marginal variance. That distinction is the whole subject: PCA and whitening also produce an \(M\), but they never look at a label, so they cannot know that the loudest direction is noise.

    Once \(M\) exists, everything downstream is free. predict is a kNN vote in the learned space, but the same transform output can feed a linear SVM, a clustering routine, a nearest-centroid classifier, or a retrieval index. The metric is a reusable view of the data rather than a model of one task.

    The five variants are five answers to "far according to what".

    nca maximises the expected accuracy of a soft nearest-neighbour rule. Each point spreads a unit of probability over the others in inverse proportion to distance, and the objective is the mass that lands on its own class. It is smooth enough to differentiate and directly optimises the quantity kNN is judged by, at the price of a non-convex landscape.

    lmnn asks for a margin. Each point's \(k\) nearest same-class neighbours become targets to pull in, and every different-class point that comes within one unit more than a target becomes an impostor to push out. With the targets held fixed the objective is convex in \(M\), which is why it is the variant people trust when the answer has to be reproducible.

    itml starts from a prior metric, usually the identity, and projects it onto the constraints "these pairs are within \(u\)" and "those pairs are beyond \(l\)". The measure of how far the metric moved is the KL divergence between the Gaussians with those covariance matrices, which makes each projection a closed-form rank-one update. There is no gradient and no learning rate.

    mvp is the closed-form relative. It maximises the mean squared distance of different-class pairs over the mean squared distance of same-class pairs. That ratio is a generalised eigenproblem, so the answer is a whitening followed by an eigendecomposition, and the leading directions are the ones in which the classes are most separated relative to their own spread. This is the between/within criterion of linear discriminant analysis, read as a Mahalanobis metric rather than as a classifier.

    deep_metric drops the linearity. A two-layer network embeds the data on the unit sphere, and a triplet loss pulls each anchor towards a positive and away from a negative by a margin. The metric is still Euclidean, but in a space the network chooses, so the decision boundary can bend around classes that no single \(M\) can separate.

    The demo has two problems. On three Gaussian clusters buried under eight noise axes, Euclidean 1-nearest-neighbour gets 59 % and every linear variant gets at least 87 %. On an xor — the sign of \(x_0x_1\) — Euclidean gets 52 %, the best linear metric 81 %, and the network 94 %.

    Derivation #

    One matrix, two shapes #

    A Mahalanobis distance is a quadratic form, and every quadratic form with a positive semidefinite matrix has a square root. Write the eigendecomposition \(M=V\Lambda V^{\top}\) with \(\Lambda\succeq0\) and set

    \[L=\Lambda^{1/2}V^{\top}, \qquad L^{\top}L=V\Lambda^{1/2}\Lambda^{1/2}V^{\top}=M . \qquad (1)\]

    The factor is not unique — any orthogonal \(Q\) gives \((QL)^{\top}(QL)=M\) — but the distances are. Working with \(L\) turns a constrained problem on the PSD cone into an unconstrained one on rectangular matrices, and computing \(\lVert L(x-z)\rVert^2\) never forms the \(d\times d\) matrix. The code uses both shapes: nca, mvp, and deep_metric optimise a map, while lmnn and itml optimise \(M\) and factor it at the end.

    The PSD constraint is not decoration. Without it, the "distance" can be negative on some directions, the triangle inequality fails, and the kNN vote is decided by a quantity that is not a distance. Every variant either works inside the cone or projects back onto it with

    \[\mathrm{proj}_{\succeq0}(A)=V\max(\Lambda,0)V^{\top}. \qquad (2)\]

    NCA #

    For a point \(i\), assign every other point \(j\) the probability

    \[p_{ij}=\frac{\exp(-d_{ij})}{\sum_{k\ne i}\exp(-d_{ik})}, \qquad d_{ij}=\lVert L(x_i-x_j)\rVert^2, \qquad (3)\]

    and define the objective as the probability mass on same-class neighbours:

    \[P(L)=\sum_i\sum_{j:\,y_j=y_i}p_{ij}. \qquad (4)\]

    This is the expected accuracy of a stochastic nearest-neighbour classifier: with probability \(p_{ij}\), point \(i\) copies the label of \(j\). The exponential makes the objective smooth, and the normalisation makes each row a distribution rather than a sum of independent terms.

    Differentiate. Let \(\Delta_{ij}=x_i-x_j\). Since \(\partial d_{ij}/\partial L=2L\Delta_{ij}\Delta_{ij}^{\top}\) and \(\partial p_{ij}/\partial L=p_{ij}(\partial\log p_{ij}/\partial L)\),

    \[\frac{\partial P}{\partial L} =2L\sum_i\left[ \left(\sum_{j\in C_i}p_{ij}\right)\sum_k p_{ik}\Delta_{ik}\Delta_{ik}^{\top} -\sum_{j\in C_i}p_{ij}\Delta_{ij}\Delta_{ij}^{\top} \right], \qquad (5)\]

    where \(C_i\) is the set of same-class indices. The first bracket is the scatter of all neighbours, scaled by how much of the row's mass is already correct; the second is the scatter of the correct neighbours. Ascending the gradient pulls mass off the wrong neighbours and onto the right ones. The code computes exactly this bracket as scatter and multiplies by \(2L\).

    The objective is not concave in \(L\), so the code takes a gradient step and then halves the rate until the objective improves. A fixed learning rate would work on easy data and stall on hard data; the line search is what makes the variant usable without tuning.

    LMNN #

    LMNN replaces the likelihood with a margin. For each point \(i\), choose its \(k\) nearest same-class neighbours and call them targets, with indicator \(\eta_{ij}=1\). The objective is

    \[\varepsilon(M)=(1-\mu)\sum_{i,j}\eta_{ij}d_{ij} +\mu\sum_{i,j,l}\eta_{ij} \left[1+d_{ij}-d_{il}\right]_+ , \qquad (6)\]

    where \(l\) ranges over different-class points, \([z]_+=\max(z,0)\), and \(\mu\) trades the pull towards targets against the push away from impostors. The first term is a sum of squared distances inside each class; the second punishes any different-class point that comes within one unit of a target.

    With the targets fixed, both terms are convex in \(M\) — the first linear, the second a sum of hinges — so (6) is convex. Its subgradient is

    \[\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial M} =(1-\mu)\sum_{i,j}\eta_{ij}\Delta_{ij}\Delta_{ij}^{\top} +\mu\sum_{i,j,l}\eta_{ij}\left[ \Delta_{il}\Delta_{il}^{\top}-\Delta_{ij}\Delta_{ij}^{\top}\right], \qquad (7)\]

    over the active triples \((i,j,l)\) satisfying \(1+d_{ij}-d_{il}>0\). The code alternates two loops: recompute targets with the current metric, then take projected gradient steps on \(M\), re-evaluating the active set after each step. The alternation is what makes the problem non-convex again — the targets depend on \(M\) — but each inner problem is convex and the outer loop usually settles in a few passes.

    ITML #

    ITML begins with a prior \(M_0\), usually \(I\), and a set of pairs labelled similar (desired distance at most \(u\)) or dissimilar (at least \(l\)). The measure of a metric's distance from the prior is the LogDet divergence,

    \[D_{\mathrm{ld}}(M,M_0) =\mathrm{tr}(M_0^{-1}M)-\log\det(M_0^{-1}M)-d , \qquad (8)\]

    which is the KL divergence between zero-mean Gaussians with covariance matrices \(M\) and \(M_0\), up to a constant. It is convex in \(M\), finite only on positive definite matrices, and its Bregman projection onto a half-space has a rank-one closed form. For one constraint \(v^{\top}Mv\le u\) or \(\ge l\), written as a target \(\delta\), the update is

    \[M\leftarrow M+\beta\,(Mv)(Mv)^{\top}, \qquad \beta=\frac{\delta-p}{p^2}, \qquad p=v^{\top}Mv . \qquad (9)\]

    The identity behind (9) is one line: after the update the distance along \(v\) is \(p+\beta p^2=\delta\), so the constraint is met exactly. The Bregman geometry picks the rank-one direction that changes the metric as little as possible, and \(\beta\) has the sign that moves \(p\) towards \(\delta\): negative for a similar pair that is too far, positive for a dissimilar pair that is too close.

    Cycling through the constraints converges to a point in their intersection, or to a cycle when they are inconsistent. The code makes one pass per max_iter, so the count is the number of updates actually performed, and it projects onto the PSD cone once at the end in case rounding has pushed an eigenvalue below zero.

    MVP #

    Write \(S_w\) for the mean outer product of same-class differences and \(S_b\) for the mean outer product of different-class differences:

    \[S_w=\frac{1}{N_s}\sum_{y_i=y_j}\Delta_{ij}\Delta_{ij}^{\top}, \qquad S_b=\frac{1}{N_d}\sum_{y_i\ne y_j}\Delta_{ij}\Delta_{ij}^{\top}. \qquad (10)\]

    The criterion is the ratio of the two mean squared distances,

    \[r(M)=\frac{\mathrm{tr}(MS_b)}{\mathrm{tr}(MS_w)}, \qquad (11)\]

    and it is homogeneous of degree zero, so only the direction of \(M\) matters. Every stationary point of the ratio is a generalised eigenvector,

    \[S_b u=\lambda S_w u , \qquad (12)\]

    and the maximum is the largest eigenvalue. The metric that attains it is the rank-one projector \(uu^{\top}\). A rank-\(r\) map keeps the span of the top \(r\) eigenvectors instead, and the code weights each direction by its ratio.

    When \(S_w\) is positive definite, whiten it: with \(W=S_w^{-1/2}\) and \(B=W S_b W\), the problem becomes the ordinary eigenproblem for \(B\). If \(B=\sum_k\lambda_k v_kv_k^{\top}\) with eigenvalues in descending order, set \(u_k=Wv_k\). The rank-\(r\) map is

    \[L=\left[\lambda_1^{1/2}u_1,\ldots,\lambda_r^{1/2}u_r\right]^{\top}, \qquad (13)\]

    so \(L^{\top}L=\sum_{k\le r}\lambda_k u_ku_k^{\top}\) weights each kept direction by how well it separates the classes. Dropping the \(\lambda_k\) gives the orthogonal projector onto the same subspace, the other common convention; the subspace is what matters, and the demo's \(r=d\) case keeps every direction and still lifts accuracy from 0.59 to 0.99 because the noisy axes have small between-class variance and large within-class variance, so their eigenvalues are near zero.

    A ridge term \(\rho I\) is added to \(S_w\) before the factorisation. It makes the whitening finite when a direction has no within-class variance, which happens whenever a feature is constant inside every class.

    Deep metric #

    A linear map can rotate and stretch, but it cannot bend. The xor is the smallest example: the classes occupy alternating quadrants, and the boundary between them is two crossing lines. An invertible affine map sends crossing lines to crossing lines, so the classes stay interleaved; no choice of \(M\) turns the problem into two separated blobs. A network can fold the plane.

    The network is two layers with a ReLU, followed by a projection onto the unit sphere. For a triplet of anchor, positive, and negative, the loss is

    \[\ell(\theta)=\left[m+\lVert f_\theta(a)-f_\theta(p)\rVert^2 -\lVert f_\theta(a)-f_\theta(n)\rVert^2\right]_+ , \qquad (14)\]

    and the training objective is the mean over the sampled triples. The gradient with respect to the three embeddings is

    \[\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial f(a)}=2(f(n)-f(p)),\qquad \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial f(p)}=2(f(p)-f(a)),\qquad \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial f(n)}=2(f(a)-f(n)), \qquad (15)\]

    and backpropagation through the sphere projection and the ReLU turns that into parameter gradients. The code samples one positive and one negative per anchor on every epoch, so the negatives change as the embedding moves, and uses Adam with bias correction.

    The projection to the unit sphere is not cosmetic. Without it, rescaling every embedding by \(c\) changes the loss to

    \[m+c^2\left(\lVert a-p\rVert^2-\lVert a-n\rVert^2\right), \qquad (16)\]

    which decreases without bound whenever the negative is farther than the positive: the network can win by inflating the space instead of separating the classes. Fixing the norm removes that direction, and it also makes the margin \(m\) a meaningful angle-like quantity. In two dimensions the embeddings live on a circle, which is a tight budget — enough for the demo's two classes, not enough for a hundred.

    Code walkthrough #

    Projecting onto the PSD cone #

    def _psd(matrix: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Project a symmetric matrix onto the positive semidefinite cone."""
    symmetric = 0.5 * (matrix + matrix.T)
    values, vectors = np.linalg.eigh(symmetric)
    values = np.maximum(values, 0.0)
    return (vectors * values) @ vectors.T

    (2), with the symmetrisation that floating-point arithmetic makes necessary. eigh returns ascending eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors, so clipping the eigenvalues and reassembling gives the nearest PSD matrix in Frobenius norm. lmnn calls this after every trial step; itml calls it once at the end.

    The NCA gradient #

                mass = np.sum(probability * same, axis=1)
            scatter = np.zeros((width, width))
            for index in range(rows):
                offset = points[index] - points
                outer = (probability[index][:, None] * offset).T @ offset
                own = (probability[index] * same[index])[:, None] * offset
                scatter += mass[index] * outer - own.T @ offset
            gradient = 2.0 * map_ @ scatter

    The two matrix products are the two terms of (5). outer is \(\sum_j p_{ij}\Delta_{ij}\Delta_{ij}^{\top}\), the scatter of every neighbour; own keeps only the same-class part; mass is \(\sum_{j\in C_i}p_{ij}\). The loop is over rows because each row has its own normalisation, which is the only place NCA differs from a plain weighted scatter. With \(n\) points and \(d\) features the inner product is \(d\times n\) times \(n\times d\), so the gradient is \(O(n^2d^2)\) and the \(n^2\) weight matrix is the memory ceiling.

    The LMNN active set #

                    across = active.sum(axis=1).astype(float)
                gradient -= self.mu * np.einsum(
                    "nl,nld,nle->de", across, difference, difference)
                within = active.sum(axis=2).astype(float)
                gradient += self.mu * np.einsum(
                    "nk,nkd,nke->de", within, pulled, pulled)

    active has shape \(n\times k\times n\): one entry per target and potential impostor. Summing over the target axis gives, for every pair \((i,l)\), how many targets of \(i\) are violated by \(l\); that count weights the impostor scatter \(\Delta_{il}\Delta_{il}^{\top}\). Summing over the impostor axis gives, for every target \((i,j)\), how many impostors violate it; that count weights the target scatter. The signs are the ones in (7): impostors increase the distance, targets decrease it. A loop over active triples would add exactly the same outer products one at a time; the counts are what let the whole gradient be two einsum calls.

    The ITML update #

                    beta = (target - current) / (current * current)
                metric = metric + beta * np.outer(metric_v, metric_v)
                metric = 0.5 * (metric + metric.T)

    (9), with current equal to \(p\) and metric_v equal to \(Mv\). The update is rank one, so a pass over \(O(n^2)\) pairs costs \(O(n^2d^2)\) but never forms an \(n\times n\) matrix. The symmetrisation is not needed in exact arithmetic; it is cheap insurance against the asymmetry that a long sequence of outer products accumulates.

    The MVP whitening #

            values, vectors = np.linalg.eigh(within)
        values = np.maximum(values, 1e-12)
        root = (vectors / np.sqrt(values)) @ vectors.T
        symmetric = root @ between @ root
        eigenvalues, eigenvectors = np.linalg.eigh(
            0.5 * (symmetric + symmetric.T))

    This is (12) in two eigendecompositions. root is \(S_w^{-1/2}\), built by dividing the eigenvector columns by the square roots of the eigenvalues; the floor keeps a direction with no within-class variance from producing an infinity. symmetric is \(W S_b W\), and its eigenvalues are the ratios \(\lambda_k\) in (13). The code sorts them descending, keeps the positive ones, and maps the eigenvectors back through root — the second eigh is on a symmetric matrix, which is why eigh rather than eig is enough.

    The triplet gradient #

            violation = self.margin + apart - away
        active = violation > 0.0
        loss = float(np.mean(np.maximum(violation, 0.0)))
        if not active.any():
            return [np.zeros_like(part) for part in self.weights_], loss
        chosen = np.flatnonzero(active)
        anchor, positive = anchors[chosen], positives[chosen]
        negative = negatives[chosen]
        grad = np.zeros_like(embedding)
        np.add.at(grad, anchor,
                  2.0 * (embedding[negative] - embedding[positive]))
        np.add.at(grad, positive,
                  2.0 * (embedding[positive] - embedding[anchor]))
        np.add.at(grad, negative,
                  2.0 * (embedding[anchor] - embedding[negative]))
        grad /= anchors.size

    (15), accumulated into a per-point gradient. The np.add.at calls are the important detail: a point can appear as the anchor of one triplet and the negative of another, and ordinary fancy-index addition would write the last contribution and silently drop the rest. The final division is by the number of anchors, because the reported loss is the mean over all triplets, not only the active ones. Once every point has one gradient, the chain rule through the sphere projection is

    \[\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial r} =\frac{1}{\lVert r\rVert}\left(g-f\,(g^{\top}f)\right),\]

    which is exactly the grad - embedding * sum(...) expression in the next few lines, and the ReLU passes the gradient only where the hidden pre-activation was positive. Adam then scales each parameter by the running second moment, which is what makes a single learning rate work for the weights and the biases at once.

    Applying the metric #

            if self.variant == "deep_metric":
            standardised = (points - self._centre) / self._scale
            return self._forward(standardised)[0]
        return points @ self.L_.T

    Both branches produce a point in the learned space, so transform, distance, predict, and score are shared. The linear branch is one matrix product; the deep branch standardises with the training statistics before the forward pass, which is why fit stores _centre and _scale. predict then runs a plain kNN vote on the embedded training rows, so the metric is the only thing that was learned — the classifier stayed the same.

    Complexity #

    Let \(n\) be the training count, \(d\) the features, \(r\) the output dimension, \(k\) the target neighbours, \(h\) the hidden width, and \(e\) the epochs.

    operation time memory
    nca, one iteration \(O(n^2d^2)\) \(O(n^2+nd)\)
    lmnn, one inner step \(O(n^2d^2)\) \(O(n^2d)\)
    itml, one pair pass \(O(n^2d^2)\) \(O(n^2d)\)
    mvp \(O(n^2d^2+d^3)\) \(O(n^2d)\)
    deep_metric, one epoch \(O(nhd+nhr)\) \(O(nh+hd)\)
    transform \(m\) points \(O(mdr)\) or \(O(mhd)\) \(O(mr)\)
    predict \(m\) points \(O(mnr)\) \(O(mn)\)

    The pairwise variants — NCA, LMNN, ITML, and MVP — all form an \(n\times n\) object before they can learn anything, and that is their practical limit. lmnn keeps the whole \(n\times n\times d\) difference tensor, which is the most expensive of the three; mvp could be computed from class means in \(O(nd^2+Kd^2)\) but the code uses pairs to keep the derivation literal; itml streams the pairs in a Python loop, so its constant is large even though its memory is only the pair list. deep_metric is the only variant whose cost is linear in \(n\) per epoch, which is why it is the one that scales to images.

    The demo's counts are modest: lmnn accepts at most 150 inner steps on the 150-point cluster problem and 50 on the 250-point xor; itml visits 11 175 pairs five times on the first and 31 125 pairs five times on the second. Those are operations, not seconds: the demo reports counts rather than timings, so its comparison does not depend on the machine.

    Failure modes #

    Forgetting the scale. A metric can undo a bad unit only if the optimiser sees the bad unit. itml's prior is the identity, so a feature measured in millimetres starts with a hundred times the influence of the same feature in centimetres; lmnn's margin of one is in whatever units the data arrived in; mvp's eigenvectors are not invariant to a diagonal rescaling, and when two eigenvalues are close the rescaling decides which direction wins. Standardise before fitting unless the units are meaningful, and remember that standardising changes the prior as well.

    Too few points per class. NCA needs neighbours to assign probability to, and LMNN needs \(k\) same-class targets. The code clamps the target count to the smallest class, but a class with two points gives one target and no real choice; the learned metric then reflects one pair. The same shortage makes ITML's quantile bounds unstable and MVP's within-class scatter rank deficient.

    The quadratic memory. At \(n=10^5\), an \(n\times n\) matrix is 80 GB in float64. LMNN and ITML are for thousands of points, not millions. The usual remedies are to sample pairs, to use a linear map with a low-rank factorisation, or to switch to a deep embedding trained on mini-batches.

    Non-convexity. NCA and deep_metric have local minima, and the demo shows the consequence: NCA reaches 0.875 on the cluster problem where the closed-form MVP reaches 0.994. Different seeds give different deep embeddings, and a different initialisation can give a different metric. Average over seeds or validate the metric on held-out pairs; do not read a single run as the method's quality.

    A collapsed or overfit metric. A learned \(M\) has \(d(d+1)/2\) parameters. With \(n\) small it can satisfy every training pair and still be arbitrary on new ones; the symptom is near-perfect training accuracy and a test accuracy no better than Euclidean. The deep variant adds a second failure: if the normalization is removed, the loss is unbounded below and the embedding inflates; if the margin is too large, every triplet stays active and the embedding collapses onto a few directions. Keep a validation split of pairs, not just of points.

    Judging a metric by its own objective. LMNN's objective can fall while kNN accuracy falls, because the objective is a surrogate with a fixed margin and fixed targets. ITML's objective_ is a log determinant, not an error. The demo therefore reports test accuracy after a separate kNN vote, and the doc's numbers are those accuracies.

    Using the wrong head. A learned metric does not turn kNN into a parametric classifier. The vote still has a \(k\), still ties, and still fails when a class has no nearby example. If the downstream model is a linear SVM, the metric's job is different from what a kNN objective rewards, and the two can disagree.

    Evaluating on the pairs that trained the metric. A pair used in the ITML constraints is not evidence that the metric generalises, and a triplet used in the deep loss is not a test triplet. Split by identity or by group, not just by row, whenever the same subject appears more than once.

    Applications #

    Face and vehicle verification. The triplet loss with a normalized embedding is the standard recipe: train on identities, then verify by a threshold on the distance between two embeddings. FaceNet made the margin and the mining scheme famous; the same pattern appears in every retrieval system that has to decide whether two images are the same thing.

    Speaker recognition. x-vectors are embeddings trained with a metric-like objective and scored with a learned backend; PLDA is a probabilistic linear metric on the same embeddings. The pipeline is the same shape as this atom: a map, a distance, and a threshold.

    Recommendation and ranking. A user-item pair is close if the user will interact with the item. Learned metrics on user and item features are the retrieval half of a recommender, and the same pull-together/push-apart pattern appears in implicit-feedback models under the name of a contrastive loss.

    Clustering and anomaly detection. A metric learned from a small labelled subset makes k-means or a nearest-neighbour anomaly score respect the labels without changing the algorithm. One-class models elsewhere in the book become much stronger when the distance is learned from normal examples.

    Science and engineering with a prior. ITML is useful when the prior is physical: a Mahalanobis metric from a covariance matrix, updated with a handful of expert constraints. The LogDet divergence keeps the update interpretable as a change of Gaussian, which matters when the metric is going into a report rather than a leaderboard.

    Few-shot learning. Prototypical networks and their relatives learn an embedding on the base classes and classify a new class by the distance to its prototype. That is nearest-centroid in a learned metric, and it is the reason metric learning is a standard baseline for low-data vision.

    Dimensionality reduction. MVP with a rank smaller than \(d\) is linear discriminant analysis: the same whitening and eigendecomposition, used for visualisation or as a preprocessing step. The difference from PCA is exactly the label-aware part — PCA keeps the loudest directions, MVP keeps the directions that separate the classes.
