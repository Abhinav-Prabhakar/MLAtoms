    Learning vector quantisation — prototypes that move under supervision

    Intuition #

    k-nearest neighbours stores the training set and calls it a model. Learning vector quantisation stores a dozen labelled points and calls it a model: the prediction is

    \[\hat y(x)=y_{\,c},\qquad c=\arg\min_k\ \lVert x-w_k\rVert^2, \qquad (1)\]

    and the whole algorithm is a rule for moving the \(w_k\). The labels are attached to prototypes and never change, so each prototype is a small, human-readable statement — "this is what class 2 looks like here" — and the decision boundary is the Voronoi diagram of a few dozen points.

    Training is competitive: for each sample, the nearest prototype wins the right to move, and supervision decides the direction. A winner whose label matches the sample is pulled towards it; a winner whose label is wrong is pushed away. That single sentence is LVQ1, and every other variant in this file is a refinement of which prototypes move and how far.

    There are two eras here and the file covers both. Kohonen's LVQ1, LVQ2.1 and LVQ3 are heuristics: plausible local updates with no objective, no convergence proof, and a window parameter. GLVQ defines a cost per sample and derives the update as its gradient, which turns the heuristic into an optimisation; GRLVQ then attaches a relevance weight to every axis and learns the metric as well. The difference is measurable. In the demo the first four variants all score 0.62 on a linear boundary buried under six axes with twice the spread — they are Euclidean and cannot know which axes matter — while GRLVQ scores 0.98 with a relevance vector of \((0.73, 0.69, 0, \dots, 0)\).

    Variants #

    variant which prototypes move ingredient metric
    lvq1 the nearest one its label fixed
    lvq2 the two nearest distance window fixed
    lvq3 the two nearest, plus a shrink window and \(\epsilon\) fixed
    glvq nearest correct and nearest wrong gradient of \(\mu\) fixed
    grlvq same two gradient of \(\mu\) learned diagonal

    lvq1 is one prototype per class in the simplest case and the default to start from. It is fast, it never diverges for \(\alpha<1\), and it inherits every weakness of Euclidean distance.

    lvq2 (properly LVQ2.1) only updates when the sample falls in a window between the nearest correct and nearest wrong prototype. The idea is that a point deep inside a class carries no boundary information, so only near-boundary samples should move anything. On easy data the window almost never opens: the demo records 3 updates out of 7 200 samples.

    lvq3 adds the missing case. When the two nearest prototypes are both of the correct class, both are shrunk towards the sample by a small factor \(\epsilon\in[0.1,0.5]\), which stops prototypes from drifting apart in regions where they agree. It is the version Kohonen recommended for multi-prototype classes.

    glvq replaces the winner-take-all rule with the cost of (7) and its exact gradient. Every update now has a sign and a magnitude that come from differentiating something, and the epoch cost is a diagnostic rather than a guess.

    grlvq puts a relevance \(\lambda_j\) on each axis and learns it by the same gradient. It is the variant to use when the features have unequal importance and you want the model to say which ones matter: the relevance vector is the answer, and it is directly interpretable.

    Derivation #

    Setup #

    Prototypes \(w_1,\dots,w_P\in\mathbb R^d\) carry labels \(\ell_k\in\{1,\dots,K\}\), seeded from the training data (one or more rows per class). Define \(d_k(x)=\lVert x-w_k\rVert^2\) for the Euclidean variants and the weighted form of (8) for GRLVQ. Prediction is (1). The learning rate is annealed linearly,

    \[\alpha(t)=\alpha_0\Big(1-\frac{t}{T}\Big), \qquad (2)\]

    so the last epoch takes steps of \(\alpha_0/T\) and the prototypes settle instead of oscillating.

    LVQ1 #

    For a sample \((x,y)\) let \(c=\arg\min_k d_k(x)\) and \(s=+1\) if \(\ell_c=y\), \(-1\) otherwise. Then

    \[w_c\ \leftarrow\ w_c+\alpha\,s\,(x-w_c), \qquad (3)\]

    which is \((1-\alpha)w_c+\alpha x\) on a match and \((1+\alpha)w_c-\alpha x\) on a miss. For a match the prototype is an exponential moving average of the samples it wins; for a miss it steps away from \(x\) by the same fraction. The demo asserts both closed forms: \((0.5,1.0)\) from \((0,0)\) with \(\alpha=0.5\) and \(x=(1,2)\), and \((3.25,3.25)\) from \((3,3)\) with a mismatched sample at \((2.5,2.5)\).

    Because only the winner moves, the prototypes partition the space between them and each one specialises. The cost is that a single outlier can drag a prototype across a boundary and there is no objective to say so.

    The window: LVQ2.1 and LVQ3 #

    Let \(c\) be the nearest prototype of the correct class and \(r\) the nearest of any other class. LVQ2.1 updates only when the sample lies in the window

    \[s<\frac{\min(d_c,d_r)}{\max(d_c,d_r)}\le 1, \qquad s\approx 0.7, \qquad (4)\]

    and then moves both, in opposite directions:

    \[w_c\leftarrow w_c+\alpha(x-w_c),\qquad w_r\leftarrow w_r-\alpha(x-w_r). \qquad (5)\]

    The ratio test is a cheap proxy for "the sample is near the boundary". A point with \(d_c/d_r=0.06\) is deep inside class \(c\); moving \(w_r\) towards it would be noise. The demo's constructed pair gives 0.0625 (no move) and 0.818 (inside, total movement 1.00), which is exactly the window doing its job.

    LVQ3 keeps (5) and adds the case where the two nearest prototypes are both correct:

    \[w_k\leftarrow w_k+\epsilon\,\alpha\,(x-w_k), \qquad k\in\{\text{two nearest}\},\ \ \epsilon\in[0.1,0.5]. \qquad (6)\]

    Without it, two prototypes of one class can drift apart and leave a hole that a wrong prototype fills. With it, they cohere.

    GLVQ #

    Define the per-sample cost from the two distances

    \[\mu(x)=\frac{d_+(x)-d_-(x)}{d_+(x)+d_-(x)}\in[-1,1], \qquad (7)\]

    where \(d_+\) is the distance to the nearest correct prototype and \(d_-\) the distance to the nearest wrong one. Minimising \(\mu\) pulls the correct prototype closer than the wrong one; the floor \(-1\) is reached when the sample sits exactly on its correct prototype. Differentiating (7) with \(d_k=\lVert x-w_k\rVert^2\),

    \[\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial w_+}=-\frac{4d_-}{(d_++d_-)^2}(x-w_+),\qquad \frac{\partial\mu}{\partial w_-}=+\frac{4d_+}{(d_++d_-)^2}(x-w_-), \qquad (8)\]

    so a gradient step is

    \[w_+\leftarrow w_++\eta\frac{4d_-}{s^2}(x-w_+),\qquad w_-\leftarrow w_--\eta\frac{4d_+}{s^2}(x-w_-), \qquad s=d_++d_-. \qquad (9)\]

    The factor 4 is not cosmetic: the derivative of the squared distance brings a 2, and the quotient brings another 2. The demo checks (8) against a finite difference and gets \(-0.369822\) from both. It also checks the floor of the cost path: on the blobs GLVQ drives the epoch mean from \(-0.9090\) to \(-0.9232\).

    GRLVQ #

    Replace the Euclidean distance by a diagonal metric with relevance vector \(\lambda\in\mathbb R^d_{\ge0}\), \(\lVert\lambda\rVert_2=1\):

    \[d^\lambda(x,w)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_j^2\,(x_j-w_j)^2 . \qquad (10)\]

    The squared relevance is the weight, so \(\lambda_j\) is a scale and \(\lambda_j^2\) a share of the metric. The prototype update is (9) with \(d^\lambda\) and with each coordinate additionally multiplied by \(\lambda_j^2\); differentiating (7) in \(\lambda\) gives

    \[\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial\lambda_j} =\frac{4\lambda_j}{s^2}\Big(d_-^\lambda(x_j-w_{+j})^2 -d_+^\lambda(x_j-w_{-j})^2\Big), \qquad (11)\]

    and the relevance takes a descent step followed by projection onto the non-negative unit sphere:

    \[\lambda\leftarrow \frac{\max\big(\lambda-\eta_\lambda\nabla_\lambda\mu,\ 0\big)} {\big\lVert\max(\lambda-\eta_\lambda\nabla_\lambda\mu,\ 0)\big\rVert_2}. \qquad (12)\]

    The bracket in (11) is the whole mechanism: an axis on which the wrong prototype is closer than the correct one contributes a positive gradient and loses relevance, and an axis on which the correct prototype wins gains it. On the demo's buried linear boundary the six noise axes end at exactly zero and the two signal axes at \(0.73\) and \(0.69\).

    One check makes the relationship between the two variants exact: at uniform \(\lambda_j=1/\sqrt d\) the weighted distance is \(1/d\) times the Euclidean one, so \(s^2\) gains \(1/d^2\) and \(\lambda_j^2\) gains \(1/d\); in (9) the factors cancel and the GRLVQ step equals the GLVQ step. The demo asserts that to machine precision, which is why GRLVQ can be read as "GLVQ plus directions" rather than as a different algorithm.

    Code walkthrough #

    Prototypes are seeded per class from the training rows, using the same seeded generator that later shuffles the epochs:

            for code in range(classes.size):
            members = np.flatnonzero(codes == code)
            if members.size < self.prototypes_per_class:
                raise ValueError("a class has fewer rows than prototypes requested")
            chosen = rng.choice(members, self.prototypes_per_class, replace=False)

    Choosing rows rather than class means matters: a prototype is then an actual example, which is what makes the model readable, and GRLVQ's relevance is learned from distances between real points instead of a synthetic centroid.

    The distance function is where the metric enters. Two branches, one einsum each, and no materialised \((P,n,d)\) cube beyond the temporary:

            diff = X[:, None, :] - self.prototypes_[None, :, :]
        if self.relevance_ is None:
            return np.einsum("npd,npd->np", diff, diff)
        return np.einsum("d,npd,npd->np", self.relevance_ ** 2, diff, diff)

    Squaring the relevance in the second branch is the convention of (10); using \(\lambda_j\) directly would make a negative relevance indistinguishable from a positive one of the same size.

    LVQ1 is (3) in five lines, including the sign:

            d = self.distances(x[None])[0]
        k = int(np.argmin(d))
        delta = rate * (x - self.prototypes_[k])
        if self.prototype_codes_[k] == label:
            self.prototypes_[k] += delta
        else:
            self.prototypes_[k] -= delta

    Computing delta once and flipping the sign is the difference between (3) and two separate code paths that can drift apart. Note that d is recomputed for every sample: the prototypes change after each update, so caching distances across samples is wrong, not merely slower.

    LVQ2.1's window test is a ratio of the two smallest distances, computed by sorting rather than by two argmin passes:

            first, second = np.argsort(d)[:2]
        near, far = d[first], d[second]
...
        if self.prototype_codes_[first] == self.prototype_codes_[second]:
            return 0.0
        if near / far <= self.window:
            return 0.0
        correct = first if self.prototype_codes_[first] == label else second
        if self.prototype_codes_[correct] != label:
            return 0.0
        wrong = second if correct == first else first
        self.prototypes_[correct] += rate * (x - self.prototypes_[correct])
        self.prototypes_[wrong] -= rate * (x - self.prototypes_[wrong])

    The near + far <= 0 guard above these lines is not paranoia: a sample sitting exactly on a prototype makes the ratio 0/0. The correct/wrong resolution through the labels, rather than assuming first is right, is what lets the same block serve LVQ2.1 and LVQ3.

    The \(\epsilon\) term of (6) is the only difference between those two variants, and it lives in the branch where both nearest prototypes are correct:

            if both_correct:
            if self.variant == "lvq3" and self.epsilon > 0.0:
                for k in (first, second):
                    self.prototypes_[k] += self.epsilon * rate * (
                        x - self.prototypes_[k])

    GLVQ and GRLVQ share the search for the two operative prototypes, which is worth reading closely: np.where(same, d, inf) and np.where(same, inf, d) select the nearest correct and nearest wrong in the same pass, with no Python loop and no risk of picking the same prototype twice.

            plus = int(np.argmin(np.where(same, d, np.inf)))
        minus = int(np.argmin(np.where(same, np.inf, d)))
        total = d[plus] + d[minus]
...
        toward = x - self.prototypes_[plus]
        away = x - self.prototypes_[minus]
        weight = 4.0 * rate / total ** 2

    toward and away are computed before either prototype is mutated, and that ordering is the one subtle line in the GRLVQ branch. The relevance gradient needs the pre-update differences; computing it after the prototype step would differentiate the wrong point.

                lam_sq = self.relevance_ ** 2
            self.prototypes_[plus] += weight * d[minus] * lam_sq * toward
            self.prototypes_[minus] -= weight * d[plus] * lam_sq * away
            gradient = (4.0 * self.relevance_ / total ** 2) * (
                d[minus] * toward ** 2 - d[plus] * away ** 2)
            proposal = np.maximum(self.relevance_ - relevance_rate * gradient,
                                  0.0)
            norm = float(np.linalg.norm(proposal))
            if norm > 0.0:
                self.relevance_ = proposal / norm

    The projection is (12): clip at zero, then divide by the norm. Skipping the renormalisation lets the relevance vector grow without bound — the gradient is homogeneous, so nothing else constrains its scale — and the metric then collapses onto whichever axis accumulated the most mass first.

    Prediction is a nearest-prototype lookup and nothing else, which is the point of the atom:

            d = self.distances(X)
        return self.classes_[self.prototype_codes_[np.argmin(d, axis=1)]]

    prototype_codes_ holds integer class indices and classes_ maps them back, so the labels can be strings without any per-sample conversion.

    Complexity #

    With \(n\) samples, \(d\) features, \(P\) prototypes, \(K\) classes, \(T\) epochs:

    • One update: \(O(Pd)\) to find the nearest prototype, plus \(O(d)\) to move it. GRLVQ adds \(O(d)\) for the relevance step.
    • One epoch: \(O(nPd)\); the whole fit is \(O(TnPd)\).
    • Memory: \(O(Pd)\) for the prototypes, \(O(1)\) per sample. Seeds and shuffles are \(O(n)\).
    • Against the chapter: this is the cheapest atom in it. Kernel ridge is \(O(n^3)\), metric learning forms an \(n\times n\) matrix, and even kNN pays \(O(nd)\) memory. LVQ pays for that with a fixed-capacity model: \(P\) prototypes cannot represent more than \(P\) prototypes' worth of boundary.

    The demo's largest fit is 300 samples with 4 prototypes over 30 epochs, which is 36 000 distance rows — less than one \(200\times200\) kernel matrix.

    Failure modes #

    The window closes and the model stops learning. On well-separated classes LVQ2.1 and LVQ3 update on almost nothing: 3 of 7 200 samples in the demo, with the accuracy essentially LVQ1's. If n_updates_ is tiny, the window variants are LVQ1 with two extra hyperparameters. Diagnose by counting updates; fix by seeding more prototypes per class or by accepting that the data has no boundary to refine.

    Prototype initialisation decides the outcome. LVQ1/2/3 have no objective, and even GLVQ's cost has local minima. A prototype seeded on an outlier can stay there for the whole fit, because it only moves when it wins and it wins only nearby samples. A control run outside the demo — a 2-d XOR embedded in six loud axes — recovered the two signal axes on three training seeds out of five and stalled at chance on the other two, with relevance mass spread over the noise. Diagnose with several seeds and a held-out score; the fix is more prototypes or a relevance warm-up, not a smaller learning rate.

    Euclidean blindness, and the diagonal metric that only partly fixes it. LVQ1 and GLVQ score 0.62 on the demo's buried boundary because six axes with twice the spread dominate the distance. GRLVQ fixes that case, but its metric is diagonal: it can down-weight an axis, not rotate the space, so two informative axes that are strongly correlated are still seen as two axes. When the useful directions are oblique, the full matrix of metric_learning is the right tool.

    A learning rate that does not decay. With \(\alpha=1\) a prototype lands exactly on the sample and forgets everything else; with a constant \(\alpha\) the last epoch undoes part of the previous one. The schedule in (2) is not a formality — it is what makes the final prototypes an average rather than a snapshot.

    Applications #

    LVQ was built for phoneme recognition and handwritten digit classification, and it remains a strong choice when the model must be small and inspectable: a few dozen prototypes can be printed, plotted and argued about with a domain expert, which no kernel machine or forest offers. In clinical and industrial settings that readability is often the deciding feature.

    GRLVQ's relevance vector is a feature-ranking device. Training it on a high-dimensional panel and reading off the nonzero \(\lambda_j\) is a supervised screening step that accounts for interactions through the prototypes, and it costs a fraction of a wrapper search. The same idea underlies divergence-based LVQ, where the metric is chosen to match the data's statistics rather than the Euclidean prior.

    As a baseline, one prototype per class is the cheapest classifier worth reporting — it is nearest-centroid with a learned instead of an averaged position — and it often beats logistic regression on small, well-separated problems.

    It is a trap when the classes are not convex and \(P\) is small: prototypes tile the space with Voronoi cells, so a class that wraps around another needs many prototypes. It is also a trap for high-stakes probability estimates: LVQ outputs a label and a distance, not a calibrated posterior, and the distance is not a confidence.
