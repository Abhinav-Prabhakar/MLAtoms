    Sampling

    Intuition #

    Sampling is how a distribution becomes data. Four different jobs get called "sampling", and mixing them up is the usual source of subtle bugs:

    1. Draw from a known distribution — inverse CDF, alias, rejection.
    2. Draw from a stream you cannot store — reservoir.
    3. Draw without replacement or from a truncated distribution — Gumbel top-k, nucleus, negative sampling.
    4. Estimate an expectation with fewer draws — stratified, systematic, importance sampling.

    The first three must be exact; the fourth is allowed to be biased or higher-variance as long as the estimator is right. Keeping that distinction explicit is what separates a sampler from a heuristic.

    Variants #

    Variant Draws from Cost per draw The property that earns it a slot
    uniform U(0,1) O(1) Inverse-CDF template for every continuous law.
    weighted_alias categorical O(1) after O(k) build Constant time regardless of vocabulary size.
    reservoir uniform subset of a stream O(1) amortised, O(k) memory Unknown length, one pass.
    stratified one per stratum O(n) Kills the between-stratum component of variance.
    systematic one per stratum, shared offset O(n) Stratification with a single random number.
    gumbel_top_k categorical, without replacement O(k log k) Exact Plackett-Luce via a sort.
    top_p truncated categorical O(k log k) Truncation adapts to model confidence.
    negative_sampling categorical, excluding positives O(log k) (alias) Cheap, unbiased-ish negatives at scale.
    importance_sampling expectation under p, samples from q O(n) Lets you sample from the wrong distribution.
    rejection arbitrary density O(M/area) proposals Works when no inverse CDF exists.

    Derivation #

    Alias method. Split the unit interval into k columns of width 1/k, one per item, and give item i a mass k·w_i (so the total is k). Columns heavier than 1 donate their excess to columns lighter than 1; each light column is then "filled" by exactly one donor. A draw picks a column uniformly and accepts it with probability prob[i], otherwise takes alias[i]. Both the acceptance and the fallback are O(1), and the table is built in O(k) by the two-list sweep in alias_table.

    The invariant worth testing is not Σ prob[i] = k (it is not, in general). What holds is that the implied distribution equals the weights:

    \[\hat w_i = \frac{1}{k}\left(\text{prob}[i] + \sum_{s:\,\text{alias}[s]=i}\bigl(1-\text{prob}[s]\bigr)\right) = w_i\]

    which is exactly what the demo checks.

    Reservoir (Algorithm R). Claim: after processing i items, each is in the reservoir with probability k/i. Base case i = k is clear. Inductive step: item i+1 enters with probability k/(i+1); for an item already present, it survives with probability

    \[\Bigl(1-\tfrac{k}{i+1}\Bigr) + \tfrac{k}{i+1}\cdot\tfrac{k-1}{k} = \frac{i}{i+1}\]

    so its inclusion probability is (k/i)·(i/(i+1)) = k/(i+1). No length is needed anywhere — that is the whole point.

    Gumbel-max. For logits z, let G_i ~ Gumbel(0,1) i.i.d. Then

    \[\Pr\!\left[\arg\max_i (z_i + G_i) = j\right] = \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_i e^{z_i}}\]

    Proof sketch: argmax = j iff z_j + G_j > max_{i≠j}(z_i + G_i), and the max of independent Gumbels is Gumbel with the log-sum-exp of the offsets. Taking the top-k instead of the top-1 yields the Plackett-Luce distribution: sample the first item proportionally to w, remove it, repeat. That identity is why top-k/top-p decoding is a sort, not a sequence of categorical draws, and why the same code is differentiable-friendly under the Gumbel-softmax relaxation.

    Nucleus (top-p). Sort probabilities descending, keep the shortest prefix with cumulative mass ≥ p, renormalise, draw once. The truncation is a function of the distribution's own shape: a peaked model keeps one token, a flat one keeps most of the vocabulary. Top-k cannot do that with a fixed k.

    Rejection. Propose x ~ g, accept with probability f(x)/(M g(x)) for f(x) ≤ M g(x). The accepted points are distributed as f, and the acceptance rate is area(f)/(M·area(g)). For the demo's f(x)=2x, g=U(0,1), M=2, the rate is exactly 1/2, so half the proposals are wasted — the price of not having an inverse CDF.

    Importance sampling. For any q with q(x) > 0 wherever p(x)f(x) ≠ 0:

    \[\mathbb{E}_p[f] = \int f(x)\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}q(x)\,dx = \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[f(x)\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right]\]

    The estimator is unbiased but its variance is E_q[(f·p/q)²] − E_p[f]², which explodes when q is much narrower than p (the weights grow without bound). The diagnostic is effective sample size, ESS = (Σw)²/Σw²: below ~10% of n, the estimate is noise.

    Stratified vs systematic. Partition the population into n strata and draw n_i from each. The variance decomposition

    \[\mathrm{Var}(\bar X) = \underbrace{\sum_i \frac{\sigma_i^2}{n_i}}_{\text{within}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_i (\mu_i - \mu)^2}_{\text{between}}\]

    shows the gain: stratification removes the between-stratum term entirely. With n_i = 1 in every stratum (the demo's case) the estimate is unbiased with variance O(1/n²) for a smooth population, versus O(1/n) for i.i.d. draws — a factor of n fewer samples for the same error. Systematic sampling is stratified with n_i = 1 and one shared u, which is n−1 fewer random numbers.

    Code walkthrough #

    The file is split into "exact draws" and "designs and estimators", and the split is functional: everything above # Designs returns samples from a specified law, everything below returns indices or estimates whose bias is a design choice.

    alias_table is the only non-trivial build, and it is a two-list sweep:

        k = w.size
    p = w / w.sum() * k
    prob = np.zeros(k)
    alias = np.arange(k)
    small = [i for i in range(k) if p[i] < 1.0]
    large = [i for i in range(k) if p[i] >= 1.0]
    while small and large:
        s = small.pop()
        ell = large.pop()
        prob[s] = p[s]
        alias[s] = ell
        p[ell] -= 1.0 - p[s]
        (small if p[ell] < 1.0 else large).append(ell)

    p is the mass in units of 1/k, so the total is k, not 1. A column with p[i] < 1 keeps p[i] and is filled by one donor ell, which gives up 1 − p[s] of its mass; the donor's remaining mass shrinks and it re-enters whichever list it now belongs to. The line prob[s] = p[s] records the keep probability, and alias[s] = ell records where the remainder goes. Each pair removes one element from small, so the loop runs at most k times.

    The trailing line is easy to dismiss:

        for i in large + small:  # floating-point leftovers
        prob[i] = 1.0

    After the loop, any remaining entry is within 1e-16 of 1; setting prob = 1 makes it "always keep". Dropping the line leaves prob[i] = 0 for those indices, so they are never kept and always route to alias[i] — a bias of order 1e-16 per item that is invisible in a histogram and reproducible in a seed. The demo checks the implied distribution, not Σprob, because Σprob = k is false in general:

        implied = prob / w.size
    np.add.at(implied, alias, (1.0 - prob) / w.size)
    assert np.allclose(implied, w / w.sum()), (implied, w / w.sum())

    np.add.at again, for the same reason as in metrics.py: several columns can alias to the same donor, and buffered += would count only the last one. This three-line reconstruction is the whole correctness proof in code form.

    weighted_alias is two vectorised draws and a select:

        i = rng.integers(0, k, size=n)
    take_alias = rng.random(n) >= prob[i]
    return np.where(take_alias, alias[i], i)

    Column i is uniform; with probability prob[i] keep i, otherwise take alias[i]. >= rather than > matches "accept with probability prob" only up to a measure-zero event, but it matters for reproducibility across RNGs that can return exactly 0.

    reservoir is five lines and the off-by-one is the entire algorithm:

        for i, x in enumerate(stream):
        if i < k:
            res.append(x)
        else:
            j = int(rng.integers(0, i + 1))
            if j < k:
                res[j] = x

    At item index i (0-based), there are i+1 items seen, and the new one must enter with probability k/(i+1), which requires j uniform on 0..i inclusive — rng.integers(0, i+1). Writing rng.integers(0, i) gives i outcomes instead of i+1, so later items are over-represented relative to early ones; the sample is still "random-looking" and only an inclusion-frequency test (the demo's 20k trials) reveals it.

    gumbel_top_k turns a categorical draw into an argsort:

        u = np.clip(rng.random(z.shape), 1e-300, 1.0 - 1e-16)
    g = -np.log(-np.log(u))
    return np.argsort(-(z + g), kind="stable")[:k]

    u must be clipped at both ends: u = 0 gives log(0) = −inf and then −log(inf) = −inf; u = 1 gives −log(0) = +inf. Either produces an infinite perturbation and the corresponding item is always first (or produces nan via inf − inf when two collide). The bounds are not symmetric because the float nearest to 1 is 1 − 2⁻⁵³, not 1 − 1e-16; the clip is a safety net, not a precision guarantee. kind="stable" matters when two perturbed scores tie exactly: a non-stable sort can reorder equal scores and break Plackett-Luce's exchangeability. The top-k of one Gumbel draw is not k independent draws — it is exactly the without-replacement distribution, which is why the demo checks the pair probabilities and not just the marginals.

    rejection batches proposals because Python-level loops are the bottleneck:

        while filled < n:
        batch = max(64, 2 * (n - filled))
        x = rng.uniform(lo, hi, size=batch)
        u = rng.uniform(0.0, envelope, size=batch)
        acc = x[u < pdf(x)]

    u ~ U(0, envelope) and accept when u < pdf(x): the accepted x has density pdf/envelope, so after normalising over [lo,hi] it is pdf/area. If envelope is not a true upper bound the accepted sample is not from pdf — it is from min(pdf, envelope), silently truncated. The batch factor 2× is a guess at the acceptance rate; if the rate is far below 1/2 the loop runs many times, which is correct but slow.

    Stratified and systematic are the same idea with one random number versus n:

        bounds = np.linspace(0, population_size, n + 1)
    u = rng.random(n)
    return np.floor(bounds[:-1] + u * (bounds[1:] - bounds[:-1])).astype(int)

        step = population_size / n
    start = rng.random() * step
    return np.floor(start + step * np.arange(n)).astype(int)

    Both return indices into a population of size population_size, so the last stratum is [bounds[-2], bounds[-1]) and the floor is safe. systematic differs by using a single start for every stratum; the spacing is exactly step, so np.unique(s // step) has n elements by construction — the demo asserts this rather than checking a mean. When population_size is not divisible by n, the last index can be population_size (out of range) if start is close to step; the demo uses a divisible case, and production code must clamp.

    top_p is three lines of sorting and the +1 is the off-by-one:

        order = np.argsort(-q)
    cum = np.cumsum(q[order])
    keep = int(np.searchsorted(cum, p) + 1)

    searchsorted(cum, p) with the default side="left" returns the first index where cum[i] >= p, which is the zero-based count minus one, so +1 converts it to a count. If p is larger than the total mass (impossible after normalisation) it returns q.size, and +1 would overflow — hence the min(keep, q.size). The draw is over the renormalised support:

        w = q[support]
    return int(support[rng.choice(keep, p=w / w.sum())])

    Forgetting w / w.sum() and passing w directly is the failure mode the doc names: np.random.choice requires probabilities that sum to 1 and raises, so that one is loud; the quiet version is sampling from w after truncation without renormalising by hand and reusing the original p, which biases toward the head.

    negative_sampling is one exponentiation and a zeroing:

        w = np.asarray(counts, dtype=float) ** exponent
    w[np.asarray(positives, dtype=int)] = 0.0
    w = w / w.sum()

    counts**0.75 flattens Zipf; exponent=1 is exact unigram and over-samples the head. Zeroing after the exponent is irrelevant numerically but keeps the normaliser consistent. If every item is positive, w.sum() == 0 and the division yields nan, which rng.choice rejects — the demo always leaves at least one negative, and real code should assert that.

    importance_sampling is deliberately plain:

        weights = np.asarray(p(x), dtype=float) / np.asarray(q(x), dtype=float)
    fx = x if f is None else f(x)
    return float(np.mean(fx * weights))

    No self-normalisation, no clipping. The estimator is unbiased by construction, and the failure is variance, not bias: when q is narrow, a single weight can dominate the mean. The demo's p(t)=2t, q=U(0,1) has bounded weights 2t ≤ 2, so 200k draws land within 0.005; a Gaussian q centred away from the mass of p would need an ESS report to look trustworthy.

    Complexity #

    Routine Time Space
    uniform O(n) O(n)
    alias_table O(k) build O(k)
    weighted_alias O(k + n) O(k)
    reservoir O(N) O(k)
    stratified / systematic O(n) O(n)
    gumbel_top_k O(k log k) O(k)
    top_p O(k log k) O(k)
    negative_sampling O(log k) per draw with an alias table O(k)
    importance_sampling O(n) O(n)
    rejection O(n·M/area) expected O(n)

    The headline: alias turns categorical sampling from O(k) into O(1), and that constant is why it is the default in word2vec and in large-scale recommenders.

    Failure modes #

    1. Alias with floating-point leftovers. The two-list sweep can leave a column with p ≈ 1 ± 1e-16 in both lists; the trailing for i in large + small matters. Drop it and the last item is under-sampled by a tiny, untraceable amount.
    2. Reservoir off-by-one. j = randint(0, i+1) uses the count including the current item; randint(0, i) makes later items too likely. The symptom is a bias toward the head of the stream — invisible unless you test inclusion frequencies.
    3. Top-p without renormalising. Sampling from q_i over the truncated support without dividing by the kept mass turns a decoding heuristic into a sampling bug; the model emits <eos> less often than intended.
    4. Negative sampling with exponent=1. Exact unigram sampling spends almost all draws on the few most frequent items, so rare items never become negatives. The 3/4 exponent is a deliberate, well-tested bias.
    5. Importance sampling with a mismatched q. Unbiased in theory, useless in practice when the weights are dominated by a handful of samples. Always report ESS, and prefer a defensive q with heavier tails than p.
    6. Rejection without a valid envelope. If M is not a true upper bound, the accepted distribution is silently wrong (not just slow). Check max(f/g) on a dense grid before trusting the sampler.

    Applications #

    • Decoding: top_p (nucleus) and gumbel_top_k are the two standard LLM samplers; the Gumbel view is what makes them a single sort on device.
    • Embeddings: negative_sampling with the 3/4 exponent, plus the alias table for O(1) draws over a 10⁵–10⁶ vocabulary.
    • Streaming: reservoir sampling for unbiased evaluation sets, log sampling, and "keep a uniform sample of everything I have seen" counters.
    • Monte Carlo: stratified/systematic for quasi-Monte-Carlo integrals and variance reduction; importance sampling for rare-event estimation and for off-policy evaluation.
    • Distributed training: systematic sampling to shard without overlap — one offset per epoch, exactly one draw per shard.
