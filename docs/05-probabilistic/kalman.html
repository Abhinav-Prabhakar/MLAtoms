    05 · Probabilistic models/tier 2/6 variants/source

    Kalman filter

    Intuition #

    A hidden state drifts; a noisy sensor reports on it. Both halves are Gaussian, so the posterior stays Gaussian, and a Gaussian is two numbers. That is the whole reason this filter is forty lines instead of an MCMC run: the belief never needs a representation more complicated than a mean and a covariance.

    Two sentences drive the recursion. Predict is the law of total expectation: the state moves through the dynamics, so the mean moves through \(A\) and the covariance is pushed through \(A\) and inflated by process noise \(Q\). Update is conditioning on one observation: among all state values consistent with the prediction, weight them by how well they would have predicted what you saw.

    \[m_t^{-} = A m_{t-1}, \qquad P_t^{-} = A P_{t-1} A^{\top} + Q \qquad (1)\]
    \[S_t = C P_t^{-} C^{\top} + R, \qquad K_t = P_t^{-} C^{\top} S_t^{-1} \qquad (2)\]
    \[m_t = m_t^{-} + K_t\,(z_t - C m_t^{-}), \qquad P_t = P_t^{-} - K_t C P_t^{-} \qquad (3)\]

    \(S_t\) is the covariance of the innovation \(z_t - C m_t^{-}\): the observation noise \(R\) plus the state uncertainty mapped into observation space. \(K_t\) is the fraction of the innovation you accept. When \(R\) is tiny the filter believes the sensor and \(P\) collapses; when \(P^{-}\) is tiny it believes the model and \(K \to 0\).

    The filter is worth more than its estimates, because the same recursion gives the likelihood. Each innovation is Gaussian under the model,

    \[p(z_{1..T}) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} N\!\left(z_t;\; C m_t^{-},\, S_t\right) \qquad (4)\]

    so the log-likelihood is a running sum with no extra work. That is how the parameters \(Q\) and \(R\) are usually fitted, and it is why the demo checks (4) against the exact joint Gaussian of the whole sequence — a check that catches a wrong \(S\) immediately, where the means alone might not.

    Variants #

    Variant What changes Cost Use when
    kf the recursion (1)–(3) \(O(n^3 + m^3)\) per step the model is linear and Gaussian — this is exact
    ekf \(f\) and \(h\) linearised at the mean one Jacobian per step mildly nonlinear, Jacobians available
    ukf \(2n+1\) sigma points through \(f\) and \(h\) \(2n+1\) evaluations per step nonlinear, no Jacobian, or the EKF's linearisation is degenerate
    information_filter the same posterior as \((y, J) = (P^{-1}m, P^{-1})\) \(O(n^3)\) per step, two inversions many sensors fused, or a distributed estimate
    rts_smoother backward pass after the forward pass one extra \(O(n^3)\) per step the data is offline; you want \(p(z_t \mid z_{1..T})\)
    missing_obs update restricted to the observed rows same as kf sensors drop out, irregular sampling, ragged panels

    The split is real, not cosmetic. kf, information_filter, rts_smoother and missing_obs compute the exact posterior and differ only in algebra or in what they do with the answer; ekf and ukf are approximations whose error is second order in the curvature of \(f\) and \(h\).

    Derivation #

    1. Why the update is a matrix identity #

    Everything follows from the Gaussian conditioning identity. If \((x, z) \sim N(\mu, \Sigma)\) with blocks, then

    \[x \mid z \;\sim\; N\!\left(\mu_x + \Sigma_{xz}\Sigma_{zz}^{-1}(z - \mu_z),\;\; \Sigma_{xx} - \Sigma_{xz}\Sigma_{zz}^{-1}\Sigma_{zx}\right) \qquad (5)\]

    For the state-space model, \(z_t = C x_t + v\) with \(v \sim N(0, R)\), so \(\Sigma_{xz} = P^{-}C^{\top}\), \(\Sigma_{zz} = C P^{-} C^{\top} + R = S\), and \(\Sigma_{xx} = P^{-}\). Substituting into (5) gives (2)–(3) with nothing left over. The reason \(P\) shrinks is the Schur complement: conditioning removes the part of the variance that the observation explains.

    2. The likelihood is the product of innovations #

    Write \(p(z_{1..T}) = \prod_t p(z_t \mid z_{1..t-1})\). Under the model, \(z_t \mid z_{1..t-1} \sim N(C m_t^{-}, S_t)\) — the predictive distribution the filter already computed. Hence (4). This is also the cleanest way to see that a filter and a smoother are different questions: the filter's product is \(p(z_{1..T})\), while its per-step posterior is \(p(x_t \mid z_{1..t})\).

    The demo verifies this the hard way, by assembling the \(Tm \times Tm\) covariance of the whole observation sequence from the state covariances and taking its Cholesky log-density. The filter's sum matches to \(10^{-8}\): same model, two routes, one number.

    3. Smoothing #

    The filter answers "where was the state given the past". The smoother answers "given everything", and for offline data it is strictly better. The RTS recursion runs backwards from the last filtered moment:

    \[G_t = P_t A^{\top}\left(A P_t A^{\top} + Q\right)^{-1} \qquad (6)\]
    \[m_t^{s} = m_t + G_t\left(m_{t+1}^{s} - A m_t\right), \qquad P_t^{s} = P_t + G_t\left(P_{t+1}^{s} - P_{t+1}^{-}\right)G_t^{\top} \qquad (7)\]

    \(G_t\) is the same matrix the forward pass built to predict \(P_{t+1}^{-}\) — which is why the smoother needs the filter's covariances, not just its means. The variance can only shrink: \(P_t^{s} \preceq P_t\), with equality at \(t = T\) where there is no future to condition on. The demo checks both facts, and the payoff is visible: position RMSE \(0.622\) filtered against \(0.351\) smoothed on the same data.

    4. The information form #

    Premultiply (3) by \(P^{-1}\) and define \(J = P^{-1}\), \(y = Jm\). The update becomes additive:

    \[J_t = J_t^{-} + C^{\top}R^{-1}C, \qquad y_t = y_t^{-} + C^{\top}R^{-1}z_t \qquad (8)\]

    Fusing \(k\) sensors is \(k\) additions — the reason decentralised and multi-sensor systems are written this way. The prediction is the ugly half:

    \[J_t^{-} = \left(A J_{t-1}^{-1} A^{\top} + Q\right)^{-1}, \qquad y_t^{-} = J_t^{-} A J_{t-1}^{-1} y_{t-1} \qquad (9)\]

    Two inversions per step, so the form is a loss unless the sensor count is high or the estimate is being split across machines. The demo fits both and requires agreement to \(10^{-9}\): same posterior, different arithmetic.

    5. Linearisation: the EKF #

    Replace the model by its first-order Taylor expansion at the current mean:

    \[f(x) \approx f(m) + F(m)(x - m), \qquad h(x) \approx h(m) + H(m)(x - m) \qquad (10)\]

    and run (1)–(3) on the linearised model. The covariance transform is correct to first order; the mean is biased by \(\tfrac12\mathrm{tr}(F'' P)\) and \(\tfrac12\mathrm{tr}(H'' P)\) — terms that vanish on a linear model, which is why the demo's linear-model assertion (EKF equals KF to \(10^{-9}\)) is the right unit test and says nothing about accuracy on a curve.

    The failure is not subtle when the linearisation point is wrong. The demo's one-step test has \(h(x) = x^2\) and a prior mean of zero, so \(H = 2m = 0\): the filter concludes the observation is uninformative and returns the prior. The true evidence is \(-1.323\) nats, the UKF reports \(-1.290\), the EKF reports \(-4.768\).

    6. The unscented transform #

    Instead of differentiating, propagate a small deterministic set of points that matches the first two moments exactly. With \(n\) states, \(2n+1\) points:

    \[\mathcal{X}_0 = m, \qquad \mathcal{X}_{i}^{\pm} = m \pm \left[\sqrt{(n+\lambda)P}\right]_{i}, \qquad \lambda = \alpha^2(n+\kappa) - n \qquad (11)\]
    \[w_0^{m} = \frac{\lambda}{n+\lambda}, \quad w_0^{c} = w_0^{m} + 1 - \alpha^2 + \beta, \quad w_i^{m} = w_i^{c} = \frac{1}{2(n+\lambda)} \qquad (12)\]

    Push the points through \(f\), take the weighted mean and covariance, and you have the predicted moments to second order — no Jacobian, only \(2n+1\) evaluations. The update needs a cross-covariance, which the same points supply:

    \[\Sigma_{xz} = \sum_i w_i^{c}\left(\mathcal{X}_i - m\right)\left(\mathcal{Z}_i - \hat z\right)^{\top}, \qquad K = \Sigma_{xz} S^{-1} \qquad (13)\]

    Two details that are easy to get wrong, and both are in the code. The sigma points used for \(h\) must be redrawn from the predicted moments: the points that went through \(f\) describe the previous state, not this one. And \(\beta = 2\) is the correction for a Gaussian prior; with \(\beta = 0\) the predicted covariance of a quadratic is biased low.

    \(\alpha\) controls how far the points sit from the mean. Many implementations set \(\alpha = 10^{-3}\) so the points hug the mean, which is a numerical convenience in high dimensions — it costs accuracy, and at \(\alpha \to 0\) the transform degenerates towards the EKF. This file defaults to \(\alpha = 1\), where the weights are \(O(1)\) and (11) is exact for the moments it claims.

    7. Missing observations #

    A missing entry is not a zero and not a NaN to be interpolated. It means the update has no information from that coordinate, so the correct posterior is

    \[m_t = m_t^{-} + K (z_t^{\text{obs}} - C^{\text{obs}} m_t^{-}), \qquad K = P^{-}(C^{\text{obs}})^{\top}\left(C^{\text{obs}} P^{-} (C^{\text{obs}})^{\top} + R^{\text{obs}}\right)^{-1} \qquad (14)\]

    with \(C^{\text{obs}}\) and \(R^{\text{obs}}\) the observed rows and block. When no row survives, the posterior is the prediction and \(P\) grows by \(Q\) — the filter gets less certain because nothing arrived. The demo checks a sharp consequence: observing one coordinate of a two-dimensional observation must give exactly the same answer as a filter built for that coordinate alone.

    Code walkthrough #

    One frozenset, one dispatcher, and the two halves of the algorithm behind it.

    VARIANTS = frozenset(
    ["kf", "ekf", "ukf", "information_filter", "rts_smoother", "missing_obs"]
)

    The dispatcher is where the API's one awkwardness lives: transition and observation are matrices for four variants and callables for two. Rather than two entry points, kalman_filter reads the variant and converts. rts_smoother is the forward filter plus a backward pass, so it shares the matrix branch.

        if variant in ("kf", "information_filter", "rts_smoother", "missing_obs"):
        A, C = _as_matrix(transition, n), _as_matrix(observation, n)
        if variant == "information_filter":
            return _information_filter(z, A, C, Q, R, m0, P0)
        out = _kf(z, A, C, Q, R, m0, P0, skip=variant == "missing_obs")
        if variant == "rts_smoother":
            out.smooth_mean, out.smooth_cov = _rts(out, A, Q)
        return out

    The core is (1)–(3). The one line that is not the textbook formula is np.linalg.solve(St, Ct @ P).T: the gain is \(P C^{\top} S^{-1}\), and solving for \(S\,X = (CP)^{\top}\) then transposing avoids forming \(S^{-1}\) explicitly — the same number, half the work and a slightly better conditioned result.

            Ct = C[obs]
        St = Ct @ P @ Ct.T + R[np.ix_(obs, obs)]
        innov = z[t, obs] - Ct @ mu
        loglik += _gauss_logpdf(innov, St)
        K = np.linalg.solve(St, Ct @ P).T  # P Cᵀ S⁻¹, without an explicit inverse
        mu = mu + K @ innov
        P = P - K @ Ct @ P
        P = 0.5 * (P + P.T)

    That symmetrisation is not decoration. The update is a subtraction of two positive-definite matrices, so round-off can leave \(P\) with a small negative eigenvalue; symmetrising does not cure it but keeps the error from compounding, and a Cholesky inside _gauss_logpdf will raise rather than silently propagate a non-positive matrix. In long runs, the Joseph form \(P = (I-KC)P(I-KC)^{\top} + KRK^{\top}\) is the answer, at twice the cost.

    Missing data is a mask, not a special case, which is why missing_obs is one boolean argument rather than a second filter. The raise is the interesting branch: kf refuses a NaN and names the variant that handles it.

            if skip:
            obs = ~np.isnan(z[t])
        else:
            if np.isnan(z[t]).any():
                raise ValueError(
                    "z has a missing observation; use variant='missing_obs', which "
                    "restricts the update to the observed rows"
                )
            obs = np.ones(z.shape[1], dtype=bool)

    An empty observation set is a real state: the update is the identity, and the filter has to record the prediction as the posterior. Conflating this with "skip the step entirely" is the classic bug — the state would stop moving.

            if not obs.any():
            # Nothing to condition on: the posterior *is* the prediction. The
            # update still happens, it is just the identity.
            n_skipped += 1
            mean[t], cov[t] = mu, P
            continue

    The information form is (8)–(9) literally: two adds for the measurement, two inversions for the prediction. This is also where the filter's arithmetic shows its cost — note that the posterior is recovered by inverting \(J\) at every step purely so the two variants can be compared.

                J = J_pred + Ct.T @ np.linalg.solve(Rt, Ct)
            y = y_pred + Ct.T @ np.linalg.solve(Rt, zt)

    The smoother is (6)–(7) and nothing else. P_pred is recomputed rather than stored: \(A P_t A^{\top} + Q\) is exactly what the forward pass used, so the smoother could read it off a saved array, and caching it would save one matrix product per step.

        for t in range(T - 2, -1, -1):
        P_pred = A @ out.cov[t] @ A.T + Q
        G = np.linalg.solve(P_pred, A @ out.cov[t]).T
        sm[t] = out.mean[t] + G @ (sm[t + 1] - A @ out.mean[t])
        sc[t] = out.cov[t] + G @ (sc[t + 1] - P_pred) @ G.T
        sc[t] = 0.5 * (sc[t] + sc[t].T)

    The UKF is where the interesting mistakes live. The weights are (12), and the two weight vectors differ only in the first entry — that single number is the second-order correction for a Gaussian, and dropping it biases the predicted variance low on any curved \(f\).

        lam = alpha**2 * (n + kappa) - n
    wm = np.full(2 * n + 1, 0.5 / (n + lam))
    wc = wm.copy()
    wm[0] = lam / (n + lam)
    wc[0] = wm[0] + (1.0 - alpha**2 + beta)

    The update then redraws the sigma points from the predicted moments. Reusing the points that went through \(f\) — the tempting version, and the bug this file had first — conditions on the previous state's distribution and is off by more than a unit in the mean on the demo's linear model.

                Xs = _sigma_points(mu, P, n + lam)
            Z = np.array([h(x) for x in Xs])
            zhat = wm @ Z
            Zt = Z[:, obs]
            S = _weighted_cov(Zt, Zt, zhat[obs], zhat[obs], wc) + R[np.ix_(obs, obs)]
            cross = _weighted_cov(Xs, Zt, mu, zhat[obs], wc)

    _weighted_cov is public-minded about a detail that is easy to get wrong: the cross-covariance is between state deviations and observation deviations, and its transpose is what makes the gain \((n, m)\) rather than \((m, n)\).

    def _weighted_cov(a: np.ndarray, b: np.ndarray, ma: np.ndarray, mb: np.ndarray,
                  w: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """`Σ_i w_i (a_i − ma)(b_i − mb)ᵀ` — the cross-covariance the gain needs.

    Computing the cross term directly, rather than reading it off a scatter
    matrix, is what lets the UKF update a state it never observed.
    """
    return (a - ma).T @ (w[:, None] * (b - mb))

    The demo's exact likelihood check is the one piece of test code worth reading, because it is a different algorithm: the joint covariance of \(z_{1..T}\) built from the state covariances, with \(C A^{i-j}\) carrying a state \(j\) forward to observation \(i\).

        S = np.zeros((T * m, T * m))
    for i in range(T):
        for j in range(i + 1):
            Pij = states[j]
            for _ in range(i - j):
                Pij = A @ Pij
            block = C @ Pij @ C.T

    Complexity #

    With \(n\) states, \(m\) observations and \(T\) steps:

    Variant Time Memory Inversions per step
    kf \(O(T(n^3 + m^3))\) \(O(T n^2)\) 0 (two solves)
    information_filter \(O(T n^3)\) \(O(T n^2)\) 2
    rts_smoother \(O(T n^3)\) \(O(T n^2)\) 0 (one solve)
    ekf \(O(T(n^3 + c_J + m^3))\) \(O(T n^2)\) 0
    ukf \(O(T(n^3 + (2n+1)(c_f + c_h)))\) \(O(T n^2)\) 0
    missing_obs \(O(T(n^3 + m_{\text{obs}}^3))\) \(O(T n^2)\) 0

    The cubic is the covariance. It is why the filter is cheap for \(n = 10\) and hopeless for \(n = 10^4\) — and why the ensemble and particle filters exist: they replace \(O(n^3)\) with \(O(Nn)\) for \(N\) samples, at the price of Monte Carlo error. Storing every covariance is \(O(T n^2)\); if you only need the final state, do not allocate the arrays.

    ukf is the most expensive variant per step in practice, because \(h\) is evaluated \(2n+1\) times and each evaluation may be a numerical model. That is still linear in \(n\) and usually cheaper than deriving a Jacobian by hand.

    Failure modes #

    The linearisation point is not where the probability is. The EKF's \(H = 0\) at a symmetric prior makes a quadratic sensor look blind: \(-4.768\) nats against a true \(-1.323\) on one observation. Nothing about the estimate looks broken — the mean is right, the variance is untouched — but the likelihood is wrong by 3.4 nats, so any model comparison, any \(Q\)/\(R\) fit by maximum likelihood, and any gate that thresholds the innovation will be wrong. Diagnostic: rerun with ukf; if the likelihood moves by more than a nat on a one-step problem, the linearisation is the problem.

    The covariance stops being positive definite. \(P - KCP\) is a difference of positive-definite matrices, and in float64 it loses definiteness after thousands of steps or immediately when \(R\) is tiny. The symptom is a LinAlgError from a Cholesky deep inside a likelihood evaluation, or a covariance with a \(-10^{-12}\) eigenvalue that the next update turns into a real failure. Diagnostic: track the smallest eigenvalue of \(P\); fix: the Joseph form, or a square-root filter that propagates a Cholesky factor and cannot represent an indefinite matrix.

    Divergence on a strongly curved model. A first version of this demo used \(f(x) = 0.5x + 25x/(1+x^2)\) with a quadratic sensor, where the derivative vanishes near the mean: with no information arriving, \(P\) grew geometrically until it reached inf at step 109. The filter did not raise; it produced nans and a likelihood of nan. Diagnostic: log the innovation and \(S\) — a growing \(S\) with a shrinking gain is the filter telling you the model and the data disagree; use ukf, inflate \(Q\), or fix the model.

    Treating a missing value as a number. Interpolating a gap and feeding it to kf as if it were data makes the filter more confident at exactly the steps where it should be less. missing_obs exists so that this is a decision, not an accident: the posterior at a gap is the prediction, and the trace of \(P\) grows.

    A wrong \(R\) that hides in the covariance. \(R\) too small makes \(K \to 1\), the filter chases noise, and \(P\) collapses so hard that later real changes are ignored ("filter lock"). \(R\) too large does the opposite: the filter ignores the sensor and follows the model, and the innovation becomes systematically biased rather than white. Diagnostic: the normalised innovation squared \(e^{\top} S^{-1} e\) should average about \(m\); the demo's exact likelihood check is the same idea in one number.

    Applications #

    Navigation and sensor fusion. The origin of the method, and still its largest use: an IMU integrated at high rate between GPS fixes, with the information form when several sensors report on the same state. Apollo's onboard computer ran a 15-state filter with a 24 Hz update.

    Tracking and radar. Constant-velocity or constant-acceleration models with position-only observations; missing_obs is the normal case, not an exception, because a target drops out of a radar sweep for a few frames all the time.

    Econometrics and structural time series. Local level, local linear trend and seasonal components are all state-space models, fitted by maximising (4) over \(Q\) and \(R\), and smoothed for historical estimates. statsmodels' UnobservedComponents is this file with a parameter optimiser on top.

    Control. The LQG controller is a Kalman filter feeding an LQR gain; the separation principle says the two can be designed independently precisely because the conditional mean is a sufficient statistic for a linear-Gaussian system.

    Online learning of a linear model. Recursive least squares is the Kalman filter with \(A = I\), \(Q = 0\), \(C\) the feature row and \(R\) the noise variance — the same recursion seen from a different literature. ridge.py is the batch version.

    Where it is a trap: any posterior that is not unimodal, and any model where the nonlinearity is the point rather than a nuisance. A Gaussian belief cannot represent "either here or there", and a filter that is confident and wrong is worse than one that is uncertain. particle_filter.py exists for that case, and mcmc.py when the state is not sequential at all.
