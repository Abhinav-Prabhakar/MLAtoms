    05 · Probabilistic models/tier 3/5 variants/source

    Gaussian process classification

    Intuition #

    Regression with a Gaussian process is a matrix solve because a Gaussian prior times a Gaussian likelihood is Gaussian. gaussian_process.py gets a closed-form posterior for free, and the whole atom is one Cholesky factorisation.

    Classification breaks that, and it breaks it completely. The likelihood is \(\sigma(f_i)\) or \(\Phi(f_i)\) — bounded, and not exponential of a quadratic — so the posterior over the \(n\) latent values is an \(n\)-dimensional distribution with no closed form, and the predictive probability is an integral over it. Nothing about the prior's conjugacy survives.

    What survives is the shape. The posterior over \(f\) is approximately Gaussian: it is a Gaussian times a product of sigmoids, and a sigmoid is close to a Gaussian in log space over the region where the data pin the function down.

       prior p(f)              likelihood             posterior p(f|y)
   ─────────               ──────────             ─────────────────
     N(0, K)               ∏ σ(yᵢfᵢ)              Gaussian × sigmoids
   ╭─────╮                 ╭──╮                    ╭──╮
  ╱       ╲               ╱    ╲                  ╱    ╲___
 ───────────             ────────                ──────────
   symmetric               saturates              skewed, heavier
   exactly Gaussian        at both ends           on the uncertain side

    So both approximations here return a Gaussian, and they differ in how they choose it:

    • Laplace matches the log-density quadratically at its maximum. One point, second-order there, nothing else. Cheap, local, and exact in the limit of lots of data — the Bernstein–von Mises argument from laplace_approximation.py applies unchanged.
    • EP matches the marginal mean and variance of every latent, by iteratively replacing the likelihood with one Gaussian factor per data point. Global in the sense that every site's update feels every other site, but it optimises nothing and carries no guarantee.

    Neither is uniformly better, and the demo is built to show that rather than assert otherwise. On two data points, against a 121×121 grid:

    posterior mean error evidence error
    Laplace \(4.5\times10^{-2}\) \(1.4\times10^{-2}\) nats
    EP \(3.8\times10^{-6}\) \(1.2\times10^{-5}\) nats

    EP wins decisively there. On a 60-point problem the two produce predictions that differ by at most 0.021 in probability and score identically on held-out data — which is the usual situation, and the reason nobody rewrites a working Laplace-based model.

    The three other variants are the parts people get wrong. probit and logistic are the two likelihoods; the probit one has closed-form derivatives, closed-form EP moments and a closed-form predictive, while the logistic one needs quadrature in both places. one_vs_rest is what you actually have to do for more than two classes, because neither approximation generalises to \(k\) classes without a new derivation.

    Variants #

    Variant Likelihood Inference Predictive Use
    laplace logistic Laplace plug-in \(\sigma(\mu)\) the textbook baseline; fast, slightly overconfident
    logistic logistic Laplace integrate over \(q(f_*)\) the same fit, honest predictive
    probit probit EP analytic \(\Phi(\mu/\sqrt{1+v})\) closed forms everywhere; the classic pairing
    expectation_propagation logistic EP integrate best marginals, needs damping
    one_vs_rest logistic Laplace softmax over log-odds more than two classes

    The predictive axis is not cosmetic. At the demo's test point the exact predictive is 0.5519, the plug-in reports 0.5607 — overconfident, always, in the direction of the mode — integrating over the same Gaussian gives 0.5508, and EP gives 0.5520. Plugging the mode in is what most implementations do and it is the one choice that is systematically wrong.

    probit bundles two independent facts that happen to line up: the probit likelihood has closed-form EP moments (truncated-Gaussian integrals), and its predictive given a Gaussian posterior is exact, not an approximation. Its drawback is the same as everyone's: \(\Phi\) is not in numpy.

    laplace and logistic share every line of the fit. They are separate atoms in the table because the predictive is a different function, and because a table that hides that difference teaches the wrong thing.

    Derivation #

    1. The model, and the integral that is not there #

    \[f \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, k), \qquad y_i \mid f_i \sim p(y_i \mid f_i), \qquad p(f) = N(f; 0, K) \qquad (1)\]

    with \(K_{ij} = k(x_i,x_j)\) and \(y_i \in \{-1,+1\}\). Bayes gives

    \[p(f\mid y) = \frac{1}{p(y)}\,N(f;0,K)\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(y_i\mid f_i), \qquad p(y) = \int N(f;0,K)\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(y_i\mid f_i)\,df \qquad (2)\]

    The product of \(n\) sigmoids is not the exponential of a quadratic in \(f\), so (2) has no closed form and no conjugate update exists. Every method below is a Gaussian ansatz \(q(f) = N(f;\mu,\Sigma)\) plus a rule for choosing it — and the predictive is

    \[p(y_*=1\mid y) = \int \sigma(f_*)\,p(f_*\mid y)\,df_* \qquad (3)\]

    which is one more integral, again not available.

    2. Laplace: quadratic at the mode #

    Write \(\Psi(f) = \log p(f,y) = -\tfrac12 f^\top K^{-1}f + \sum_i \log p(y_i\mid f_i)\). Then

    \[\nabla\Psi = -K^{-1}f + t, \qquad \nabla^2\Psi = -K^{-1} - W, \qquad t_i = \frac{\partial \log p(y_i\mid f_i)}{\partial f_i}, \quad W_{ii} = -\frac{\partial^2 \log p(y_i\mid f_i)}{\partial f_i^2} \qquad (4)\]

    with \(W\) positive because each log-likelihood is concave in \(f_i\). Newton's method on \(\nabla\Psi = 0\) is

    \[(K^{-1} + W)\,\delta = t - K^{-1}f, \qquad f \leftarrow f + \delta \qquad (5)\]

    and at convergence \(\hat f\) the posterior is approximated by

    \[q(f) = N\!\left(\hat f,\ (K^{-1}+W)^{-1}\right) \qquad (6)\]

    The evidence follows from the same expansion as laplace_approximation.py (3), one dimension per latent:

    \[\log p(y) \approx -\tfrac12 \hat f^\top K^{-1}\hat f + \sum_i \log p(y_i\mid \hat f_i) - \tfrac12\log\lvert I + W^{1/2}KW^{1/2}\rvert \qquad (7)\]

    The predictive needs two things from (6). The mean uses an identity that is easy to miss: at the mode \(K^{-1}\hat f = t\), so

    \[\mathbb{E}[f_*] = k_*^\top K^{-1}\hat f = k_*^\top t \qquad (8)\]

    — no inverse of \(K\) required, just the gradient vector the optimiser already has. The variance uses the Woodbury form

    \[k_{**} - k_*^\top (K + W^{-1})^{-1}k_* = k_{**} - \big\lVert L^{-1}W^{1/2}k_*\big\rVert^2, \qquad B = I + W^{1/2}KW^{1/2} = LL^\top \qquad (9)\]

    3. The numerical trick that makes (5) work #

    \(W_{ii}\) can be enormous — for a well-separated point \(p(1-p) \to 0\), so \(W^{-1}\) is fine but \(W\) is nearly singular, and solving with \(K^{-1}+W\) directly loses precision. The code keeps everything on the \(B\) side:

    \[a = (K+W^{-1})^{-1}\big(Wf + t\big), \qquad \hat f = Ka \qquad (10)\]

    and evaluates (10) as \(\;a = b - W^{1/2}L^{-\top}L^{-1}W^{1/2}Kb\) with \(b = Wf + t\). Every solve is triangular, no inverse of \(K\) or \(W\) is formed, and the determinant in (7) is \(\sum_i \log L_{ii}\). That is Rasmussen & Williams (2006) Algorithm 3.1, and it is the difference between a method that converges and one that does not on separable data.

    4. EP: a Gaussian site per data point #

    EP replaces each likelihood factor by an unnormalised Gaussian in \(f_i\):

    \[t_i(f_i) = \tilde Z_i\,N(f_i;\,\nu_i,\,1/\tau_i), \qquad q(f) \propto N(f;0,K)\prod_i t_i(f_i) \qquad (11)\]

    This is a Gaussian, so its parameters are two matrix formulas:

    \[\Sigma = (K^{-1} + T)^{-1}, \qquad \mu = \Sigma\,(\tau \odot \nu), \qquad T = \mathrm{diag}(\tau) \qquad (12)\]

    Sites are updated one at a time. Removing site \(i\) from \(q\) gives the cavity, which in natural parameters is a subtraction of one precision and one linear term. With Sherman–Morrison on the rank-one difference:

    \[\sigma_{-i}^2 = \frac{1}{1/\Sigma_{ii} - \tau_i}, \qquad \mu_{-i} = \sigma_{-i}^2\left(\frac{\mu_i}{\Sigma_{ii}} - \tau_i\nu_i\right) \qquad (13)\]

    The tilted distribution is the cavity times the true likelihood,

    \[\hat q_i(f_i) \propto q_{-i}(f_i)\,p(y_i\mid f_i) \qquad (14)\]

    and EP chooses the new site so that \(q\) — which is \(q_{-i}\) times the site — has \(\hat q_i\)'s mean and variance. Solving for the site parameters:

    \[\tau_i^{\text{new}} = \frac{1}{\tilde\sigma_i^2} - \frac{1}{\sigma_{-i}^2}, \qquad \nu_i^{\text{new}} = \frac{1}{\tau_i^{\text{new}}}\left(\frac{\tilde\mu_i}{\tilde\sigma_i^2} - \frac{\mu_{-i}}{\sigma_{-i}^2}\right) \qquad (15)\]

    The division by \(\tau\) in (15) is where implementations go wrong. The bracket is the site's natural linear parameter \(\eta_i\); \(\nu_i = \eta_i/\tau_i\) is its mean. Storing \(\eta\) where the code expects \(\nu\) leaves the posterior variance right and the mean wrong by a factor of \(\tau_i\) — precisely the bug the demo found, and it looked plausible because the variances matched.

    For a probit likelihood, (14) is a Gaussian times a Gaussian CDF, and its moments are the truncated-normal formulas:

    \[z_i = \frac{y_i\mu_{-i}}{\sqrt{1+\sigma_{-i}^2}}, \quad \hat Z_i = \Phi(z_i), \quad \tilde\mu_i = \mu_{-i} + \frac{y_i\sigma_{-i}^2\,\varphi(z_i)}{\Phi(z_i)\sqrt{1+\sigma_{-i}^2}}, \quad \tilde\sigma_i^2 = \sigma_{-i}^2 - \frac{\sigma_{-i}^4\,\varphi(z_i)\big(z_i + \varphi(z_i)/\Phi(z_i)\big)}{\Phi(z_i)(1+\sigma_{-i}^2)} \qquad (16)\]

    One site, one closed form, no quadrature. For the logistic likelihood there is no such formula and the demo computes the same three moments with 40-point Gauss–Hermite quadrature.

    5. The EP evidence, and the amplitude nobody remembers #

    Moment matching in (15) fixes \(\tau_i\) and \(\nu_i\) — the shape of the site — but not \(\tilde Z_i\). The missing condition is that the site must carry the same mass as the likelihood it replaces, measured under the cavity:

    \[\tilde Z_i = \frac{\hat Z_i}{N(\nu_i;\,\mu_{-i},\,\sigma_{-i}^2 + 1/\tau_i)} \qquad (17)\]

    With that, the approximate evidence is the integral of (11):

    \[\log Z_{\text{EP}} = \sum_i \log \tilde Z_i + \log N\!\big(0;\,\nu,\,K + S\big), \qquad S = \mathrm{diag}(1/\tau) \qquad (18)\]

    Drop (17) and the estimate is not even exact for a Gaussian likelihood, which is the check that catches it. Keep it, and the one-point case becomes exact for a different reason: with \(n=1\) the cavity is the prior, so \(\hat Z_1 = p(y_1)\) and the two Gaussian terms in (18) cancel by symmetry. The demo asserts this to \(10^{-13}\).

    6. Two likelihoods, one difference #

    \[\text{logistic: } \log p(y\mid f) = -\log(1+e^{-yf}), \qquad t = y\sigma(-yf), \qquad W = \sigma(f)\sigma(-f) \qquad (19)\]
    \[\text{probit: } \log p(y\mid f) = \log\Phi(yf), \qquad t = \frac{y\varphi(z)}{\Phi(z)}, \qquad W = \frac{\varphi(z)}{\Phi(z)}\left(z + \frac{\varphi(z)}{\Phi(z)}\right) \qquad (20)\]

    Both are concave, so both Laplace and EP apply. The probit ratios \(\varphi(z)/\Phi(z)\) have to be computed as \(\exp(\log\varphi - \log\Phi)\), not as a quotient of two small numbers; the demo's log_ndtr does that and falls back to the asymptotic series in the far tail. The logistic \(W\) uses \(\sigma(f)\sigma(-f) = \sigma(f)(1-\sigma(f))\), which is the same quantity and is what the Fisher information of a Bernoulli is.

    The predictive is where they differ most:

    \[p(y_*=1\mid y) \approx \begin{cases} \Phi\!\left(\mu_*/\sqrt{1+v_*}\right) & \text{probit} \\ \int\sigma(\mu_* + \sqrt{v_*}z)\,\varphi(z)\,dz & \text{logistic} \end{cases} \qquad (21)\]

    The probit one is exact given the Gaussian \(q\) — the convolution of a Gaussian with a Gaussian CDF is a Gaussian CDF — so probit:predictive is exact under the approximation, not a second approximation on top. The logistic one is a genuine integral, done with Gauss–Hermite. Both pull the prediction toward \(\tfrac12\) relative to the plug-in, because uncertainty in \(f_*\) cannot make the model more confident.

    7. More than two classes #

    Neither approximation extends to \(k\) classes by itself; the multiclass probit posterior is a product of \(k\) coupled Gaussians and the site structure changes shape. The cheap route is \(k\) one-vs-rest binary problems, and then a combination rule. Normalising the \(k\) probabilities is wrong — they are not calibrated against each other and the sum is an artefact — so the code combines log-odds:

    \[P(c\mid x) = \frac{\exp(r_c)}{\sum_{c'}\exp(r_{c'})}, \qquad r_c = \log\frac{p_c(x)}{1-p_c(x)} \qquad (22)\]

    \(r_c\) is the latent mean of classifier \(c\) up to the link, so (22) is a softmax over the \(k\) latent functions. It is still not a coherent probability model — the \(k\) classifiers were fitted independently and can all be confident about different classes — which is why multinomial EP and one-vs-one exist. It works well enough that it is what most libraries do.

    Code walkthrough #

    The probit site function is (20), and the only interesting line is the ratio of two exponentially small numbers, which has to be an exponential of a difference:

    def _probit_site(y: np.ndarray, f: np.ndarray):
    """Same three quantities for `p(y|f) = Φ(y f)`."""
    z = y * f
    ll = log_ndtr(z)
    r = np.exp(-0.5 * z * z - 0.5 * LOG2PI - ll)          # φ(z)/Φ(z)
    return ll, y * r, r * (z + r)

    log_ndtr is a hand-rolled \(\log\Phi\), because numpy has no erfc:

    def ndtr(z: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Standard normal CDF. numpy has no `erf`, so this goes through `math`."""
    z = np.asarray(z, dtype=float)
    flat = np.array([0.5 * math.erfc(-v / SQRT2) for v in z.ravel()], dtype=float)
    return flat.reshape(z.shape)

    The Newton step is (10). Two triangular solves, and the K_ @ b inside the first one is the only matrix-matrix product in the loop:

                sW = np.sqrt(W)
            L = np.linalg.cholesky(np.eye(n) + sW[:, None] * self.K_ * sW[None, :])
            b = W * f + t
            # a = (K + W⁻¹)⁻¹ b, and the mode is K a. Doing it this way keeps
            # every solve triangular; forming K + W⁻¹ would need an inverse.
            v = np.linalg.solve(L, sW * (self.K_ @ b))
            a = b - sW * np.linalg.solve(L.T, v)
            new = self.K_ @ a

    The evidence is (7), and slogdet is not used: the Cholesky factor is already there, so the log-determinant is the sum of its log-diagonal in the same pass that the predictive needs.

            self.a_ = np.linalg.solve(self.K_, f)
        self.logZ_ = (-0.5 * float(f @ self.a_) + float(self._site(f)[0].sum())
                      - float(np.log(np.diag(self.L_)).sum()))
        # predictive: mean = k*ᵀ t, var = k** − ‖L⁻¹ √W k*‖²
        self._coef, self._chol, self._scale = self.t_, self.L_, self.sqrtW_

    EP's posterior is (12), computed without ever forming \(K^{-1}\): the shape is \(K - V^\top V\) with \(V = L^{-1}T^{1/2}K\), which is the Woodbury identity applied to \((K^{-1}+T)^{-1}\).

            sT = np.sqrt(np.maximum(tau, 0.0))
        L = np.linalg.cholesky(np.eye(n) + sT[:, None] * self.K_ * sT[None, :])
        V = np.linalg.solve(L, sT[:, None] * self.K_)
        Sigma = self.K_ - V.T @ V                      # (K⁻¹ + diag τ)⁻¹
        return Sigma, Sigma @ (tau * nu)

    The cavity and the site update are (13) and (15). The comment on nu_i is the one that matters — it is the line the demo's first version had wrong.

                    s2 = 1.0 / max(1.0 / Sigma[i, i] - tau[i], 1e-12)
                m = s2 * (mu[i] / Sigma[i, i] - nu[i] * tau[i])
                m_t, s2_t, _ = self._tilted(m, s2, self.y_[i])
                tau_i = 1.0 / max(s2_t, 1e-300) - 1.0 / s2
                # natural-form site: precision `tau_i`, linear term `eta_i`.
                # `nu` is stored as the *mean*, eta/tau, which is what makes
                # `mu = Sigma @ (tau * nu)` one matrix-vector product.
                nu_i = (m_t / max(s2_t, 1e-300) - m / s2) / tau_i

    The probit tilt is (16) written out. r is again \(\varphi/\Phi\), and the variance is the truncated-normal expression with the \(z + r\) correction that makes the site narrower than the cavity — the term without it is the naive answer and it is wrong by a factor that grows in the tail.

                z = y * m / math.sqrt(1.0 + s2)
            lz = float(log_ndtr(np.array([z]))[0])
            r = math.exp(-0.5 * z * z - 0.5 * LOG2PI - lz)
            mean = m + y * s2 * r / math.sqrt(1.0 + s2)
            var = s2 - s2 * s2 * r * (z + r) / (1.0 + s2)
            return mean, var, lz

    The amplitude fix (17) is the loop at the end of _ep: the tilted mass against the mass the site would have under the cavity. log_zq is the second term of (18), one Cholesky of \(K + S\).

                lz = self._tilted(m, s2, self.y_[i])[2]
            v = s2 + S[i]                                  # variance of q_{-i} * site
            log_nu = -0.5 * LOG2PI - 0.5 * math.log(v) - 0.5 * (nu[i] - m) ** 2 / v
            log_amp += lz - log_nu
        self.logZ_ = log_amp + log_zq

    Prediction is (21), three branches, and this is the whole of the laplace versus logistic variant difference:

            mean, var = self._latent(X)
        if self.predictive == "plug":
            return sigmoid(mean)
        if self.predictive == "analytic":       # probit: exact integral, no quadrature
            return ndtr(mean / np.sqrt(1.0 + var))
        x, w = np.polynomial.hermite.hermgauss(20)
        nodes = mean[:, None] + np.sqrt(2.0 * var)[:, None] * x
        return (w * sigmoid(nodes)).sum(axis=1) * INV_SQRT_PI

    The multiclass wrapper is (22), and the only subtlety is the clip: a classifier that says 0.9999 would otherwise produce an infinite log-odds.

                P = np.stack([m.predict_proba(X) for m in self.models_], axis=1)
            logit = np.log(np.clip(P, 1e-12, 1.0)) - np.log(np.clip(1.0 - P, 1e-12, 1.0))
            logit -= logit.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
            E = np.exp(logit)
            return E / E.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

    Complexity #

    With \(n\) training points, \(m\) test points, and \(T\) EP sweeps:

    Step Time Memory
    kernel matrix \(O(n^2d)\) \(O(n^2)\)
    one Newton step \(O(n^3)\) \(O(n^2)\)
    full Laplace fit \(O(I n^3)\), \(I \approx 5\) \(O(n^2)\)
    one EP sweep \(O(n^3 + n^2)\) \(O(n^2)\)
    full EP fit \(O(Tn^3)\), \(T \approx 25\) \(O(n^2)\)
    predictive \(O(n^2m)\) \(O(nm)\)

    EP costs roughly five Laplace fits, and both are cubic for the same reason as in regression: the bottleneck is a Cholesky of an \(n\times n\) dense matrix. The demo's numbers are 5 Newton steps and 25 damped EP sweeps for 60 points.

    The predictive is the part that scales badly in the wrong place. Every test point needs a row of \(K_*\) and a triangular solve, so \(m\) test points cost \(O(n^2m)\) and \(O(nm)\) memory — and at \(m = 10^5\) that is the whole budget. The usual fix is the same as in regression: inducing points (a sparse GP), which replaces \(K\) with \(K_{nm}K_{mm}^{-1}K_{mn}\) everywhere here, and turns the cost into \(O(nm^2)\). With a non-Gaussian likelihood the sparse version needs the sites as well, and that is the sparse EP of Kuss (2006).

    Failure modes #

    EP not converging. The site updates are a fixed-point iteration with no Lyapunov function; on some problems they oscillate, and the code's damping = 0.7 is what makes the demo converge in 25 sweeps instead of never. EP's fixed points can also be multiple. Diagnostic: watch delta across sweeps; if it plateaus above tol, lower the damping and raise n_iter. A converged EP on a misspecified model is still wrong, and the failure is silent.

    The cavity variance going negative. (13) has \(1/\Sigma_{ii} - \tau_i\) in a denominator, and for a nearly-deterministic site the two terms are equal to within rounding. The code clamps at \(10^{-12}\); without a guard the sign flips and the next site update receives a negative precision. Diagnostic: any site with \(\tau_i \le 0\) after fit, or nan in Sigma_.

    EP being overconfident on the wrong model. EP matches moments of the approximation, not of the truth, and its variance estimates are famously too small when the likelihood does not fit the data — outliers, label noise, a Gaussian process whose kernel is wrong. Laplace errs in the same direction but by a different amount. Diagnostic: EP versus Laplace on the same data; a large disagreement in variance (not in mean) is the signature, and neither is trustworthy in that case.

    Treating the evidence as a Bayes factor you can trust. (7) and (18) are both approximations to the same quantity, and they disagree by more than their individual errors would suggest. On the demo's two points they are 0.014 nats apart; on a misspecified model they can differ by tens. Diagnostic: compare with a nested estimate or a short MCMC run before reporting a Bayes factor difference below about 1 nat.

    The plug-in predictive. sigmoid(mean) is the mode's opinion, and the mode's opinion is more extreme than the posterior's average, always. On the demo it is 0.0087 too high, and the error grows with the predictive variance — so it is largest exactly where the model is least sure. Diagnostic: integrate=True versus integrate=False; if the two differ by more than the uncertainty you are reporting, the plug-in one is the wrong number.

    Separable data. If the classes are separable, the likelihood has no maximum at any finite \(f\) and the posterior is improper along a direction. Newton walks off, W collapses, and B becomes the identity — which is numerically stable and statistically meaningless. The kernel and the prior are what prevent this; there is no prior_prec knob here because the GP prior is the regulariser. Diagnostic: \(\lVert\hat f\rVert\) growing without bound and logZ_ increasing monotonically as the step count rises.

    One-vs-rest incoherence. The \(k\) binary classifiers are independent, so the combined probabilities are not those of any single generative model; a point can be far from all \(k\) class means and still receive a confident label. This is not a bug to be fixed by better normalisation — normalising probabilities is just as incoherent — but a property of the approach. Diagnostic: the maximum probability on points far outside the training region, which should be near \(1/k\) and will not be.

    \(n\) above a few thousand. Dense \(O(n^3)\) with \(n = 10^4\) is \(10^{12}\) flops per Newton step and 800 MB for the kernel matrix. Both approximations are exact algorithms with an exact cost; they do not degrade, they stop. Use inducing points or a variational bound.

    Applications #

    Any binary classification with calibrated uncertainty. GP classification is the default when the answer is "how likely" rather than "which" — medical screening, credit risk, fault detection. The predictive variance is a real quantity here, not a by-product, and it is the reason to pay \(O(n^3)\) instead of logistic regression.

    Spatial and spatiotemporal data. Geo-referenced presence/absence records, species distribution modelling, air-quality threshold exceedance: a kernel on coordinates is a more honest prior than feature engineering, and the latent surface is the interpretable object. The regression version is gaussian_process.py; this file is the same thing for counts that have been thresholded.

    Bayesian optimisation with constraints. Constrained BO keeps two GPs — the objective and the feasibility classifier — and evaluates only where the expected improvement is high and the probability of feasibility is high. That probability is exactly predict_proba from this file.

    Ordinal regression. Ratings, severity scores, Likert data: probit GP with \(K-1\) ordered thresholds is the standard model, and it reduces to this file for \(K=2\).

    Active learning. The predictive variance is the acquisition function. Points with \(v_*\) large and \(\lvert\mu_*\rvert\) near zero are where the model does not know, and the EP version's better-calibrated variances make it the better acquisition.

    Where it is a trap. Multiclass problems where a coherent joint matters (use multinomial EP or a proper softmax GP), datasets where the labels are noisy in a way the likelihood does not model, and anything above a few thousand points without a sparse approximation. Also: using the evidence to compare a GP classifier with a non-probabilistic model, where the comparison is between two different notions of likelihood.
