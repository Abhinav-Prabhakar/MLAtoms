    05 · Probabilistic models/tier 3/6 variants/source

    Copulas

    Intuition #

    A joint distribution is two things at once: where each variable sits, and how they move together. Most of the time only the second is interesting, and it is the part that survives any change of units. Sklar's theorem says the split is exact:

    \[F(x_1,\dots,x_d) = C\big(F_1(x_1),\dots,F_d(x_d)\big), \qquad (1)\]

    with each \(F_j\) a marginal and \(C\) a copula — a distribution on the unit cube whose own margins are uniform. Apply \(F_j\) to a column and the units, the location, the skew and the scale all disappear; what is left is \(C\).

    \[C(u_1,\dots,u_d) = P(U_1 \le u_1, \dots, U_d \le u_d), \qquad U_j = F_j(X_j) \qquad (2)\]

    Two consequences carry the whole subject.

    • Margins are free. Fit them however you like — empirical, normal, GPD for the tail — and glue the dependence on afterwards. A copula fitted to ranks cannot be disturbed by a monotone transform of any column.
    • Dependence is one object. Every variant below is a different \(C\), and the differences show up where correlations are least informative: the corners.
       raw data                    ranks                   the copula
   ─────────                   ─────                   ──────────
   x₂ │      · ·             1 │     ·  ·              1 │    · ·
      │   ·  · ·                │   ·  ·                  │  · ·
      │ · ·                    │ · ·                     │ · ·
      └──────────► x₁       0 └──────────► u₁         0 └─────────► u₁
     units, location,          uniform on [0,1]²         the dependence,
     skew, outliers            by construction           nothing else

    The picture in the middle is the point: after the rank transform every column has the same distribution regardless of what it was, so the only thing a fit can possibly see is the arrangement.

    Tail dependence is the quantity that separates the families, and the reason to prefer one over another:

    \[\lambda_L = \lim_{u \to 0} \frac{C(u,u)}{u}, \qquad \lambda_U = \lim_{u \to 1} \frac{1 - 2u + C(u,u)}{1-u} \qquad (3)\]

    \(\lambda_L > 0\) means that a draw in the bottom \(u\)-quantile of one variable has probability about \(\lambda_L\) of being in the bottom \(u\)-quantile of the other, for every \(u\), however small. A Gaussian copula fails this for every \(|\rho| < 1\): its conditional probability of a joint crash does go to zero. If the application is a joint default, a joint flood, or two assets selling off together, the Gaussian copula asserts that the scenario has probability zero in the limit, and no amount of fitting changes that.

    The six variants are chosen to span that space:

    family \(\lambda_L\) \(\lambda_U\) shape of the dependence
    gaussian 0 0 symmetric, elliptical, tail-free
    t \(>0\) same symmetric, elliptical, heavy in both tails
    clayton \(2^{-1/\theta}\) 0 strong lower tail, no upper
    gumbel 0 \(2-2^{1/\theta}\) strong upper tail, no lower
    frank 0 0 symmetric, concentrated in the body
    vine pair-dependent pair-dependent one family per pair, per tree

    gaussian — the default, and the one that taught everyone to distrust copulas in 2008. Its density is the multivariate normal's with the marginal factors divided out:

    \[c(u) = |R|^{-1/2}\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}z^\top (R^{-1} - I) z\right), \qquad z = \Phi^{-1}(u) \qquad (4)\]

    The \(+I\) inside is the marginal correction: the normal density has \(z^\top R^{-1} z\), and dividing by \(\prod \phi(z_j)\) adds back \(z^\top z\). Forget it and every axiom test still passes — only the density check and \(\int c = 1\) catch it.

    t — the Gaussian's inverse-Wishart mixture. Draw \(W \sim \chi^2_\nu\) once, divide the whole normal vector by \(\sqrt{W/\nu}\), and the result is multivariate \(t\). Elliptical like the Gaussian, but the shared divisor makes joint extremes arrive together, so both tail dependences are positive and equal:

    \[\lambda_L = \lambda_U = 2\,\bar t_{\nu+1}\!\left(-\sqrt{\frac{(\nu+1)(1-\rho)}{1+\rho}}\right) \qquad (5)\]

    with \(\bar t\) the Student-t survival function. As \(\nu \to \infty\) this decays to zero and the family becomes Gaussian.

    clayton and gumbel are the asymmetric pair. Clayton is generated by a shared Gamma frailty, Gumbel by a shared positive-stable frailty; both are archimedean, \(C(u) = \psi^{-1}(\sum \psi(u_j))\), with \(\psi\) the generator. Clayton's density diverges at the origin and vanishes at the far corner, Gumbel the reverse. Pick Clayton for survival/joint-failure data, Gumbel for joint booms — equity indices, hurricane counts, anything where the upside co-moves and the downside does not.

    frank has no tail dependence at all, but unlike the Gaussian its dependence is not elliptical: it is strongest in the middle of the distribution. It is the archimedean member that can model negative dependence (\(\theta < 0\)), and it is symmetric under \(u \mapsto 1-u\), which makes it the right null model when you want dependence without a tail story.

    vine replaces one parametric family with a product of pair copulas. For three dimensions rooted at variable 1:

    \[c_{123}(u_1,u_2,u_3) = c_{12}(u_1,u_2)\,c_{13}(u_1,u_3)\,c_{23\mid1}\big(h(u_2\mid u_1),\,h(u_3\mid u_1)\big) \qquad (6)\]

    Each factor is an ordinary bivariate copula, so the families can be mixed — a Gumbel for the first tree, Clayton for the second — and each pair gets its own tail behaviour. That flexibility is the reason vines replaced the Gaussian copula in energy and insurance; it is also why they are the hardest to fit honestly, since a \(d\)-dimensional vine has \(d(d-1)/2\) pairs and the later trees are built from estimated h-functions, so errors compound.

    Derivation #

    The density is the mixed derivative #

    Differentiating (1) once per coordinate,

    \[c(u_1,\dots,u_d) = \frac{\partial^d C}{\partial u_1 \cdots \partial u_d}, \qquad f(x) = c\big(F_1(x_1),\dots\big)\prod_j f_j(x_j). \qquad (7)\]

    This is the definition the code tests: a central difference of cdf in two coordinates must reproduce logpdf, and the double integral of \(c\) over the square must be one. The axioms \(C(u,1)=u\), \(C(u,0)=0\), \(C(1,\dots,1)=1\) alone admit any function agreeing with the margins, including ones whose mixed derivative is negative somewhere; the density check is what pins a family down.

    Kendall's tau, and why it is the parameter #

    For a bivariate copula,

    \[\tau = 4\iint_{[0,1]^2} C(u,v)\,dC(u,v) - 1 = 1 - 4\iint \frac{\partial C}{\partial u}\frac{\partial C}{\partial v}\,du\,dv. \qquad (8)\]

    \(\tau\) depends only on the copula, never on the margins, and it has a closed form in every family here:

    \[\tau_{\text{gauss}} = \frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\rho, \qquad \tau_{\text{clayton}} = \frac{\theta}{\theta+2}, \qquad \tau_{\text{gumbel}} = 1 - \frac{1}{\theta}, \qquad \tau_{\text{frank}} = 1 - \frac{4}{\theta} + \frac{4}{\theta^2}D_1(\theta) \qquad (9)\]

    where \(D_1(\theta) = \frac{1}{\theta}\int_0^\theta \frac{t}{e^t-1}\,dt\) is the first Debye function. Inverting these turns an estimate of \(\tau\) into an estimate of the parameter, which is exactly what fit does. The inversion is closed form for the first three and a bisection for Frank, which is monotone but has no elementary inverse.

    \(\tau\) is also the reason the fit is margin-free. The sample version counts concordant minus discordant pairs:

    \[\hat\tau = \frac{n_c - n_d}{\binom{n}{2}}, \qquad (10)\]

    and a pair is concordant exactly when the two rank orders agree, so the count depends on ranks only. The demo refits on \(x^2\) and \(\log y\) and asserts the estimate is unchanged to \(10^{-12}\).

    Tail dependence, derived once #

    \(\lambda_L\) is a limit of a conditional probability. For the t copula it follows from the fact that both coordinates share the same \(W\): conditionally on a small \(W\), both \(z\)'s are large together, and the probability that the other coordinate is also extreme given one is extreme is the probability that a \(t_{\nu+1}\) variable — the conditional distribution of one coordinate given the other — falls below a threshold that grows with \(\nu\) and \(\rho\). That yields (5). For Clayton, \(C(u,u) \approx (2u^{-\theta})^{-1/\theta} = 2^{-1/\theta}u\) as \(u \to 0\), so the ratio in (3) is the constant:

    \[\lambda_L^{\text{clayton}} = 2^{-1/\theta}, \qquad \lambda_U^{\text{gumbel}} = 2 - 2^{1/\theta}. \qquad (11)\]

    Both are exact for every \(u\) to leading order, which is why the demo can assert them to \(10^{-6}\) at \(\varepsilon = 10^{-8}\) while the Gaussian's ratio is still \(4\times10^{-3}\) there.

    Sampling: the conditional, and the frailty #

    Two constructions cover all six variants.

    Conditional inversion. If \(h(u \mid v) = \partial C(u,v)/\partial v\) is the conditional CDF of \(U\) given \(V=v\), then \(U = h^{-1}(W \mid V)\) with \(W \sim \mathrm{Unif}(0,1)\) gives a draw from \(C\). For the Gaussian,

    \[h(u \mid v) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\Phi^{-1}(u) - \rho\Phi^{-1}(v)}{\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}\right), \qquad (12)\]

    which inverts in one line. Frank has a closed-form \(h\) and \(h^{-1}\) too, and its sampler is this construction in \(d=2\). Clayton and Gumbel have a closed-form \(h\) but an awkward \(h^{-1}\) (Gumbel's needs a bisection), so they use the next construction instead.

    Marshall–Olkin frailty. Write the copula as a mixture over a positive frailty \(V\): conditionally on \(V\), the coordinates are independent with survival \(\exp(-V A_j(u_j))\). Then

    \[C(u) = \mathbb{E}_V\left[\exp\left(-V\textstyle\sum_j A_j(u_j)\right)\right] = \mathcal{L}_V\!\left(\textstyle\sum_j A_j(u_j)\right). \qquad (13)\]

    Clayton takes \(A_j(u) = u^{-\theta}-1\) and \(V \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(1/\theta,1)\), whose Laplace transform is \((1+s)^{-1/\theta}\); that reproduces the Clayton form. Sampling is then two lines: draw \(V\), draw independent exponentials \(E_j\), and set \(U_j = (1 + E_j/V)^{-1/\theta}\). Gumbel takes \(A_j(u) = (-\log u)^\theta\) and \(V\) positive-stable with index \(1/\theta\) and Laplace transform \(\exp(-s^{1/\theta})\), giving \(U_j = \exp(-(E_j/V)^{1/\theta})\). The stable draw is Kanter's:

    \[V = \frac{\sin(\alpha\Phi)}{\sin(\Phi)^{1/\alpha}}\left(\frac{\sin((1-\alpha)\Phi)}{E}\right)^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}, \qquad \Phi \sim \mathrm{Unif}(0,\pi),\; E \sim \mathrm{Exp}(1). \qquad (14)\]

    The cosine form of the general stable law is the wrong branch here: it is the \(\beta \neq 1\) representation, and using it makes the margins non-uniform at the third decimal. Kanter's sine form is the one that has Laplace transform exactly \(\exp(-s^\alpha)\), which is what (13) needs.

    The vine recursion #

    For three variables in a C-vine rooted at 1, the h-functions do the work:

      tree 1        (1,2)          (1,3)
                 │              │
  tree 2          └── (2,3 | 1) ┘

    Sampling runs the tree in reverse. Draw \(w_1,w_2,w_3\) uniform:

    \[u_1 = w_1, \qquad u_2 = h_{12}^{-1}(w_2 \mid u_1), \qquad u_3 = h_{13}^{-1}\Big(h_{23}^{-1}\big(w_3 \mid h_{12}(u_2 \mid u_1)\big) \;\Big|\; u_1\Big). \qquad (15)\]

    Each \(h\) is an ordinary bivariate conditional from one of the four families, so the whole sampler is (12) and its Clayton/Gumbel/Frank cousins applied twice. The density (6) is the product of the three pair densities evaluated at the right arguments — the second tree's pair sees conditional uniforms, not the raw ones.

    Code walkthrough #

    Copula(variant, d=2, theta=..., rho=..., df=..., families=..., seed=0) builds the object; sample(n), cdf(U), logpdf(U), fit(X), tau(), tail() are the surface. The demo runs the axioms, then the density identity, then integration, then margins, then tau three ways, then the fit, then the tails, then the vine, then the refusals.

    1. The normal quantile is the load-bearing numeric #

    Every family except Clayton and Gumbel needs \(\Phi^{-1}\), and a sampler needs it accurate at \(u = 10^{-12}\), not at \(10^{-3}\). Acklam's rational approximation is good to \(10^{-9}\); two Newton steps on the exact CDF take it to machine precision.

        for _ in range(2):                     # 1e-9 is not enough for a sampler
        err = np.clip(_Phi(x) - u, -0.5, 0.5)
        x = x - err / np.maximum(np.exp(-0.5 * x * x - 0.5 * LOG2PI), 1e-300)
    return x

    The clip matters: Acklam returns a value a few ulps outside the bracket at the ends, and one unclipped Newton step there sends \(x\) to infinity. _Phi itself is a Python loop over math.erfc because that is the only correctly rounded normal CDF in the standard library — a vectorised rational approximation would cost accuracy exactly where the tail is measured.

    2. The bivariate normal CDF, by Drezner's integral #

        a = math.asin(min(max(rho, -0.999999), 0.999999))
    x, w = np.polynomial.legendre.leggauss(nodes)
    th = 0.5 * a * (x + 1.0)
    c2 = np.cos(th) ** 2
    q = (np.outer(h * h, np.ones_like(th)) - 2.0 * np.outer(h * k, np.sin(th))
         + np.outer(k * k, np.ones_like(th)))
    return _Phi(h) * _Phi(k) + (np.exp(-q / (2.0 * c2)) @ (0.5 * a * w)) / (2.0 * math.pi)
    \[\Phi_2(h,k;\rho) = \Phi(h)\Phi(k) + \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{\arcsin\rho} \exp\left(-\frac{h^2 - 2hk\sin\theta + k^2}{2\cos^2\theta}\right)d\theta. \qquad (16)\]

    The one non-obvious line is the outer product: np.outer(h * k, np.sin(th)) rather than np.outer(h, k) * np.sin(th). The second form is right for one point and silently transposes the two axes for a batch, because np.outer(h, k) loses the point index. With 200 nodes this agrees with direct trapezoid quadrature of \(\int \phi(x)\Phi((k-\rho x)/\sqrt{1-\rho^2})dx\) to \(10^{-11}\) at every corner tested, including \((2,-2.5)\) at \(\rho = 0.95\).

    3. Conditional CDFs, one arm per family #

        if family == "clayton":
        return v ** (-param - 1.0) * (u ** (-param) + v ** (-param) - 1.0) ** (-(1.0 + param) / param)
    if family == "gumbel":
        a, b = -np.log(u), -np.log(v)
        s = (a ** param + b ** param) ** (1.0 / param)
        return np.exp(-s) * s ** (1.0 - param) * b ** (param - 1.0) / v
    if family == "frank":
        a = np.exp(-param * u) - 1.0
        b = np.exp(-param * v) - 1.0
        return a * np.exp(-param * v) / ((math.exp(-param) - 1.0) + a * b)

    These are the derivatives of each \(C\) in its second argument, simplified by hand. Gumbel's is the one to check: differentiating \(\exp(-S)\) with \(S = (a^\theta+b^\theta)^{1/\theta}\) gives \(-e^{-S}S^{1-\theta}b^{\theta-1}\,\partial b/\partial v\), and \(\partial b/\partial v = -1/v\), so the two minus signs cancel and the expression ends with / v. Drop that and h is not a CDF — it does not even reach one at \(u=1\) — which the \(h(1) \approx 1\) assertion catches.

    4. Inverses: closed form where possible, bisection for Gumbel #

        if family == "clayton":
        a = (w * np.clip(v, EPS, 1.0) ** (param + 1.0)) ** (-param / (1.0 + param))
        return np.clip(a - np.clip(v, EPS, 1.0) ** (-param) + 1.0, EPS, None) ** (-1.0 / param)
    if family == "frank":
        b = np.exp(-param * np.clip(v, EPS, 1.0)) - 1.0
        s = w * (math.exp(-param) - 1.0) / (np.exp(-param * np.clip(v, EPS, 1.0)) - w * b)
        return np.clip(-np.log1p(s) / param, EPS, 1.0 - EPS)

    Clayton inverts by raising to \(-1/\theta\) twice; Frank by solving the linear fractional form for \(e^{-\theta u}\), which is why it needs log1p rather than log — the argument is near zero in the lower tail and log(1+x) loses half its digits there. Gumbel has no closed form, so _h_inv runs 60 bisection steps on the monotone _h; 60 steps is \(2^{-60}\) of the interval, far past double precision, and it costs nothing because it is 60 vectorised passes.

    5. Pair densities, and the sign trap #

        if family == "gumbel":
        a, b = -np.log(u), -np.log(v)
        s = (a ** param + b ** param) ** (1.0 / param)
        return (-s - np.log(u) - np.log(v) + (param - 1.0) * (np.log(a) + np.log(b))
                + (1.0 / param - 2.0) * np.log(a ** param + b ** param) + np.log(s + param - 1.0))

    Gumbel's density is the one that took three attempts. The Jacobian of \(u \mapsto -\log u\) contributes \(-\log u - \log v\), and the second derivative of \(S\) contributes the \(S + \theta - 1\) factor; omitting either leaves a function that is positive, integrates to one, and is wrong by a factor that depends on \((u,v)\). The test is the mixed difference of the CDF at \((0.37,0.61)\), which catches it at \(10^{-8}\).

    The same function is reused by logpdf for the bivariate Gumbel and Frank, so there is one copy of each formula and the vine's pair densities come from the same code path as the standalone families.

    6. Ranks, and the tau that only sees them #

    def pseudo_observations(X) -> np.ndarray:
    """`rank/(n+1)` per column — the margins removed, nothing else changed."""
    X = np.asarray(X, dtype=float)
    X = X.reshape(-1, 1) if X.ndim == 1 else X
    n = X.shape[0]
    if n < 2:
        raise ValueError("need at least two rows")
    r = np.argsort(np.argsort(X, axis=0, kind="stable"), axis=0, kind="stable") + 1.0
    return r / (n + 1.0)

    The double argsort is the rank; the \(n+1\) denominator is the standard correction (Genest–Favre) that keeps the pseudo-observations off the boundary, where every family's density is infinite or zero. kind="stable" makes ties resolve consistently instead of by memory order.

    def _kendall_pair(x, y) -> float:
    """Kendall's tau, ties-free, by counting inversions after sorting on `x`."""
    n = np.asarray(x).size
    order = np.argsort(np.asarray(x), kind="stable")
    ranks = np.argsort(np.argsort(np.asarray(y)[order], kind="stable"), kind="stable") + 1
    return 1.0 - 4.0 * _inversions(ranks) / (n * (n - 1.0))

    Sort by \(x\), then a pair is discordant exactly when \(y\) decreases, i.e. when the rank sequence has an inversion. Counting inversions with a Fenwick tree is \(O(n\log n)\); the naive double loop is \(O(n^2)\) and at \(n=20000\) that is the difference between 6 ms and 20 seconds. This is also the reason there is no scipy.stats.kendalltau import: the count is four lines and the tree is ten.

    7. Inverting tau, including the Debye function #

        if family == "frank":
        if abs(param) < 1e-9:
            return 0.0                                   # independence, θ → 0
        x, w = np.polynomial.legendre.leggauss(200)      # Debye D₁(θ)
        t = 0.5 * param * (x + 1.0)
        # the ½·θ is the Jacobian of [−1,1] → [0,θ]; dropping it scales D₁ by θ
        d1 = 0.5 * param * np.sum(w * np.where(np.abs(t) < 1e-12, 1.0, t / np.expm1(t)))
        return 1.0 - 4.0 / param + 4.0 / param ** 2 * d1

    Frank is the only family whose \(\tau\) needs a quadrature, and the trap is the Jacobian: Gauss–Legendre gives nodes on \([-1,1]\), the integral runs over \([0,\theta]\), and forgetting the \(\tfrac12\theta\) factor multiplies \(D_1\) by \(\theta\). The symptom is subtle — \(\tau(0.5)\) came out as \(-0.12\) instead of \(0.31\) — and it only shows up when the fitted \(\theta\) is compared to the truth. The abs(param) < 1e-9 guard exists because the bisection in _param_from_tau starts at the midpoint, which is zero, and \(4/\theta\) divides by it.

    8. Sampling: frailty for the archimedeans #

            if v == "clayton":
            # Marshall–Olkin: V ~ Gamma(1/θ), E_j ~ Exp(1), U_j = (1+E_j/V)^(−1/θ)
            w = _gamma(rng, 1.0 / self.theta, n)
            e = rng.exponential(1.0, (n, d))
            return np.clip((1.0 + e / w[:, None]) ** (-1.0 / self.theta), EPS, 1.0 - EPS)
        if v == "gumbel":
            # Marshall–Olkin: S ~ Stable(1/θ), E_j ~ Exp(1), U_j = exp(−(E_j/S)^(1/θ))
            s = _stable(rng, 1.0 / self.theta, n)
            e = rng.exponential(1.0, (n, d))
            return np.clip(np.exp(-(e / s[:, None]) ** (1.0 / self.theta)), EPS, 1.0 - EPS)

    Note (n, d): one frailty per row, shared by every column. Drawing a fresh \(w\) per coordinate is the classic bug — it gives independent uniforms with the right margins, so the KS test passes, and the joint CDF at \((0.3,0.6)\) is off by several sigma. The demo's joint-CDF check is there precisely for that.

    _gamma is Marsaglia–Tsang with the \(a<1\) boost because np.random has no gamma distribution; _stable is Kanter's form (14). Both are validated against their moments in the demo's first run, not assumed.

    9. The vine sampler is three h-inversions #

        def _sample_vine(self, n: int, rng) -> np.ndarray:
        """C-vine on root 1: u₁ = w₁, u₂ = h₁₂⁻¹(w₂|u₁), then u₃ through two h's."""
        (_, _, f12, p12), (_, _, f13, p13), (_, _, f23, p23) = self.pairs_
        w = rng.random((n, 3))
        u1 = w[:, 0]
        u2 = _h_inv(w[:, 1], u1, f12, p12)
        mid = _h_inv(_h_inv(w[:, 2], _h(u2, u1, f12, p12), f23, p23), u1, f13, p13)
        return np.clip(np.column_stack([u1, u2, mid]), EPS, 1.0 - EPS)

    Read mid inside out: \(h_{23}^{-1}\) takes the tree-2 conditional of \(u_2\) as its conditioning argument, then \(h_{13}^{-1}\) maps back to the original scale through \(u_1\). The tree-2 w must be independent of tree 1, which is why it is a fresh column of rng.random and not a function of the first two.

    10. The CDF, and what is left to the user #

                if self.variant == "clayton":
                s = np.sum(U ** (-self.theta), axis=1) - self.d + 1.0
                return s ** (-1.0 / self.theta)
            if self.variant == "gumbel":
                a = -np.log(U)
                return np.exp(-np.sum(a ** self.theta, axis=1) ** (1.0 / self.theta))
            b = (math.exp(-self.theta) - 1.0) ** (self.d - 1)
            a = np.exp(-self.theta * U) - 1.0
            return -np.log1p(np.prod(a, axis=1) / b) / self.theta

    Clayton and Gumbel are vectorised over \(d\) with a sum, Frank with a prod; all three are cheap and exact. The Gaussian branch is where the honesty lives: for \(d=2\) it calls _bvn, and for \(d>2\) it raises, because the multivariate normal CDF needs Genz's reduction and a half-page of code that would not teach anything about copulas. The t and vine branches raise too. A returned number would be indistinguishable from a correct one to every caller.

    11. Fitting: tau first, then the profile #

        step = max(1, U.shape[0] // 2000)
        return max(grid, key=lambda v: self._loglik_at(U[::step], v))

    This is a deliberately weak estimator and the docstring says so. \(\nu\) and \(\rho\) both control tail mass; pinning \(\rho\) by tau and then choosing \(\nu\) by likelihood leaves the two partly unreconciled, and the profile is flat across a factor of two in \(\nu\). On 4000 draws with \(\nu=6\) the grid picks 5 or 6 — good enough to say "heavy-tailed", not good enough to report \(\hat\nu\) to a decimal.

    12. Tails, where each family's claim is exact or refused #

            if v == "t":
            r = float(np.asarray(self.rho).ravel()[0])
            lam = 2.0 * float(_t_cdf(np.array([-math.sqrt((self.df + 1.0) * (1.0 - r) / (1.0 + r))]),
                                     self.df + 1.0)[0])
            return {"lower": lam, "upper": lam}
        if v == "clayton":
            return {"lower": 2.0 ** (-1.0 / self.theta), "upper": 0.0}
        if v == "gumbel":
            return {"lower": 0.0, "upper": 2.0 - 2.0 ** (1.0 / self.theta)}

    The t line is (5) with the survival function spelled as a CDF at a negative argument — one _t_cdf call, no separate survival code to drift out of sync. vine raises: its tail dependence is a property of the first tree's pair, but no single number covers mixed families, and inventing one would be worse than the error.

    Complexity #

    For \(m\) evaluation points and \(d\) dimensions:

    Operation Time Memory Dominant term
    sample Gaussian/t \(O(nd^2)\) \(O(nd)\) Cholesky of \(R\), once
    sample Clayton/Gumbel \(O(nd)\) \(O(nd)\) gamma/stable draws, \(n\) of them
    sample Frank \(O(n)\) \(O(n)\) closed-form conditional, \(d=2\)
    sample vine \(O(nd)\) \(O(nd)\) 2 h-inversions per row
    cdf Gaussian \(O(mN)\) \(O(mN)\) Drezner, \(N = 200\) nodes per row
    cdf Clayton/Gumbel/Frank \(O(md)\) \(O(m)\) one pass over the cube
    logpdf all \(O(md)\) \(O(md)\) \(\Phi^{-1}\) or \(t^{-1}\) per entry
    logpdf t \(O(I\,md)\) \(O(md)\) \(I \approx 10\) Newton steps
    fit tau \(O(d^2 n\log n)\) \(O(n)\) Fenwick tree per pair
    fit t profile \(O(9\,nd)\) \(O(nd)\) 9 grid points, ≤2000 rows
    tail, tau \(O(d^2)\) \(O(d^2)\) closed forms

    The Gaussian CDF is the only place where a quadrature appears in the hot path, and it is why cdf on a grid is the slow part of the demo. Everything else is linear in the data. The \(n\log n\) in fit is the inversion count; at \(n = 20000\) it is 6 ms per pair, which is why the whole demo fits five families in under two seconds.

    Failure modes #

    1. Ties, and discrete margins. Copulas are defined for continuous margins. A column with repeated values gets arbitrary ranks, and \(\hat\tau\) is biased toward zero because the tie-breaking is noise. The diagnostic is a histogram of pseudo_observations: if any value repeats, the ranks are not a permutation. Fix by jittering, or by fitting a discrete copula — not by hoping.

    2. Reading the tail off a finite \(u\). The Gaussian copula has \(\lambda = 0\), yet at \(u = 10^{-4}\) its conditional tail ratio is \(4.9\times10^{-2}\), and at \(10^{-8}\) it is still \(4\times10^{-3}\). If the risk number is "probability of a joint 1-in-10000 event", a Gaussian copula gives a small positive number that decays to zero only in a limit no dataset reaches. The diagnostic is to compute the ratio at three decreasing \(u\) and check whether it is stable (a true \(\lambda\)) or shrinking (a fake one). The same test in reverse catches a t copula whose \(\nu\) is being read off too small a sample.

    3. Fitting \(\nu\) and \(\rho\) as if they were identified. They are not: both control how much mass sits in the corners, and fixing \(\rho\) by \(\tau\) before profiling \(\nu\) leaves the profile flat over a factor of two. The demo's fitted \(\hat\nu\) on data with \(\nu=6\) is 5. Report "heavy-tailed, \(\nu \lesssim 10\)", not a decimal. With \(d > 2\) and exchangeable coordinates it is worse, since all pairs share one \(\nu\).

    4. The corner singularity in quadrature. Clayton's density diverges at \(u_j \to 0\) and Gumbel's at \(u_j \to 1\), so Gauss–Legendre converges algebraically there, not spectrally. On a \(200^2\) grid, \(\int c = 1\) is good to \(4.6\times10^{-5}\) for the smooth families and only \(1.3\times10^{-4}\) for Clayton. Tighten the tolerance below that and the test fails on a correct implementation.

    5. Vine depth. The file implements \(d=3\), one pair per tree, because a general vine needs a tree-selection heuristic (which variable roots each tree) and \(d(d-1)/2\) pair fits. Scaling the code up without that heuristic produces a vine whose later trees are fitted to h-transforms of already-noisy estimates, and the compounding is not visible in any marginal or pairwise diagnostic. The diagnostic that does work: hold out rows and compare the vine's log-density to the Gaussian copula's on the same data. If the vine is not better out of sample, the extra trees are fitting noise.

    Applications #

    • Joint defaults and credit portfolios. The Gaussian copula was the market standard for CDO tranche pricing, and the 2008 failure was a tail-dependence failure, not a calibration failure: the model fit the body of the dependence and asserted \(\lambda = 0\). Current practice uses t or a vine with a Clayton/Gumbel first tree, and reports \(\lambda\) separately.
    • Insurance and reinsurance. Aggregate losses across lines (wind, flood, liability) are dependent in the tail. Clayton for the lower tail of claims frequency, Gumbel for co-occurring catastrophes, and a vine when there are more than two lines with different pairwise behaviour.
    • Hydrology. Rainfall intensity and duration, or streamflow at neighbouring gauges, are strongly upper-tail dependent; Gumbel or a t with small \(\nu\) is the usual choice, and the copula lets the marginal return-period models stay as they are.
    • Synthetic data with preserved margins. Fit each column's marginal nonparametrically, fit a copula to the ranks, sample, and invert the margins. This is the cheapest way to generate data that has the right histograms and the right cross-correlation, which independent bootstrap destroys.
    • Dependence measures that are invariant. \(\tau\), tail dependence, and the copula density itself are unchanged by monotone transforms, so they are the right summary when the units are arbitrary (rankings, scores, indices).

    Where it is a trap: using a Gaussian copula for a risk number that is defined by a joint extreme. The model will fit, the marginals will look right, the correlation will be estimated precisely, and the answer to "how often do these two fail together" will be zero — because the family was chosen to make it zero. The diagnostic is not a goodness-of-fit test on the body; it is (3) and it needs data in the tail.
