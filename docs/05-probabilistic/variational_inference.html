    05 · Probabilistic models/tier 2/5 variants/source

    Variational inference

    Intuition #

    Integration is the bottleneck. The posterior is a ratio of two quantities, and the denominator — the evidence — is an integral over every parameter. MCMC sidesteps it by sampling; variational inference avoids it by bounding it.

    The bound comes from one line of algebra. For any density \(q(z)\),

    \[\log p(x) = \log \int q(z)\,\frac{p(z, x)}{q(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z \;\ge\; \int q(z) \log \frac{p(z, x)}{q(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z \;=\; \mathrm{ELBO}(q)\]

    which is Jensen's inequality, and it is an equality exactly when \(q\) is the posterior. Turning the inequality around gives the reason the method works:

    \[\log p(x) - \mathrm{ELBO}(q) \;=\; \mathrm{KL}\!\left(q(z)\,\|\,p(z \mid x)\right) \;\ge\; 0 \qquad (1)\]

    The gap is not an abstraction. It is a distance between two distributions, it is computable in the conjugate cases, and it has exactly two sources:

    • the family. If the truth is not inside the set of \(q\)'s you allow, no amount of optimisation closes the gap. This is the approximation gap.
    • the optimiser. Finite steps, finite samples, a step size that is too large. This is the estimation gap, and it is supposed to be small.

    Everything in this file is an attempt to move one of those two numbers, and the demo is built to separate them: a target with a known answer is fitted by a family that contains it (diagonal, 0.511 away from the truth; full covariance, 0.002) and by a family that does not.

    The punchline of the mean-field story is a theorem you can see in two dimensions. A factorised \(q\) is always too narrow: the marginal variance it reports is smaller than the true one. In the demo the truth has variance \(2.778\) and the diagonal approximation reports \(1.000\). The means, remarkably, are exact — the correlation is thrown away only in the second moment.

    Variants #

    All five variants maximise the same objective. They differ in the family and in how the expectation under \(q\) is differentiated.

    Variant Family \(q\) Target it accepts Gradient needed Use when
    mean_field diagonal Gaussian GaussianTarget none (closed form) the target is quadratic; you want the exact mean-field solution in one pass
    cavi \(q(\mu)q(\tau)\), Gaussian × Gamma array of observations none (closed form) the model is conjugate and the coordinate updates are derivable
    reparameterization diagonal Gaussian log_prob + grad yes differentiable target, \(d\) from 2 to 10⁶ — the default
    structured_vi full-covariance Gaussian via Cholesky log_prob + grad yes the posterior is strongly correlated and \(d\) is small
    black_box_vi diagonal Gaussian log_prob only no the density is a simulator, a compiled model, or has no usable gradient

    The three gradient variants are the same loop with a different estimator for \(\nabla_\phi \mathbb{E}_q[\log p]\). mean_field and cavi are the closed forms available when the expectation is analytic; they exist here so that the Monte Carlo estimators can be checked against something exact.

    Derivation #

    1. The bound and what it costs #

    \[\mathrm{ELBO}(q) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(z, x)] + \mathbb{H}[q(z)] \qquad (2)\]

    Two terms, and the tension between them is the whole method. The first wants \(q\) concentrated where the joint density is high; the second wants \(q\) spread out. Their sum is maximised in between, at the posterior. Maximising (2) is therefore mode-seeking but entropy-regularised — the reason VI is never as sharp as a Laplace approximation at the mode, and never as confident as a point estimate.

    Because \(\log p(x)\) does not depend on \(q\), any \(q\) that raises (2) is closer to the posterior in KL. That is the licence to use any optimiser: the bound is a proper objective function.

    2. Mean field: why the variance is pinned #

    Factorise \(q(z) = \prod_j q_j(z_j)\) and take the target in information form,

    \[\log p(z) = -\tfrac12 z^{\top} P z + b^{\top} z \qquad (3)\]

    unormalised, since the ELBO only needs \(p\) up to a constant. The optimal factor for coordinate \(j\) is \(\log q_j^\star(z_j) = \mathbb{E}_{-j}[\log p(z)] + \text{const}\), and the expectation of a quadratic in \(z_j\) is another quadratic:

    \[\log q_j^\star(z_j) = -\tfrac12 P_{jj} z_j^2 + z_j\Big(b_j - \sum_{k \ne j} P_{jk} m_k\Big) + \text{const} \qquad (4)\]

    Reading off the Gaussian gives both updates at once:

    \[v_j = \frac{1}{P_{jj}}, \qquad m_j = \frac{b_j - \sum_{k \ne j} P_{jk} m_k}{P_{jj}} \qquad (5)\]

    The variance is not the exact posterior variance \([P^{-1}]_{jj}\); it is fixed by the diagonal entry alone, and by Cauchy–Schwarz \([P^{-1}]_{jj} \ge 1/P_{jj}\). The means, however, solve \((Pm)_j = b_j\) at the fixed point, i.e. \(m = P^{-1}b\) — and \(P^{-1}b\) is exactly the posterior mean. Mean field gets the means right and the variances wrong, whenever the target is Gaussian. That is not a coincidence: it is the reason VI is used for means, and the reason its uncertainties are unusable without a correction.

    In the demo, \(P = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0.8\\ 0.8 & 1\end{pmatrix}\) and \(b = (0.5, -0.25)^{\top}\). The means converge to \((1.944, -1.806)\) to machine precision in 53 sweeps, the variances are reported as \((1.000, 1.000)\) against a truth of \((2.778, 2.778)\), and the bound sits at \(2.550\) against an evidence of \(3.061\) — a KL of \(0.511\), all of it the discarded correlation.

    3. CAVI on a conjugate model #

    The Normal–Gamma model is the smallest model where coordinate ascent is exact and the answer is known:

    \[x_i \mid \mu, \tau \sim N(\mu, \tau^{-1}), \qquad \mu \mid \tau \sim N\!\left(\mu_0, (\kappa_0\tau)^{-1}\right), \qquad \tau \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(a_0, b_0) \qquad (6)\]

    with \(q(\mu, \tau) = q(\mu)q(\tau)\). The two conditionals are Gaussian and Gamma, so every expectation in (2) is a closed form in \(\psi(a)\), \(\log b\), \(a/b\):

    \[a = a_0 + \frac{n+1}{2}, \qquad \mu = \frac{\kappa_0\mu_0 + n\bar x}{\kappa_0 + n} \qquad (7)\]
    \[v = \frac{b}{(\kappa_0+n)\,a}, \qquad b = b_0 + \tfrac12\kappa_0\left(v + (\mu - \mu_0)^2\right) + \tfrac12\left(\textstyle\sum_i (x_i-\mu)^2 + n v\right) \qquad (8)\]

    The n+1 in (7) is the tell. The exact posterior for \(\tau\) has shape \(a_0 + n/2\): the likelihood contributes \(n/2\), the prior on \(\mu\) contributes \(\tfrac12\) through the integral when you marginalise \(\mu\) out. Coordinate ascent does not integrate \(\mu\); it replaces \(\mu\) by its mean, so the \(\tfrac12\log\tau\) from \(p(\mu \mid \tau)\) survives as an extra half-degree. The variational factor is therefore a Gamma with the wrong shape — barely wrong at \(n=40\) (\(22.5\) against \(22.0\)), catastrophic at \(n=0\).

    Substituting \(v\) into \(b\) makes the fixed point explicit, because the system is linear in \(b\):

    \[b = b_N + \frac{b}{2a} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b = \frac{b_N}{1 - 1/(2a)}, \qquad b_N = b_0 + \tfrac12\left[\textstyle\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2 + \frac{\kappa_0 n}{\kappa_n}(\bar x - \mu_0)^2\right] \qquad (9)\]

    So \(q(\tau)\) keeps the exact posterior's rate, inflated by \(1/(1 - 1/(2a))\) — about 2% at \(a=22.5\) — and the whole approximation is one number. The demo checks (9) to \(10^{-12}\), and checks the consequence: \(q(\mu)\) reports a variance of \(0.1007\) against the exact \(0.1055\), the same underestimate as Section 2.

    The evidence, computed by integrating (6) analytically, is

    \[\log p(x) = -\frac{n}{2}\log 2\pi + \tfrac12\log\frac{\kappa_0}{\kappa_n} + a_0\log b_0 - a_n\log b_n + \log\Gamma(a_n) - \log\Gamma(a_0) \qquad (10)\]

    with \(\kappa_n = \kappa_0 + n\), \(a_n = a_0 + n/2\), \(b_n = b_N\). Using \(a_0 + (n+1)/2\) here instead of \(a_0 + n/2\) — the variational shape, which is the tempting copy-paste — makes the "ELBO" exceed the evidence by \(0.7\) nats on this demo. The bound is only a bound if both sides describe the same prior.

    4. Pathwise gradients #

    For a reparameterisable family, \(z = m + L\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \sim N(0, I)\) moves the randomness outside the parameters, so the gradient goes through the sample:

    \[\nabla_m \mathrm{ELBO} = \mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_z \log p(z)\right], \qquad \nabla_L \mathbb{E}_q[\log p] = \mathbb{E}\left[g(z)\,\varepsilon^{\top}\right], \quad g = \nabla_z\log p \qquad (11)\]

    Since \(L\) is Cholesky, only the lower triangle is a free parameter; the upper triangle is noise and must be masked. The entropy of a Gaussian in \(L\) is \(\tfrac12\log\det(2\pi e\,LL^{\top})\), whose derivative with respect to each diagonal entry is exactly \(1/L_{ii}\) — a term with no sampling error at all.

    For the diagonal family, parameterise \(\log\sigma\) (unconstrained, and the \(+1\) in the entropy derivative becomes a literal + 1.0):

    \[z = m + \sigma\odot\varepsilon, \qquad \nabla_m = \mathbb{E}[g], \qquad \nabla_{\log\sigma} = \mathbb{E}[g \odot \sigma\odot\varepsilon] + 1 \qquad (12)\]

    The reason this is the default is variance, not elegance. At a fixed \(q\), the demo measures the gradient of the logistic posterior and finds the score-function estimator 921× and 887× noisier in the two coordinates. The pathwise estimator costs one gradient evaluation per sample and has the variance of the gradient; the score-function estimator has the variance of the density times the score, which grows as \(1/\sigma^2\) precisely when the fit is getting sharp.

    5. Score-function gradients #

    When there is no gradient, the identity \(\nabla_\phi q = q\,\nabla_\phi\log q\) still holds, so the derivative moves onto the log-density:

    \[\nabla_\phi \mathbb{E}_q[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_q\left[f(z)\,\nabla_\phi \log q(z)\right] \qquad (13)\]

    For a diagonal Gaussian this is \(\nabla_m = f(z)\,(z-m)/\sigma^2\) and \(\nabla_{\log\sigma} = f(z)\,(\varepsilon^2 - 1)\), plus the same entropy term. Subtracting a baseline \(B\) is free — \(\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log q] = 0\) — and it is the difference between usable and not: the estimator's variance is \(\mathrm{Var}(f)\), so centring \(f\) removes the part of the noise that has nothing to do with the parameter.

    Code walkthrough #

    The public API is one frozenset, and fit dispatches on it.

    VARIANTS = frozenset(
    ["mean_field", "cavi", "reparameterization", "structured_vi", "black_box_vi"]
)

    fit takes three different kinds of target because the variants genuinely consume different information: an array (a dataset, for cavi), a GaussianTarget (an information-form quadratic, for mean_field), or a callable pair. The ValueError in the last branch is deliberate — a structured_vi call with no gradient fails at the door with what to do instead.

        def fit(self, target: object, grad: Grad | None = None, *,
            mean0: np.ndarray | None = None) -> VariationalInference:
        """Run the variant. See the class docstring for what `target` must be."""
        if self.variant == "cavi":
            return self._fit_cavi(np.asarray(target, dtype=float))
        if self.variant == "mean_field":
            return self._fit_mean_field(target)
        if self.variant == "black_box_vi":
            return self._fit_gradient(target, None, mean0)
        if grad is None:
            raise ValueError(
                f"variant {self.variant!r} needs grad=...; use 'black_box_vi' when only "
                "the log density is available"
            )
        return self._fit_gradient(target, grad, mean0)

    The mean-field loop is (5) written out. off is the off-diagonal contribution \(P_{j,\cdot}\,m\) minus the term already owned by coordinate \(j\); recomputing it per coordinate is what makes a sweep \(O(d^2)\) rather than a matrix inverse. The variance never appears in the loop at all — it is 1 / P_jj, known before the first step.

            P, b = target.precision, target.linear
        lam = np.diag(P).copy()
        m = np.zeros(b.size)
        self.elbo_history_ = []
        n_done = 0
        for _ in range(self.n_iter):
            n_done += 1
            previous = m.copy()
            for j in range(b.size):
                off = P[j] @ m - P[j, j] * m[j]
                m[j] = (b[j] - off) / lam[j]
            self.elbo_history_.append(self._elbo_exact(target, m, 1.0 / lam))
            if np.max(np.abs(m - previous)) < self.tol:
                break

    All four gradient variants share one optimiser loop and differ only in the estimator. _pack turns the free parameters into (mean, covariance) once per recorded step, so the ELBO is always evaluated at a valid Gaussian — the log σ and Cholesky parameterisations never produce a non-positive covariance.

                if self.variant == "black_box_vi":
                grads, baseline = self._score_grads(log_prob, m, log_s, rng, baseline)
            elif structured:
                grads = self._pathwise_grads(grad, m, rng, L=L)
            else:
                grads = self._pathwise_grads(grad, m, rng, log_s=log_s)
            opt.step(grads)

    The pathwise estimator is (11) and (12). The structured branch's one subtle line is np.tril: the gradient of \(LL^{\top}\) with respect to the upper triangle is zero, so any entry above the diagonal must be dropped or the factor slowly drifts out of the triangular family. The two branches then add the entropy derivative — 1 / diag(L) for the Cholesky parameterisation, 1.0 for \(\log\sigma\).

            eps = rng.standard_normal((self.n_samples, m.size))
        if L is not None:
            z = m + eps @ L.T
            g = np.array([grad(row) for row in z])
            g_m = g.mean(axis=0)
            g_L = np.tril((g.T @ eps) / self.n_samples)
            g_L[np.diag_indices_from(g_L)] += 1.0 / np.diag(L)
            return [g_m, g_L]
        s = np.exp(log_s)
        z = m + s * eps
        g = np.array([grad(row) for row in z])
        g_m = g.mean(axis=0)
        g_ls = (g * (s * eps)).mean(axis=0) + 1.0
        return [g_m, g_ls]

    The score-function estimator is (13). The baseline is an exponential moving average of log p at the current \(q\), which is the cheap choice: a single sample per step still produces a centred advantage, and the return value feeds the next step.

            s = np.exp(log_s)
        eps = rng.standard_normal((self.n_samples, m.size))
        z = m + s * eps
        lp = np.array([log_prob(row) for row in z])
        level = float(lp.mean()) if baseline is None else baseline
        adv = lp - level
        g_m = (adv[:, None] * (z - m) / s**2).mean(axis=0)
        g_ls = (adv[:, None] * (eps**2 - 1.0)).mean(axis=0) + 1.0
        return [g_m, g_ls], 0.9 * level + 0.1 * float(lp.mean())

    The ELBO itself is estimated with log q analytic, because a Gaussian's log density is known in closed form even when \(p\) is not. That halves the variance of the diagnostic: only the \(\log p\) half is sampled.

            d = mean.size
        L = np.linalg.cholesky(cov)
        eps = rng.standard_normal((n_samples, d))
        z = mean + eps @ L.T
        lp = np.array([log_prob(row) for row in z])
        solve = np.linalg.solve(L, (z - mean).T).T
        log_q = -0.5 * (d * LOG2PI + 2.0 * np.sum(np.log(np.diag(L))) + np.sum(solve**2, 1))
        return float(np.mean(lp - log_q))

    CAVI is (7)–(9) in six lines. mu is computed once before the loop and never moves, because the update for \(\mu\) contains \(\mathbb{E}[\tau]\) in both numerator and denominator and it cancels — a fact worth noticing, since it is why the Normal–Gamma mean-field problem is one-dimensional even though it looks like two.

            a = a0 + 0.5 * (n + 1.0)
        mu = (kappa0 * mu0 + n * xbar) / (kappa0 + n)
        var_mu = 1.0 / (kappa0 + n)
        b = b0 + 0.5 * float(np.sum((x - xbar) ** 2))

    The convergence test is the one non-obvious line in the file. Near a fixed point the ELBO is flat — its gap is quadratic in the parameter error — so a threshold on \(\Delta\mathrm{ELBO}\) retires the loop while \(b\) is still wrong in the fifth digit. The loop therefore watches the parameter; the contraction factor is \(1/(2a)\), so seven sweeps are enough for twelve digits.

                self.elbo_history_.append(self._cavi_elbo(x, mu, var_mu, a, b))
            # Stop when the *parameter* stops moving, not when the ELBO does:
            # near a fixed point the bound is flat (its gap is quadratic in the
            # error), so an ELBO threshold retires the loop while b is still
            # wrong in the fifth digit. This fixed point contracts by 1/(2a).
            if abs(b - previous) < self.tol * max(1.0, abs(b)):
                break

    Finally the evidence, (10), which the demo uses as the ceiling. The docstring records the half-degree, because that is the mistake this function exists to detect.

    def _normal_gamma_evidence(x: np.ndarray, mu0: float, kappa0: float, a0: float,
                           b0: float) -> float:
    """`log p(x)` under the Normal-Gamma prior, in closed form.

    The shape here is `a0 + n/2`, *not* the `a0 + (n+1)/2` that the variational
    factor picks up: marginalising `μ` out of the joint is not the same as
    averaging it out of `log p`, and the extra half-degree is exactly the
    mean-field distortion in `q(τ)`.
    """

    Complexity #

    With \(d\) parameters, \(n\) observations, \(S\) draws per step and \(T\) steps, and letting \(c_p\), \(c_g\) be the cost of one log-density and one gradient evaluation:

    Variant Per sweep/step Memory Sweeps or steps to converge
    mean_field \(O(d^2)\) \(O(d^2)\) for \(P\) \(O(d)\) at worst, correlation-limited
    cavi \(O(n)\) \(O(n)\) \(\sim\log(1/\epsilon)/\log(2a)\) — seven here
    reparameterization \(O(S\,c_g + d)\) \(O(d)\) thousands; set by the noise floor
    structured_vi \(O(S\,c_g + d^2)\) \(O(d^2)\) thousands, and the \(d^2\) parameterisation dominates
    black_box_vi \(O(S\,c_p + d)\) \(O(d)\) thousands more, for the same accuracy

    The asymmetry in the last column is the practical content of the file. Closed forms converge geometrically and exactly; stochastic gradients converge at \(O(1/\sqrt{T})\) and stop moving at a noise floor set by \(\mathrm{lr} \times \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(g) / S}\). That is why the demo's structured_vi recovers the covariance to \(0.097\) rather than to machine precision: it is a Monte Carlo ELBO, and the last three percent are noise.

    Storing elbo_history_ is \(O(T)\); the ELBO itself is evaluated every n_iter // 100 steps, which is why the curve has 100 points regardless of \(T\).

    Failure modes #

    The variance is too small and nobody notices. This is the signature failure, not an edge case: a factorised \(q\) is under-dispersed by construction. The demo measures it three ways in one run — 1.000 against 2.778 on the quadratic target, a correlation of \(-0.81\) forcing a diagonal \(q\) to be 40% narrower than the Laplace approximation, and \(0.1007\) against \(0.1055\) in the conjugate model. Every credible interval built from std_ is too short, and downstream decision rules that compare an uncertain quantity to a threshold will be overconfident. Diagnostic: fit a structured_vi to the same target; if the covariance changes the uncertainty, the diagonal family was the problem, not the data.

    The bound exceeds the evidence. Impossible, so it means the two sides disagree about the model. In this file that happens exactly once, and it is instructive: using the variational shape \(a_0 + (n+1)/2\) in (10) instead of the posterior's \(a_0 + n/2\) reports an evidence of \(-93.209\) against a bound of \(-92.512\) — the bound stands \(0.70\) nats above the ceiling it is supposed to respect. The same class of bug appears when the target is unnormalised and its constant is added back by hand. Diagnostic: evaluate at \(q = p\). The ELBO must come out equal to the evidence to numerical precision; if it does not, the two formulas are not describing the same model.

    The step size is the accuracy. With lr = 0.06 and 256 samples the structured_vi covariance is off by \(0.097\); quadrupling the samples and halving the step does not beat that by much, because the gradient's variance never goes away. Two failure shapes follow. Too large a step: the iterate oscillates around the optimum and the covariance jitters — visible as a non-monotone elbo_history_ tail. Too small a step in black_box_vi: the fit stops short, and the reported std_ \((0.277, 0.392)\) sits below the pathwise fit's \((0.303, 0.416)\) — under-dispersion that is the optimiser's fault, on top of the family's. Diagnostic: rerun with two seeds and one different step size. An answer that moves by more than the reported standard error is not converged.

    The score-function gradient dies as the fit sharpens. The estimator's variance carries \(1/\sigma^2\); a tight posterior is exactly when black_box_vi stops being able to move. The demo needs 6,000 steps and 24 samples to match what 2,000 steps and 8 samples achieve with gradients, ending \(0.03\) away in the mean and narrower in both coordinates. Fix: a baseline (implemented), control variates, or a reparameterisable family.

    Applications #

    Latent variable models at scale. The reparameterisation trick is what made the variational autoencoder possible: an amortised encoder \(q_\phi(z \mid x)\) turns per-datapoint inference into a shared network, and (11) makes the gradient of the ELBO flow through the sample. Every VAE, and every normalising flow trained variationally, is this file's reparameterization variant plus a neural network for \(q\).

    Topic models and mixture models. LDA and its descendants are fit by CAVI — the same coordinate ascent as _fit_cavi, with Dirichlet factors. When the corpus is too large to sweep, stochastic VI replaces the full-data expectation with a minibatch estimate and keeps the same bound; gmm.py does the mixture-model version of this with a Dirichlet prior on the weights.

    PPLs and general-purpose inference. Stan, PyMC and NumPyro all ship an ADVI mode that is mean_field with automatic differentiation supplying grad, plus fullrank (this file's structured_vi with a transformed Cholesky factor). It is the first thing to run on a new model because it is fast and gives a gradient of the bound — not because it gives a correct posterior.

    Sparse Gaussian processes. Titsias' variational approximation to a GP posterior is an ELBO over inducing points, and it is the reason sparse GPs are cheap; gaussian_process.py's sparse_gp variant is the SoR form of the same idea.

    Where it is a trap: a low-dimensional, strongly correlated, or multimodal posterior. A diagonal Gaussian cannot represent a banana, and the KL it minimises is the reverse KL — it will happily put mass in a valley between two modes rather than commit to one. mcmc.py exists for those cases, and a model small enough for MCMC should not be approximated.
