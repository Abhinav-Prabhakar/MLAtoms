    05 · Probabilistic models/tier 3/5 variants/source

    Dirichlet processes

    Intuition #

    Every mixture model this far needed \(k\) up front. The DP removes that requirement by putting a prior on the distribution itself, and letting the data decide how many components to occupy:

    \[G \sim \mathrm{DP}(\alpha, G_0), \qquad \theta_i \mid G \sim G, \qquad x_i \mid \theta_i \sim F(\theta_i) \qquad (1)\]

    \(G\) is a random probability measure. Draw it once, then draw the parameters from it — and because a DP draw is discrete, the \(\theta_i\) collide, and the collisions are the clusters. The number of distinct values is not a parameter and not fixed: it is a random variable whose distribution the concentration \(\alpha\) controls.

    The same object has two faces, and the code uses both.

      stick-breaking                          Chinese restaurant
  ───────────────                         ──────────────────
  v_k ~ Beta(1, α)                        table 1: ● ● ●      n₁ = 3
  w_k = v_k ∏_{j<k}(1−v_j)                table 2: ●          n₂ = 1
  G = Σ_k w_k δ_{θ_k}                     table 3: new?
  ├──●───────●───●────●────────► w        p(join k) ∝ n_k
    0.4  0.24  .14  .09   ...             p(new)    ∝ α

    Left: break a unit stick at \(v_1\), keep the piece, break the rest at \(v_2\), and so on. The pieces are the mixture weights, and they are a size-biased permutation — the first is the largest in expectation, which is what makes a DP mixture look like a finite mixture with a decaying tail.

    Right: seat customers at tables one at a time, each preferring a crowded table in proportion to its current occupancy, or opening a new one with probability proportional to \(\alpha\). This is the view that turns into a sampler, because it gives the conditional distribution of one label given all the others — which is all a Gibbs sweep needs.

    The consequence that matters for modelling is that \(\alpha\) controls \(k\) logarithmically:

    \[\mathbb{E}[K_n] = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+i-1} \approx \alpha\log\!\left(1+\frac{n}{\alpha}\right) \qquad (2)\]

    Four times the data does not give four times the clusters; it gives a constant number more. That is the whole reason to use a DP instead of growing \(k\) by cross-validation: it is a prior on model size, and it is a weak one.

    Three ideas in this chapter do the same job differently, and the differences are worth stating once:

    what is random how \(k\) is chosen cost
    finite mixture + BIC \(k\) by search penalised likelihood one fit per \(k\)
    DP mixture the measure posterior over \(k\) Gibbs, \(O(TnK)\)
    DP-means nothing threshold \(\lambda\) \(O(nKd)\) per sweep

    The last one is the small-variance limit of the first: as the cluster variance goes to zero, the DP mixture's MAP estimate becomes \(k\)-means with a penalty \(\lambda\) per cluster, and "open a new cluster" becomes "this point is more than \(\sqrt{\lambda}\) from every centroid". The demo's \(\lambda = 9\) is a distance of 3, and on data whose cluster centres are 5.7 to 8 apart it recovers them exactly.

    Variants #

    Variant Object Fitted by Exact statement the demo checks
    crp the partition prior seating, one draw at a time EPPF sums to 1 over all 52 partitions of 5
    stick_breaking the weights GEM, one stick at a time \(\mathbb{E}[w_k]=\alpha^{k-1}/(1+\alpha)^k\)
    dpmm an infinite mixture collapsed Gibbs predictive = NIW Student-t integral
    truncated_dp a \(K\)-component mixture collapsed Gibbs over \(K\) sticks truncation error = residual mass
    dp_means hard clustering assignment + split same partition as Lloyd at \(k=3\)

    crp and stick_breaking are the prior, not a model, so fit refuses them and says which method to use instead. They are variants because they are the two ways a reader meets a DP, and because every assertion about them is exact: partitions of five items are enumerable, and \(\mathbb{E}[w_k]\) is a product of beta means.

    The other three are fitters, and they differ in how much of the DP they keep:

    • dpmm keeps all of it. Collapsed Gibbs over a CRP prior, with the cluster parameters integrated out. The predictive is a Student-t, exactly.
    • truncated_dp keeps \(K\) sticks. Same sampler, finite prior, and a measurable error: the mass left on the sticks it did not use. On the demo's data it uses 3 of the 8 it was offered, carries 0.9921 of the mass, and leaves 0.0079.
    • dp_means keeps none of it. No posterior, no \(\alpha\), one threshold.

    The two Gibbs samplers share the predictive and differ in one line — the prior on the assignment:

    dpmm           p(z_i = k) ∝ n_k           p(z_i = new) ∝ α
truncated_dp   p(z_i = k) ∝ E[w_k | n]    no new component beyond K

    That is the entire difference between an infinite mixture and its truncation, and it is one substitution in the code.

    Derivation #

    1. What a Dirichlet process is #

    For every finite measurable partition \(A_1,\dots,A_k\) of the space,

    \[\left(G(A_1),\dots,G(A_k)\right) \sim \mathrm{Dirichlet}\!\left(\alpha G_0(A_1),\dots,\alpha G_0(A_k)\right) \qquad (3)\]

    That is the definition, and it has two immediate consequences. First, \(G(A)\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha G_0(A),\alpha(1-G_0(A)))\), so \(G\) is a probability measure with a prior mean \(G_0\) and a prior "sample size" \(\alpha\). Second, \(G\) is almost surely discrete: draw two independent \(\theta,\theta'\) from \(G\) and they are equal with positive probability, because a discrete measure puts mass on atoms.

    Ferguson (1973) defined it this way; Blackwell and MacQueen (1973) gave the Pólya urn that makes it usable, and Sethuraman (1994) gave the construction that makes it computable.

    2. Stick-breaking #

    Let \(v_1,v_2,\dots\) be independent \(\mathrm{Beta}(1,\alpha)\) and \(\theta_k\) independent \(G_0\). Then

    \[w_k = v_k\prod_{j

    is a draw from \(\mathrm{DP}(\alpha,G_0)\). The weights sum to one because the residual after \(K\) sticks is

    \[R_K=\prod_{k=1}^{K}(1-v_k), \qquad \mathbb{E}[R_K]=\left(\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\right)^{K} \qquad (5)\]

    and \(R_K\to0\) almost surely. Because the \(v_k\) are independent,

    \[\mathbb{E}[w_k]=\mathbb{E}[v]\left(\mathbb{E}[1-v]\right)^{k-1}=\frac{\alpha^{k-1}}{(1+\alpha)^{k}} \qquad (6)\]

    which is a geometric decay: the mixture weights of a DP mixture are not exchangeable across components, they are ordered by expected size. That is why truncating at \(K\) is not the same as a \(K\)-component mixture with free weights — the truncation has to break the last stick somewhere.

    3. The partition, and the restaurant #

    Integrating \(G\) out turns the DP into a distribution over partitions. For a partition into clusters of sizes \(n_1,\dots,n_K\) summing to \(n\), the exchangeable partition probability function is

    \[p(n_1,\dots,n_K)=\frac{\alpha^{K}\prod_{k=1}^{K}(n_k-1)!}{\alpha(\alpha+1)\cdots(\alpha+n-1)} \qquad (7)\]

    which is what eppf computes in log form with \(\Gamma\) functions. It depends only on the sizes, not on the labels or the order — that is exchangeability, and it is the reason a DP is a sensible prior for clustering: the data's order carries no information.

    Summing (7) over all set partitions gives one. That is not obvious from the formula, which is why the demo checks it by brute force: 52 terms for \(n=5\), 203 for \(n=6\), both exactly 1.

    The conditional of one label given the rest is the seating rule:

    \[p(z_i=k\mid z_{-i})=\frac{n_k^{(-i)}}{\alpha+n-1}, \qquad p(z_i=\text{new}\mid z_{-i})=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+n-1} \qquad (8)\]

    and (2) follows by summing the probability of a new table over \(i=1,\dots,n\).

    4. The mixture, and why the predictive is a Student-t #

    Attach a likelihood and the DP becomes a mixture with infinitely many components and a prior on their weights. For a Gaussian likelihood with a normal–inverse-\(\chi^2\) prior on \((\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\),

    \[\sigma^2\sim\mathrm{InvGamma}(a_0,b_0), \qquad \mu\mid\sigma^2\sim N\!\left(\mu_0,\frac{\sigma^2}{\kappa_0}\right) \qquad (9)\]

    the posterior given \(m\) points is available in closed form:

    \[\kappa_m=\kappa_0+m, \quad \mu_m=\frac{\kappa_0\mu_0+m\bar x}{\kappa_m}, \quad a_m=a_0+\frac{m}{2}, \quad b_m=b_0+\frac{1}{2}\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2+\frac{\kappa_0 m}{2\kappa_m}(\bar x-\mu_0)^2 \qquad (10)\]

    and the posterior predictive — what a Gibbs sweep needs — is

    \[p(x_*\mid\text{cluster})=t_{2a_m}\!\left(\mu_m,\ \frac{b_m(\kappa_m+1)}{a_m\kappa_m}\right) \qquad (11)\]

    a Student-t with \(2a_m\) degrees of freedom. Two things follow. The predictive has tails heavier than a Gaussian's, which is correct: the variance is itself unknown. And with \(m=0\) the same formula is the prior predictive, which is why the code can use one function for "join cluster \(k\)" and "open a new table".

    The demo checks (11) against a numerical integral over the posterior of \(\sigma^2\): substituting \(\tau=b_m/\sigma^2\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(a_m,1)\), the integral is

    \[p(x_*)=\int_0^\infty N\!\left(x_*;\mu_m,\frac{b_m}{\tau}\left(1+\frac{1}{\kappa_m}\right)\right)\frac{\tau^{a_m-1}e^{-\tau}}{\Gamma(a_m)}\,d\tau \qquad (12)\]

    and 400k trapezoid points agree with the closed form to \(10^{-13}\).

    5. Collapsed Gibbs, in one line #

    Because the cluster parameters are integrated out, a sweep only touches the labels. For point \(i\), remove it from its cluster and sample

    \[p(z_i=k)\propto\begin{cases}n_k^{(-i)}\,p(x_i\mid\text{cluster }k) & k \text{ occupied} \\ \alpha\,p(x_i\mid\text{prior}) & \text{new table}\end{cases} \qquad (13)\]

    Everything is in log space, because \(n_k\) can be zero-sized and because a Student-t density underflows fast. The subtlety is the leave-one-out: cluster \(k\)'s predictive must be computed without \(x_i\), or \(x_i\) contributes to its own likelihood and every cluster looks better than it is. This is not a cosmetic detail — with the point left in, the sampler in this file still converged, and on the truncated variant it over-split.

    6. Truncation, and the size of the error #

    Replace the infinite sum (4) with \(K\) terms and normalise, or keep the last stick's residual as a point mass. The cost is a bound: for any measurable set \(A\),

    \[\left\lvert \sum_{k\le K}w_k\,\delta_{\theta_k}(A)-\sum_{k}w_k\,\delta_{\theta_k}(A)\right\rvert\le R_K \qquad (14)\]

    so the \(L_1\) distance between the truncated and full weight vectors is at most \(\mathbb{E}[R_K]\), and for a single draw it is exactly \(R_K\) — the total-variation distance between the two probability measures is half that. The demo asserts the exact version: the \(L_1\) distance between its 3-component weight vector and the 203-component one is 0.0079, the residual, to \(10^{-12}\).

    For sampling, the truncated model has a closed-form conditional too. With counts \(n=(n_1,\dots,n_K)\),

    \[v_j\mid n\sim\mathrm{Beta}\!\left(1+n_j,\ \alpha+\sum_{l>j}n_l\right) \qquad (15)\]

    independently, so the mean weight of component \(k\) is a product of beta means, which is posterior_weights. No sampling of the sticks is needed for the assignment step, and the leftover mass \(R_K\) enters prediction as one extra component with the prior predictive — which is exactly what a component beyond the truncation would predict.

    The practical rule is that \(K\) should be large enough that \(R_K\) is small on the fitted solution, and no larger, because every extra stick redistributes mass. The demo offers 8 sticks, uses 3, and reports the 0.0079 it left behind.

    7. The small-variance limit #

    Put a penalty \(\lambda\) per cluster into the DP mixture's MAP objective, scale it as \(\lambda/2\sigma^2\) so that it survives the limit, and let the cluster variance go to zero:

    \[\sum_i\log N\!\left(x_i;\mu_{z_i},\sigma^2\right)-\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma^2}K\;\xrightarrow[\sigma^2\to0]{}\;-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\left[\sum_i\left\lVert x_i-\mu_{z_i}\right\rVert^2+\lambda K\right] \qquad (16)\]

    so the surviving objective is

    \[\min_{\{z,\mu\}}\ \sum_i\left\lVert x_i-\mu_{z_i}\right\rVert^2+\lambda K \qquad (17)\]

    \(k\)-means with a price per cluster. The algorithm is what the objective suggests: assign each point to its nearest centroid, and if the squared distance exceeds \(\lambda\), pay \(\lambda\) and open a new cluster there. Both operations decrease (17), so the loop terminates, and at \(\lambda\) between the within-cluster and between-cluster squared distances it finds exactly the clusters \(k\)-means with the right \(k\) would find — without being told \(k\).

    \(\lambda\) is not \(\alpha\). It has units of squared distance, it has no posterior, and it is the price at which the model prefers a new cluster to a bad fit. That is a smaller, cruder model than the DP mixture, and the demo is explicit that on this data it happens to agree with the Bayesian answer.

    Code walkthrough #

    The EPPF is (7), and the only care needed is to stay in logs: the factorials are \(\Gamma(n_k)\) and the normaliser is \(\Gamma(\alpha+n)/\Gamma(\alpha)\).

        def eppf(self, counts) -> float:
        """Exchangeable partition probability of cluster sizes `counts`."""
        counts = np.asarray(counts, dtype=float)
        n, k = counts.sum(), counts.size
        logp = (k * math.log(self.alpha)
                + float(sum(math.lgamma(c) for c in counts))
                + math.lgamma(self.alpha) - math.lgamma(self.alpha + n))
        return math.exp(logp)

    The GEM weights are (4). 1 − U^(1/α) is a \(\mathrm{Beta}(1,\alpha)\) draw by inverse CDF, which avoids needing a beta sampler, and the residual is returned rather than dropped — it is the truncation error and the demo uses it.

        w = v * np.concatenate([[1.0], np.cumprod(1.0 - v)[:-1]])
        return w, float(np.prod(1.0 - v))

    posterior_weights is (15) plus the product of means. tail is the suffix sum \(\sum_{l>k}n_l\), which is what makes the sticks dependent: the mass available to stick \(k\) is what the earlier sticks left.

            counts = np.asarray(counts, dtype=float)
        tail = np.concatenate([np.cumsum(counts[::-1])[::-1][1:], [0.0]])
        ev = (1.0 + counts) / (1.0 + counts + self.alpha + tail)
        w = ev * np.concatenate([[1.0], np.cumprod(1.0 - ev)[:-1]])
        return w, float(np.prod(1.0 - ev))

    The NIW posterior is (10). n = 0 returning the prior is not a special case to be handled elsewhere: it is the new-table prediction, and the same function serves both.

    The predictive is (11). The scale is the part people get wrong: it is \(b(\kappa+1)/(a\kappa)\), not \(b/a\), because the predictive has to account for the uncertainty in \(\mu\) as well as in \(\sigma^2\).

    def _predictive_logpdf(x: float, params) -> float:
    """`p(x | cluster)` after integrating `(μ, σ²)` out of the NIW."""
    mu, kappa, a, b = params
    scale = math.sqrt(b * (kappa + 1.0) / (a * kappa))
    return float(_log_t(x, 2.0 * a, mu, scale))

    Complexity #

    \(n\) points, \(d\) dimensions, \(K\) occupied clusters, \(T\) sweeps:

    Step Time Memory
    EPPF of one partition \(O(K)\) \(O(1)\)
    one CRP draw of \(n\) labels \(O(nK)\) \(O(n)\)
    one GEM draw of \(K\) sticks \(O(K)\) \(O(K)\)
    one Gibbs sweep (naive, as written) \(O(n^2Kd)\) \(O(nd+nK)\)
    one Gibbs sweep (cached statistics) \(O(nKd)\) \(O(nd+nK)\)
    one DP-means sweep \(O(nKd)\) \(O(nK)\)

    The code is the naive version: _cluster_params recomputes every cluster's sufficient statistics before every point's update, which is \(O(nKd)\) per point and \(O(n^2Kd)\) per sweep. That is fine at \(n=120\) and wrong at \(n=10^5\); the fix is to keep \((\text{count},\sum x,\sum x^2)\) per cluster and update them when a point moves, which is what every production sampler does and why the second row exists. With cached statistics the cost is the same as DP-means.

    The demo runs all five variants in about 2.2 s, of which most is the 20,000 partition draws that exist to make the \(\mathbb{E}[K_n]\) check tight; the two Gibbs fits take 0.3 s and 0.5 s, and the 400k-point quadrature is under a millisecond.

    Failure modes #

    Over-splitting, and the prior on the variance. The most common surprise: a DP mixture puts four clusters on three. This is not a bug and not a sampler problem — with a vague prior on \(\sigma^2\) and a weak prior on \(\mu\), splitting one Gaussian cluster into two buys enough marginal likelihood to pay for the extra component. It happened here during development: with \(\kappa_0=0.05\), \(a_0=2\), \(b_0=0.3\) the count wandered between three and five across five seeds of both samplers, on clusters whose centres are six units apart with noise 0.4. Tightening to \(\kappa_0=0.5\), \(a_0=5\), \(b_0=1\) made all five seeds agree on three, which is the configuration in the file. Diagnostic: if the count is unstable across seeds, the count is not identified; report the co-clustering matrix, not \(K\).

    Label switching. Cluster labels are exchangeable, so two chains can agree perfectly on the partition and disagree on the labels. Any average of per-cluster parameters across chains is meaningless. Diagnostic: summarise with \(p(z_i=z_j)\), an \(n\times n\) matrix that is invariant to relabelling.

    \(\alpha\) is not a knob for \(k\). Equation (2) is logarithmic: to double the expected number of clusters you square the data. Setting \(\alpha\) to hit a target \(K\) is possible and standard (Teh et al.'s VIP does it properly), but treating \(\alpha\) as "the number of clusters" produces a model that is not the one intended. Diagnostic: print expected_clusters(n) before fitting, and be surprised if the posterior is far from it.

    Truncation that truncates too much. \(K\) too small forces real clusters to share a stick, and the fitted residual \(R_K\) cannot reveal it because the sampler will never leave mass unused. Diagnostic: fit at \(K\) and \(2K\) and compare the predictive log-likelihood; if it moves, \(K\) was the constraint.

    The residual as a component. In truncated_dp the leftover mass predicts like the prior, which is right and also why the predictive has heavier tails than the fitted mixture suggests. A single extrapolated point can therefore be predicted better by the residual than by the nearest cluster. That is a feature of the model, not an artefact.

    The leave-one-out omission. Including \(x_i\) in its own cluster's statistics inflates that cluster's density at \(x_i\), which biases the sampler toward keeping points where they are and, in the truncated variant, toward splitting clusters that should be merged. Diagnostic: the sampler converges to a stable answer with the wrong \(K\); check the mask.

    Gibbs is a local method. Two seeds, two modes, and no guarantee that either is the global one. The posterior over partitions is enormous, and \(T\) sweeps of a Gibbs chain explore a tiny neighbourhood. Diagnostic: run several chains from CRP-prior initialisations, and compare the partitions rather than the parameters.

    DP-means is not Bayesian. \(\lambda\) has units, no posterior, and no uncertainty. Two data sets scaled by 10 need different \(\lambda\) for the same answer. And the algorithm's answer depends on the order of the points within a sweep, through which clusters exist when a point is visited. Diagnostic: rescale the data to unit variance and re-run; if the number of clusters changes, \(\lambda\) was doing the work rather than the data.

    Diagonal covariance. The code uses one NIW per coordinate, so each cluster is axis-aligned. Correlated clusters are split, because two axis-aligned components fit a tilted one better than one. The full-covariance version needs a normal–inverse-Wishart prior and a matrix inverse per cluster; the sampler's structure is identical.

    Applications #

    Density estimation and clustering without \(k\). The core use. Whenever the number of groups is a finding rather than an input — customer segments, astronomical object classes, cell populations in flow cytometry — the DP is the default Bayesian answer, and its posterior over \(K\) is the deliverable.

    Topic models. Latent Dirichlet allocation fixes the number of topics; its DP version lets the corpus decide, and the hierarchical DP (Teh et al., 2006) does it per document while sharing topics across the corpus. It is the reason "how many topics" is not a hyperparameter in the modern literature.

    Genetics and species sampling. The EPPF (7) is the Ewens sampling formula, which is the null model for allele frequencies and for discovery counts in population genetics. The same algebra appears as the prior in Dirichlet process mixtures for genotype clustering.

    Entity resolution and record linkage. Each entity is a cluster of records; the number of entities is unknown and grows with the data. The DP's exchangeability assumption is exactly right for records arriving in arbitrary order, and the predictive (11) gives a calibrated "does this record belong to an existing entity" score.

    Spatial and image segmentation. With a spatial base measure, DP mixtures segment images and cluster geo-referenced counts without a chosen number of regions. The truncated version is what fits on a GPU.

    Scalable clustering. DP-means is the practical answer when \(n\) is large and a Gibbs chain is not: \(O(nKd)\) per sweep, no posterior, and the number of clusters emerges. It is used as a fast alternative to \(k\)-means when \(k\) is unknown and to hierarchical clustering when \(n\) is too large for a dendrogram.

    Where it is a trap. Data with heavy-tailed clusters (the Gaussian likelihood fights back), problems where the number of clusters is genuinely fixed by the design, and any application that reports \(K\) without its posterior. Also: treating DP-means' \(\lambda\) as if it had a Bayesian interpretation, which it does not.
