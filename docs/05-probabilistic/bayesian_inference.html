    Bayesian inference

    Intuition #

    Bayes' theorem is a division. The interesting part is that for a few prior–likelihood pairs the division closes: the posterior comes back in the same family as the prior, and "doing Bayesian inference" becomes addition.

    \[\underbrace{p(\theta \mid y)}_{\text{posterior}} \;\propto\; \underbrace{p(\theta)}_{\text{prior}} \times \underbrace{p(y \mid \theta)}_{\text{likelihood}} \qquad (1)\]

    Conjugacy is the property that makes this exact and cheap. A Gaussian prior with a Gaussian likelihood returns a Gaussian; a Beta prior with Bernoulli data returns a Beta; a Dirichlet with categorical data returns a Dirichlet. The posterior's parameters are the prior's parameters plus a sufficient statistic — literally a + successes, α + counts. No sampler, no optimiser, no approximation, and the answer is the whole distribution rather than a point.

    That changes what you can ask. A point estimate answers "what value?"; a posterior answers "what values, and how sure?", which is what a decision rule needs. A credible interval is read directly off the distribution, and the predictive distribution of the next observation is a different (wider) object than the posterior of the parameter.

    The MLE and the MAP sit at the edges of this family: the MLE is the posterior mode under a flat prior, and the MAP is a penalised likelihood with the negative log-prior as the penalty. Writing both as methods of the same object makes the relationship visible rather than rhetorical.

    Variants #

    Variant Family Posterior What it computes
    mle normal — \bar y; the likelihood with no prior
    map normal N(μ_n, σ_n^2) the mode: prior and data weighted by precision
    conjugate_normal normal N(μ_n, σ_n^2) the full Gaussian posterior
    beta_binomial beta \mathrm{Beta}(a+k, b+n-k) Bernoulli data, Beta prior, predictive pmf
    dirichlet_multinomial dirichlet \mathrm{Dir}(α + c) categorical data, one update by addition
    credible_intervals any the above equal-tailed and HPD intervals
    posterior_predictive any the above the distribution of the next draw

    The two interval/predictive variants are not new models — they are what you do with a posterior once you have one. Pass family="beta" to run them on the discrete posterior; the HPD machinery is where the two families differ substantially.

    Derivation #

    Normal–normal: precisions add #

    Take μ \sim N(μ_0, σ_0^2) and y_i \mid μ \sim N(μ, σ^2) with σ^2 known. The log posterior is quadratic in μ, so the posterior is Gaussian; completing the square gives

    \[\frac{1}{σ_n^2} = \frac{1}{σ_0^2} + \frac{n}{σ^2}, \qquad μ_n = σ_n^2\left(\frac{μ_0}{σ_0^2} + \frac{n\bar y}{σ^2}\right). \qquad (2)\]

    Precisions add, and the mean is the precision-weighted average. Written as a shrinkage toward the prior,

    \[μ_n = (1-w)\,μ_0 + w\,\bar y, \qquad w = \frac{nσ_0^2}{nσ_0^2 + σ^2}, \qquad (3)\]

    the weight on the data is the fraction of total precision the data contributes. n = 0 gives w = 0 (the prior is the posterior); σ_0 → ∞ gives w → 1 (the MLE). Nothing about this depends on the shape of the prior beyond its first two moments, which is why Gaussian priors are the default even when they are not literally believed.

    MAP is a penalised likelihood #

    Maximising (1) instead of integrating it gives the mode. For the Gaussian case the log-prior is -(μ-μ_0)^2/2σ_0^2, so

    \[\hat μ_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\min_μ\; \frac{1}{2σ^2}\sum_i (y_i-μ)^2 + \frac{1}{2σ_0^2}(μ-μ_0)^2, \qquad (4)\]

    which is ridge regression with λ = σ^2/σ_0^2. Every "regularised" estimator is a MAP estimate under some prior; the regulariser is the prior, and its strength is the prior's precision. For a symmetric posterior the MAP and the posterior mean coincide, which is why (2) and (4) give the same number — and why the distinction between them only becomes visible in skewed families like Beta.

    Beta–Bernoulli: counting #

    With p \sim \mathrm{Beta}(a,b) and k successes in n Bernoulli trials,

    \[p \mid y \sim \mathrm{Beta}(a+k,\; b+n-k), \qquad \mathbb{E}[p \mid y] = \frac{a+k}{a+b+n}. \qquad (5)\]

    a and b are pseudo-counts: a \mathrm{Beta}(2,2) prior is worth two successes and two failures you did not have to collect. That is also the honest way to see what a prior costs — with n = 10 it is a fifth of the data; with n = 10^6 it is invisible.

    The posterior predictive for the next observation is not p evaluated at its mean; it is the posterior averaged over p:

    \[P(y_{n+1}=1 \mid y) = \mathbb{E}[p \mid y] = \frac{a+k}{a+b+n}, \qquad (6)\]

    and for m future trials the count follows a beta-binomial,

    \[P(K=k) = \binom{m}{k}\frac{B(a'+k,\; b'+m-k)}{B(a',b')}, \qquad (7)\]

    with a' = a+k, b' = b+n-k. Its variance is the binomial variance inflated by the posterior spread:

    \[\mathrm{Var}[K] = \frac{m\,a'b'\,(a'+b'+m)}{(a'+b')^2(a'+b'+1)}, \qquad (8)\]

    which exceeds m\,p̂(1-p̂) whenever a'+b' is finite. Using a binomial predictive after a Beta fit understates the uncertainty, and with small n it understates it badly — that is the beta-binomial's entire reason to exist.

    Dirichlet–categorical: counting in a vector #

    The same update, componentwise: α \leftarrow α + c, where c is the vector of counts. The marginal of each coordinate is \mathrm{Beta}(α_i, α_0-α_i), so every Beta result above applies per category, and the predictive for one new draw is

    \[P(z_{n+1}=i \mid c) = \frac{α_i + c_i}{α_0 + n}, \qquad \mathrm{Var} = \frac{p_i(1-p_i)}{α_0+n+1}, \qquad (9)\]

    which is the multinomial variance divided by α_0+n+1 — again, the posterior spread entering the predictive.

    Credible intervals, and what they are not #

    A 95% equal-tailed interval puts 2.5% of the posterior mass in each tail; the highest-posterior-density (HPD) interval is the shortest interval with 95% mass, defined by f(lo) = f(hi). They coincide for any symmetric unimodal posterior, which is why the normal case needs no search and the Beta case does.

    A 95% credible interval is not a 95% confidence interval, and the difference is not academic. The credible interval says "given the prior, θ is in here with probability 0.95" — a statement about θ. The confidence interval says "this procedure covers the truth 95% of the time" — a statement about the procedure. They agree exactly in one important case: a flat prior on a location parameter, where the posterior is proportional to the likelihood. Then the Bayesian interval is the frequentist one, and the demo checks the coverage empirically at 94% over 500 replications.

    With an informative prior they diverge, and the direction is predictable: the credible interval is centred near the prior, so it under-covers when the prior is wrong and over-covers when it is right. That is not a bug, it is the cost of using information you did not collect.

    Why the predictive is wider than the posterior #

    The posterior describes θ; the predictive describes a future y. For the normal case the two differ by exactly the observation variance:

    \[y_{n+1} \mid y \;\sim\; N\!\left(μ_n,\; σ^2 + σ_n^2\right). \qquad (10)\]

    Plugging \hat θ into the likelihood instead — the "plug-in predictive" — gives N(μ_n, σ^2) and is over-confident by an amount that vanishes as n → ∞ but dominates for small n. The gap σ_n^2 is the uncertainty you paid data to remove, and it belongs in the forecast.

    Code walkthrough #

    The only piece that is not textbook algebra is the Beta CDF, and without scipy it has to be written. betainc uses the standard split — a power series near zero and a continued fraction near one — because the continued fraction converges slowly where the series is fast, and vice versa. The Lentz iteration is what makes the continued fraction numerically stable:

        for m in range(1, 300):
        m2 = 2 * m
        for num in (m * (b - m) * x / ((qam + m2) * (a + m2)),
                    -(a + m) * (qab + m) * x / ((a + m2) * (qap + m2))):
            d = 1.0 + num * d
            d = 1.0 / (d if abs(d) > tiny else tiny)
            c = 1.0 + num / c
            c = c if abs(c) > tiny else tiny
            h *= d * c
        if abs(d * c - 1.0) < eps:
            break

    betainc chooses the direction by the standard criterion x < (a+1)/(a+b+2): evaluate the continued fraction for I_x when x is small, and for 1-I_{1-x} when it is large, so the fraction always runs on the side where it converges:

        front = math.exp(math.lgamma(a + b) - math.lgamma(a) - math.lgamma(b)
                     + a * math.log(x) + b * math.log1p(-x))
    if x < (a + 1.0) / (a + b + 2.0):
        return front * _betacf(a, b, x) / a
    return 1.0 - front * _betacf(b, a, 1.0 - x) / b

    The quantile is a bisection on a monotone function. 200 iterations is far more than needed for 1e-14, and bisection cannot fail the way Newton can when the density vanishes at an endpoint — which it does for every a < 1:

        lo, hi = 0.0, 1.0
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if betainc(a, b, mid) < p:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
        if hi - lo < 1e-14:
            break
    return 0.5 * (lo + hi)

    The normal fit is equation (2) written once. Note that post_var_ is computed first and then used for the mean: computing the mean first and the variance after invites the two to disagree when one is edited:

                prec = 1.0 / sigma0 ** 2 + self.n_ / self.sigma ** 2
            self.post_var_ = 1.0 / prec
            self.post_mean_ = self.post_var_ * (
                mu0 / sigma0 ** 2 + self.n_ * self.mean_ / self.sigma ** 2)
            self.post_sd_ = math.sqrt(self.post_var_)

    The Beta MAP needs two guards that the formula hides. The mode (a-1)/(a+b-2) exists only for a, b > 1; when either is at or below one the density is unbounded at an endpoint and the mode is 0 or 1, not the ratio:

                if self.family == "beta":
                a, b = self.post_a_, self.post_b_
                if a <= 1.0:
                    return 0.0
                if b <= 1.0:
                    return 1.0
                return (a - 1.0) / (a + b - 2.0)

    The HPD interval is a one-dimensional root-find. lo moves right, hi is recovered from the mass constraint, and the objective is the density gap. The search range matters: lo can run up to F^{-1}(1-\text{level}), not to 1-\text{level}, because a concentrated posterior's shortest interval may start far from zero:

            def gap(lo: float) -> float:
            hi = beta_ppf(betainc(a, b, lo) + level, a, b)
            return beta_pdf(hi, a, b) - beta_pdf(lo, a, b)

        left, right = 1e-12, beta_ppf(1.0 - level, a, b)
        for _ in range(200):
            mid = 0.5 * (left + right)
            if gap(mid) > 0.0:          # gap decreases in `lo`: root is right
                left = mid
            else:
                right = mid

    The beta-binomial variance is the one formula people get wrong, because the naive answer is the binomial one. The extra (a'+b'+m)/(a'+b'+1) factor is the whole point: it is the inflation due to not knowing p:

            if self.family == "beta":
            a, b = self.post_a_, self.post_b_
            tot = a + b
            mean = n * a / tot
            # beta-binomial: the binomial variance inflated by the Beta spread
            var = n * a * b * (tot + n) / (tot ** 2 * (tot + 1.0))
            return mean, var

    The discrete pmf is computed entirely in logs and exponentiated once, because \binom{m}{k} overflows immediately for m = 40 and the Beta functions underflow at the same time:

                logpmf = (np.array([math.lgamma(n + 1) - math.lgamma(i + 1)
                                - math.lgamma(n - i + 1) for i in k])
                      + np.array([log_beta(a + i, b + n - i) for i in k])
                      - log_beta(a, b))
            return np.exp(logpmf)

    Complexity #

    n observations, K categories, m future trials.

    Operation Normal Beta Dirichlet
    fit O(n) O(n) O(n + K)
    estimate O(1) O(1) O(K)
    credible_interval O(1) O(200 · cf) O(200 · cf)
    predictive_pmf O(1) per point O(m · cf) O(K)

    Everything is linear in the data because the posterior depends on the data only through sufficient statistics — that is what conjugacy buys. The interval costs are bisection iterations times a continued fraction, which is why they are constant-bounded but not O(1) in the same sense as the estimate. A sampler would be O(T) with T in the thousands and an O(1/T) error; for these three families it would be strictly worse.

    Failure modes #

    The prior does the work when n is small. With n = 3, a \mathrm{Beta}(20,20) prior outvotes the data entirely, and the posterior sits near 0.5 no matter what was observed. The diagnostic is the effective sample size a+b against n; the fix is a weaker prior, not a different estimator.

    A credible interval is not a confidence interval. Under an informative prior the 95% credible interval can cover the truth 70% of the time if the prior is wrong, and 99% if it is right. The diagnostic is a simulation with a known truth (the demo does exactly this); the fix is either a flat prior or an explicit sensitivity analysis across priors — not a relabelling of the interval.

    MAP is not invariant to reparameterisation. A uniform prior on σ and a uniform prior on σ^2 are different priors, and they give different MAPs for the same data because the Jacobian differs. The posterior distribution is invariant when you transform it; a mode is not, when the prior was declared in the other coordinates. The diagnostic is a transform of the estimate that does not match the estimate of the transform. Posteriors are invariant, modes are not — which is another reason to report the interval.

    An improper flat prior can give an improper posterior. σ_0 = 10^6 is a numerical stand-in for "no prior", and it works here because the normal likelihood is proper. In a hierarchical model the same trick produces a posterior that is not integrable, and the sampler wanders off. The diagnostic is a posterior that does not stabilise as the chain lengthens.

    Binomial instead of beta-binomial. Fitting p with a Beta and then forecasting with \mathrm{Binomial}(m, \hat p) drops the (a'+b'+m)/(a'+b'+1) factor and understates the variance of every count forecast. The diagnostic is predictive coverage — the nominal 95% band contains the outcome far less than 95% of the time. This is the most common error in A/B-test forecasting.

    Applications #

    A/B testing is the canonical use: a Beta posterior on the conversion rate, a credible interval on the lift, and a beta-binomial predictive for the rest of the month. The beta_binomial variant is also the standard model for over-dispersed counts — anything counted with clustering (defects per batch, goals per match) where the binomial variance is too small.

    The conjugate normal is the basis of Kalman filtering (kalman.py is this update, applied recursively), of empirical Bayes in hierarchical models, and of shrinkage estimators like the James–Stein estimator. The Dirichlet update is the M-step of latent Dirichlet allocation and the foundation of gmm.py's Bayesian cousin.

    Where it is a trap: any model where the posterior is not conjugate and a sampler is needed (the conjugacy here is a convenience, not a law); any decision that depends on the tails of the posterior, where the prior's shape matters more than its moments; and any setting where the parameter is high-dimensional — the Dirichlet is the only member here that scales to more than a handful of parameters.
