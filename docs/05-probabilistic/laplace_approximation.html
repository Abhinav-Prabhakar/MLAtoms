    05 · Probabilistic models/tier 3/4 variants/source

    Laplace approximation

    Intuition #

    The Laplace approximation is one sentence: fit a parabola to the log-density at its peak, and exponentiate it. A parabola exponentiated is a Gaussian, so the posterior becomes

    \[p(\theta \mid y) \approx N\!\left(\hat\theta,\; P^{-1}\right), \qquad P = -\nabla^2\log p(\theta\mid y)\Big|_{\theta=\hat\theta} \qquad (1)\]

    Two steps, and only the second is an approximation. Finding \(\hat\theta\) is exact — it is a root of the gradient, and Newton's method on a log-concave density gets there in a handful of iterations (seven for the demo's logistic regression, with a final gradient of \(9\times10^{-16}\)). Everything approximate is in the curvature: the Gaussian matches the height and the width at the mode, and knows nothing else.

    That sounds like a crude approximation, and near the mode it is a very good one. The reason is the Bernstein–von Mises theorem: as data accumulate, the posterior becomes Gaussian, with width shrinking like \(n^{-1/2}\). Laplace is not a hack that happens to work — it is the leading term of the asymptotic expansion, and it is the reason MLEs have standard errors.

       log p(θ|y)                        p(θ|y)
      │  ╭─╮                          │   ╭╮
      │ ╱   ╲        ← θ̂            │  ╱  ╲
      │╱     ╲___                    │ ╱    ╲___
      └─────────── θ                 └─────────── θ
        the parabola matches           its exponential is the
        the top and the width          Gaussian we report

    The variants are four answers to "how much of the curvature can you afford":

    • full_hessian — all \(d^2\) entries of \(P\). What the method means.
    • diag_hessian — 1/diag(P), \(O(d)\) storage. It treats the other parameters as known, so it is overconfident by exactly \(1-\rho^2\); the demo measures \(\rho = -0.469\) and variances 22% too small.
    • kfac_laplace — a Kronecker product per layer, \(O(k^3 + d^3)\) to invert. Exact when the output-side factor is shared across samples, otherwise not; the demo is 40% off in the precision at one output unit.
    • predictive — full Hessian, and then integrate over it instead of plugging the mode into the link. The difference is the second-order term the delta method drops: 0.0086 in probability at the demo's test point, with the sign of \(-\sigma''(\mu)\).

    The demonstration model is Bayesian logistic regression, chosen because its exact posterior is a 2-D integral that a grid can compute. Every claim below is checked against that grid, not against another approximation: the covariance is within 13.9% entry by entry, the evidence within 0.0136 nats.

    Variants #

    Variant Covariance Cost When
    full_hessian \(P^{-1}\) \(O(d^3)\) \(d\) up to a few thousand; the default and the reference
    diag_hessian \(\mathrm{diag}(1/P_{ii})\) \(O(d)\) very large \(d\); overconfident, and useful as a cheap sanity check
    kfac_laplace \((\bar A \otimes X^\top X)^{-1}\) \(O(k^3 + d^3)\) neural-network layers, where the block structure is real
    predictive \(P^{-1}\), then integrated \(O(d^3 + m d^2)\) you want probabilities, not logits

    diag_hessian is the only variant here that is provably wrong in a direction: for positive-definite \(P\), \((P^{-1})_{ii} \ge 1/P_{ii}\) always. The demo asserts the inequality and the exact shrink factor.

    kfac_laplace is the odd one out. It exists because in a network the precision of a layer's weights is a sum of Kronecker products, and a Kronecker product can be inverted factor by factor. With a single output unit there is no second factor to exploit — the approximation degenerates to replacing each sample's curvature \(a_n\) with its mean — and the demo shows it is 40% off. With \(k\) classes, the \(k\times k\) factor carries the structure and the identity is exact when \(a_n\) does not vary with \(n\).

    predictive changes what is computed, not how: the covariance is the full one, and the question is whether to report \(\sigma(\hat\mu)\) or \(\mathbb{E}[\sigma(\mu)]\). The second is the posterior predictive, and it is the one that is calibrated.

    Derivation #

    1. The expansion #

    Write \(L(\theta) = \log p(\theta, y)\) for the unnormalised log-joint. Since \(\nabla L(\hat\theta) = 0\),

    \[L(\theta) = L(\hat\theta) - \tfrac12(\theta-\hat\theta)^\top P(\theta-\hat\theta) + O\big(\lVert\theta-\hat\theta\rVert^3\big), \qquad P = -\nabla^2 L(\hat\theta) \qquad (2)\]

    Exponentiating and normalising the Gaussian gives (1). For the evidence, keep the constant too and integrate the quadratic:

    \[\log p(y) = \log\int e^{L(\theta)}\,d\theta \approx L(\hat\theta) + \frac{d}{2}\log 2\pi - \frac12\log\lvert P\rvert \qquad (3)\]

    The three terms are the height at the mode, the volume of a unit Gaussian in \(d\) dimensions, and the volume of this Gaussian. The last is the Occam factor: a model with a broad posterior pays for the parameter space it did not use. It is why the evidence can compare models of different dimension, and why it can refuse a model that fits the data well with a prior it had to travel far from.

    Taylor-expanding \(L\) one order further and integrating gives the leading error:

    \[\mathbb{E}[\theta] \approx \hat\theta + \tfrac12 P^{-1}\,\nabla\!\left(\mathrm{tr}\big(P^{-1}\nabla^2 L(\theta)\big)\right)\Big|_{\hat\theta} \qquad (4)\]

    The correction is driven by the third derivatives of \(L\) — the skewness — and it is zero when the posterior is symmetric. The demo's posterior is not: its mode and mean differ by 0.057, which no Gaussian can represent.

    The rate is the classical one. Under regularity, the posterior contracts at \(n^{-1/2}\) and the error of the Laplace approximation to \(\log p(y)\) is \(O(n^{-1})\); on the demo's \(n=60\) logistic problem the evidence is off by 0.0136 nats.

    2. Finding the mode #

    Newton's method for \(\nabla L(\theta) = 0\) is

    \[\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k + P(\theta_k)^{-1}\nabla L(\theta_k) \qquad (5)\]

    with \(P(\theta) = -\nabla^2 L(\theta)\). This is the same matrix that becomes the covariance at convergence, which is why the two steps share code: _precision is called by both the optimiser and covariance().

    For a log-concave likelihood — logistic regression is — Newton is quadratically convergent, so the step count barely depends on \(d\). It is not monotone: a full Newton step can jump across the mode into the flat tail where the quadratic model is worthless, which is why the loop halves the step until the objective stops decreasing. On a well-behaved problem the line search never triggers; on a nearly separable one it is the difference between converging and diverging.

    For Bayesian logistic regression with a Gaussian prior \(w\sim N(0,\lambda^{-1}I)\):

    \[\nabla L(w) = X^\top(y - \sigma(Xw)) - \lambda w, \qquad P(w) = X^\top\mathrm{diag}\big(p(1-p)\big)X + \lambda I \qquad (6)\]

    \(P\) is positive definite because of the prior term and because \(p(1-p) > 0\). Without the prior, separable data drives \(\lVert w\rVert\to\infty\), \(p(1-p)\to0\), and \(P\) becomes singular — the posterior is improper, and no Gaussian exists.

    3. Why the diagonal version is overconfident #

    Partition a positive-definite \(P\) and use the Schur complement:

    \[P = \begin{pmatrix} a & b^\top \\ b & C\end{pmatrix}, \qquad (P^{-1})_{11} = \frac{1}{a - b^\top C^{-1}b} \ge \frac{1}{a} \qquad (7)\]

    with equality iff \(b = 0\). The diagonal approximation reports \(1/a\), the variance you would have if you knew the other parameters. It is a conditional variance passed off as a marginal one, so it is always too small — the estimate is overconfident, not merely imprecise. For two parameters the inflation factor is exact:

    \[\frac{(P^{-1})_{11}}{1/P_{11}} = \frac{1}{1-\rho^2} \qquad (8)\]

    The demo's \(\rho = -0.469\) gives a factor of 1.28, and the measured variances are 22% low.

    4. Kronecker structure #

    For a likelihood that factorises over samples, the precision of a linear layer's weight matrix \(W\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}\) is

    \[P = \sum_{n=1}^{N} A_n \otimes x_n x_n^\top, \qquad A_n = \mathrm{diag}(p_n) - p_n p_n^\top \qquad (9)\]

    — one Kronecker product per sample, and a sum of \(N\) of them. KFAC replaces the sum of products with the product of means:

    \[P \approx \bar A \otimes \Big(\sum_n x_n x_n^\top\Big), \qquad \bar A = \frac1N\sum_n A_n \qquad (10)\]

    The two agree exactly when \(A_n\) does not depend on \(n\); otherwise the approximation discards the correlation between a sample's input and its output curvature. The payoff is the inversion:

    \[\big(A\otimes B\big)^{-1} = A^{-1}\otimes B^{-1} \qquad (11)\]

    an identity the demo asserts, and the reason a \((kd)\times(kd)\) precision costs \(O(k^3 + d^3)\) instead of \(O(k^3d^3)\). In a deep network this is applied per layer, with \(A\) from the layer's inputs and \(B\) from the backpropagated gradients; here, with one output unit, \(A\) is a scalar and the approximation has nothing left to exploit — which is exactly what the demo's 40% error measures.

    5. The predictive, and the delta method #

    For a test point \(x\) the logit is \(\mu = x^\top\hat w\) with variance \(v = x^\top P^{-1}x\). The plug-in predictive is \(\sigma(\mu)\); the integrated one is

    \[\mathbb{E}\big[\sigma(\mu+\varepsilon)\big] = \int \sigma\big(\mu+\sqrt{v}\,z\big)\varphi(z)\,dz \qquad (12)\]

    which has no elementary closed form, so the code evaluates it by 20-point Gauss–Hermite quadrature — exact to machine precision for a smooth integrand with a Gaussian weight. The second-order expansion is the delta method's missing term:

    \[\mathbb{E}\big[\sigma(\mu+\varepsilon)\big] = \sigma(\mu) + \tfrac12\sigma''(\mu)\,v + O(v^2), \qquad \sigma''(\mu) = \sigma(\mu)\big(1-\sigma(\mu)\big)\big(1-2\sigma(\mu)\big) \qquad (13)\]

    \(\sigma''\) is negative above \(\mu=0\) and positive below it, so integrating always pulls the prediction toward \(\tfrac12\): uncertainty in the weights makes the model less confident, which is the correct behaviour and which the plug-in cannot produce. The demo checks (13) to within 5% at \(\mu = \pm2.02\), and finds the gap is 0.0086 in probability — small here because the posterior is tight, and growing like \(v\) as the data thin out.

    Code walkthrough #

    The likelihood is evaluated through logaddexp rather than \(\log\sigma(a)\) written directly, because the direct form overflows for \(a \ll 0\) and underflows for \(a \gg 0\):

            a = self.X @ w
        # log σ(a) = −log(1+e^{−a}) and log(1−σ(a)) = −log(1+e^{a}); the
        # second form is what logaddexp computes without overflow
        return float(np.sum(self.y * a - np.logaddexp(0.0, a))
                     - 0.5 * self.prior_prec * float(w @ w))

    The precision is (6) in one line, and the only thing to notice is that it is built as (X.T * weights) @ X — column scaling, not a diagonal matrix:

        def _precision(self, w: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        """`−∇² log p(w | y)`: positive definite by construction."""
        p = self._p(w)
        return (self.X.T * (p * (1.0 - p))) @ self.X + self.prior_prec * np.eye(w.size)

    The optimiser is (5) with a backtracking line search. The loop condition matters: without it, a Newton step on a nearly separated dataset leaves the region where the quadratic model holds, and the objective can decrease — which is the one thing a maximiser must not do:

                step = np.linalg.solve(self._precision(w), self._grad(w))
            # Newton on a logistic posterior can overshoot into a flat region
            # where the quadratic model is worthless; halving until the
            # objective does not decrease costs one extra evaluation per step
            t = 1.0
            while t > 1e-12:
                cand = w + t * step
                new = self._log_post(cand)
                if new >= obj - 1e-12:
                    break
                t *= 0.5

    The variant dispatch is where the approximation is chosen. The diagonal branch carries the Schur-complement argument from (7) as a comment because the direction of the error is the point: it is not "less accurate", it is systematically too narrow.

            P = self._precision(self.w_)
        if self.variant == "diag_hessian":
            # for a positive-definite P, (P⁻¹)_ii ≥ 1/P_ii with equality iff the
            # row is uncorrelated: dropping the off-diagonals treats the other
            # parameters as known, so it can only *narrow* a marginal
            return np.diag(1.0 / np.diag(P))

    The evidence is (3). slogdet rather than log(det(...)) because a determinant of a \(10^3\times10^3\) precision underflows to zero long before the log does:

            P = self._precision(self.w_)
        _, logdet = np.linalg.slogdet(P)
        return self.objective_ + 0.5 * self.w_.size * LOG2PI - 0.5 * logdet

    KFAC is (10), and the mean on the left factor is what makes it exact in the shared case: with \(A_n = a\) for all \(n\), the exact sum is \(a\otimes\sum x_nx_n^\top\) and the approximation is \(\bar A\otimes\sum x_nx_n^\top\) with \(\bar A = a\).

        f = np.asarray(factors, dtype=float)
    if f.ndim not in (1, 3):
        raise ValueError("factors must be (n,) or (n, k, k)")
    A = np.atleast_2d(f.mean(axis=0) if f.ndim == 3 else f.mean())
    k = A.shape[0]
    return np.kron(A, X.T @ X) + prior_prec * np.eye(k * X.shape[1])

    The exact side of the comparison in (9) is built sample by sample, which is only affordable because the demo's model is small — and is the reason KFAC exists:

        for x, p in zip(X, probs, strict=True):
        P += np.kron(np.diag(p) - np.outer(p, p), np.outer(x, x))

    The integrated predictive is (12). Gauss–Hermite is the right tool: it is exact for polynomials of degree \(2n-1\) times the Gaussian weight, so 20 nodes resolve the integrand to machine precision, and it needs no samples:

            var = np.einsum("nd,de,ne->n", X_new, self.covariance(), X_new)
        x, w = np.polynomial.hermite.hermgauss(20)
        # ∫ σ(μ + √v z) φ(z) dz = π^{-1/2} Σ w_i σ(μ + √(2v) x_i)
        out = np.sqrt(2.0 * np.maximum(var, 0.0))[:, None] * x + mu[:, None]
        return (w * sigmoid(out)).sum(axis=1) / math.sqrt(math.pi)

    Complexity #

    With \(n\) observations, \(d\) parameters, \(I\) Newton iterations, \(m\) test points, and KFAC factors of size \(k\times k\):

    Operation Time Memory
    gradient and precision \(O(nd^2)\) \(O(d^2)\)
    Newton step \(O(nd^2 + d^3)\) \(O(d^2)\)
    full_hessian covariance \(O(d^3)\) \(O(d^2)\)
    diag_hessian covariance \(O(d)\) \(O(d)\)
    kfac_laplace covariance \(O(k^3 + d^3)\) \(O(k^2 + d^2)\)
    log_evidence \(O(d^3)\) \(O(d^2)\)
    predict_proba \(O(md^2 + 20m)\) \(O(m)\)

    The whole fit is \(O\big(I(nd^2 + d^3)\big)\), and \(I\) is small: seven for the demo, and roughly independent of \(n\) because Newton is quadratically convergent. The cost is dominated by forming \(P\), which is a \(d\times d\) matrix built from \(n\) outer products — the same operation as one gradient evaluation of a linear model, so Laplace doubles the cost of fitting rather than multiplying it.

    The predictive overhead is \(O(md^2)\): one quadratic form per test point, which for a large \(m\) is worth batching (it is einsum in the code).

    The cubic terms are where this stops being free. At \(d = 10^4\) the precision alone is 800 MB, which is why no one runs full_hessian on a network, and why kfac_laplace and diag_hessian exist at all.

    Failure modes #

    A non-Gaussian posterior. Laplace reports a Gaussian no matter what. When the posterior is skewed or multimodal — hierarchical models with a funnel, mixtures, anything with a boundary — the mode can sit far from the mean, and the Gaussian's mass is in the wrong place. The demo's own posterior is mildly skewed: mode and mean differ by 0.057, and no reparameterisation of the output fixes that. Diagnostic: compare the mode with a short MCMC run's mean (see mcmc.py); a large gap or a heavy tail means the Gaussian is the wrong shape, not just the wrong width.

    Separable data. If a hyperplane separates the classes, the likelihood has no maximum at any finite \(w\) and the posterior is improper in the flat direction. Newton walks off and the precision becomes numerically singular. The prior is what prevents this; with prior_prec too small the code will produce a huge covariance and an evidence that looks excellent. Diagnostic: the largest eigenvalue of \(P\) relative to the prior term, and the norm of \(\hat w\).

    Trusting the diagonal. It is overconfident by exactly \(1-\rho^2\) (8), so it under-covers. A 95% interval built from diag_hessian is not a 95% interval. Diagnostic: compare with full_hessian on a subsample; if the ratio of variances is far from one, the diagonal is not an approximation, it is a different model.

    KFAC where there is no factor structure. For a single output unit the approximation collapses to a mean curvature, and the demo measures 40% error in the precision and 48% in the marginal variances. It is not a milder version of the full Hessian; it is a different matrix. Diagnostic: the ratio of the KFAC and full precisions' traces, which should be near one if the factorisation is holding.

    A plug-in predictive. Reporting \(\sigma(x^\top\hat w)\) instead of (12) omits a term of order \(v\), so it is overconfident by an amount that grows exactly when you need it — at extrapolation points and with little data. Diagnostic: the difference between integrate=True and integrate=False; if it is larger than the precision you report, the plug-in number is not the one to report.

    The evidence as a model-comparison tool. (3) is an integral over the whole posterior, approximated by its behaviour at one point. It is excellent when the posterior is nearly Gaussian and misleading when it is not — singular models (mixtures with unused components, neural nets with permutation symmetry) have evidence that Laplace cannot see. Diagnostic: compare with a nested or bridge-sampling estimate before trusting a Bayes factor computed this way.

    Inverting instead of solving. np.linalg.inv on an ill-conditioned \(P\) loses half the digits, and Laplace is precisely where ill-conditioning shows up. The code uses solve for the Newton step and slogdet for the evidence; only the covariance needs an explicit inverse, and there cholesky-based solves would be more stable at large \(d\).

    Applications #

    Generalised linear models. For a GLM the Laplace approximation is the standard inference: \(P\) is the IRLS weight matrix at convergence, so the covariance is the one every GLM summary prints. A logistic regression fit with statsmodels is this file with the prior removed.

    Gaussian process classification. The posterior over the latent function is not Gaussian, and Laplace's approximation of it is the classical method — gp_classification.py implements it, and the derivation there is (2)–(3) with \(P = I + K^{1/2}W K^{1/2}\).

    Neural-network uncertainty. Last-layer Laplace — fit the network, then apply this file to the final layer — is one of the few cheap calibrated uncertainty methods for deep nets; kfac_laplace is the version that scales to a full network, applied layer by layer. variational_inference.py is the alternative when you want a global fit rather than a local one.

    Model comparison and Bayes factors. (3) is the marginal likelihood, so Laplace gives a free Bayes factor between two models. This is where BIC comes from: dropping the \(O(1)\) terms of (3) under a unit prior gives \(-2\log p(y) \approx -2L(\hat\theta) + d\log n\).

    Sequential filtering. The extended Kalman filter is a Laplace approximation applied recursively: linearise at the current mean, propagate a Gaussian, repeat. kalman.py is the exact linear-Gaussian case this degrades from.

    Where it is a trap. Hierarchical models (the funnel in hierarchical_bayes.py is exactly the geometry where a Gaussian at the mode is worst), mixtures and anything with label switching, models with a boundary-constrained parameter, and any high-dimensional posterior where the mode is not where the mass is.
