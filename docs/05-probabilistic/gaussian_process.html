    05 · Probabilistic models/tier 1/9 variants/source

    Gaussian process regression

    Intuition #

    A Gaussian process is a distribution over functions: pick any finite set of inputs and the function's values there are jointly Gaussian. The covariance of that Gaussian is given by a kernel, which is where all modelling choices live.

    \[f \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, k), \qquad y = f(x) + \varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \sim N(0, \sigma_n^2) \qquad (1)\]

    Because the prior is Gaussian and the likelihood is Gaussian, conditioning is linear algebra. No sampling loop, no gradient descent, no evidence lower bound: the posterior mean is K* K⁻¹ y and the posterior variance is K** − K* K⁻¹ K*ᵀ. Everything the model knows is a matrix inverse.

    The consequence that matters is that the model returns a distribution, not a point. predict gives a mean and a standard deviation, the standard deviation shrinks toward zero as you approach observed data, and it grows back to the prior when you leave the data behind. That is what makes GPs the default surrogate in Bayesian optimisation and active learning, and it is also why nobody uses them on a million rows: the inverse costs O(n³).

    The kernel is the model. rbf says the function is infinitely differentiable; matern says it is differentiable a specific number of times; periodic says it repeats; linear says it is a line, and a GP with that kernel is exactly Bayesian linear regression. Composition (sum_kernel, product_kernel) is how a "trend plus seasonal plus noise" model gets built without a new algorithm.

    Variants #

    Variant Kernel Say it when
    rbf σ²exp(−r²/2ℓ²) smooth, no reason to doubt infinite differentiability
    matern (1+√3r)exp(−√3r) per ν physical data; ν = 1/2 gives rough, OU-like paths
    rational_quadratic σ²(1+r²/2αℓ²)^{−α} a mixture of length scales (heavy-tailed spectrum)
    periodic σ²exp(−2sin²(πΔ/p)/ℓ²) a hard period p: orbits, seasons, duty cycles
    linear σ²xᵀx' you want to prove it is ridge regression
    sum_kernel k₁ + k₂ trend plus local wiggle, additive structure
    product_kernel k₁ · k₂ periodicity whose amplitude decays away from the trend
    sparse_gp K ≈ K_nm K_mm⁻¹ K_mn n too large for O(n³); m inducing points
    warped y = log(z − a) positive, skewed targets; a plain GP over-smooths the peaks

    The first seven change the prior; the last two change the computation. sparse_gp trades a little accuracy for a much smaller matrix, and warped changes the observation model rather than the function class.

    Derivation #

    Conditioning a joint Gaussian #

    Stack the observed outputs and the values at test inputs:

    \[\begin{bmatrix} y \\ f_* \end{bmatrix} \sim N\!\left(0, \begin{bmatrix} K + \sigma_n^2 I & K_* \\ K_*^\top & K_{**}\end{bmatrix}\right), \qquad (2)\]

    with K = k(X,X), K_* = k(X,X_*), K_{**} = k(X_*,X_*). The conditional of a partitioned Gaussian is the Schur complement:

    \[\mu_* = K_*^\top (K+\sigma_n^2I)^{-1} y, \qquad \Sigma_* = K_{**} - K_*^\top (K+\sigma_n^2I)^{-1} K_*. \qquad (3)\]

    That is the whole algorithm, and every implementation detail below exists to compute it without ever forming an inverse.

    Cholesky, not inverse #

    Let K + σ²I = LLᵀ. Then α = L⁻ᵀL⁻¹y and v = L⁻¹K_*, so

    \[\mu_* = K_*^\top\alpha, \qquad \Sigma_* = K_{**} - v^\top v. \qquad (4)\]

    Two triangular solves per prediction, no np.linalg.inv anywhere. The variance is a difference of two positive numbers, which is why it is clipped at zero in the code — with a badly conditioned K the subtraction can go slightly negative, and a negative variance is a silent nan in the standard deviation.

    The evidence: choosing a kernel without a validation set #

    The marginal likelihood of the data under the prior is available in closed form because everything is Gaussian:

    \[\log p(y \mid X) = -\tfrac12 y^\top(K+\sigma_n^2I)^{-1}y - \tfrac12\log\lvert K+\sigma_n^2I\rvert - \tfrac{n}{2}\log 2\pi. \qquad (5)\]

    The first term rewards fitting the data; the second is an Occam factor — a kernel that claims the data is surprising has a small |K|, and the log determinant punishes it. This is the principled way to compare rbf against matern on the same data, and it is why the demo reports both RMSE and evidence: they do not have to agree, and the disagreement is informative.

    The kernels #

    RBF. Stationary, isotropic, infinitely mean-square differentiable:

    \[k(x,x') = \sigma_f^2\exp\!\left(-\frac{\lVert x-x'\rVert^2}{2\ell^2}\right). \qquad (6)\]

    Matérn. The spectral density is a Student-t, so the sample paths are only ν-times differentiable in mean square. Only half-integer ν avoid a Bessel function, and those are the three that matter:

    \[k_{1/2} = e^{-r}, \qquad k_{3/2} = (1+\sqrt3 r)e^{-\sqrt3 r}, \qquad k_{5/2} = (1+\sqrt5 r+\tfrac53 r^2)e^{-\sqrt5 r}, \qquad (7)\]

    with r = ‖x−x'‖/ℓ. ν = 1/2 is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process: continuous everywhere, differentiable nowhere. ν → ∞ recovers the RBF. If a fitted RBF looks too smooth for the data, the fix is a Matérn, not a smaller length scale.

    Rational quadratic. An infinite scale mixture of RBFs, ℓ distributed inverse-gamma:

    \[k = \sigma_f^2\left(1+\frac{r^2}{2\alpha\ell^2}\right)^{-\alpha}, \qquad \alpha \to \infty \text{ gives RBF}. \qquad (8)\]

    Periodic. Correlation depends on sin of the distance, so it is exactly periodic in p and smooth in between:

    \[k = \sigma_f^2\exp\!\left(-\frac{2\sin^2(\pi\lvert x-x'\rvert/p)}{\ell^2}\right). \qquad (9)\]

    Linear. k = σ_f² xᵀx'. Substituting into (4) and using Woodbury gives

    \[\mu_* = x_*^\top\left(X^\top X + \frac{\sigma_n^2}{\sigma_f^2}I\right)^{-1}X^\top y, \qquad (10)\]

    which is ridge regression with λ = σ_n²/\sigma_f² and no intercept. A GP with a linear kernel is a Bayesian linear model — the prior on w is N(0, I/σ_f²). The demo asserts this to 1e-8, which is a much stronger test of the GP solver than any regression score.

    Composition. Covariances of independent functions add when the functions add and multiply when the functions multiply, so

    \[k_{1+2} = k_1 + k_2, \qquad k_{1\cdot2} = k_1\,k_2, \qquad (11)\]

    and the result is a valid kernel because the pointwise operations preserve positive semi-definiteness (Schur product theorem for the product). That is the entire "automatic statistician" idea: a grammar of kernels, and one search over it.

    Sparse: replace K_nn by a low-rank approximation #

    With m inducing points Z, the subset-of-regressors approximation is

    \[K_{nn} \approx Q = K_{nm}K_{mm}^{-1}K_{mn}, \qquad (12)\]

    which is the exact covariance if the function is determined by its values at Z. Substituting into (3) would form an n×n matrix; the Woodbury identity keeps everything at m×m. Writing A = L_m^{-1}K_{mn} (so Q = A^\top A) and B = \sigma_n^2 I_m + AA^\top:

    \[(Q+\sigma_n^2I)^{-1} = \sigma_n^{-2}\left(I - A^\top B^{-1}A\right), \qquad \lvert Q+\sigma_n^2I\rvert = \sigma_n^{2(n-m)}\lvert B\rvert, \qquad (13)\]

    so both the mean and the evidence cost O(nm²) and no n×n matrix is allocated. The price is a variance that is too small between inducing points, because the approximation is exact only on the manifold spanned by k(·, Z).

    Warped: a Gaussian process for a non-Gaussian target #

    If y is positive and skewed, a GP on y wastes its prior on impossible values and over-smooths the peaks. Warped GPs put the GP on a transformed scale,

    \[g(y) = \log(y - a), \qquad p(y) = p(g)\,\lvert g'(y)\rvert, \qquad \log p(y) = \log p(g) - \log(y-a), \qquad (14)\]

    and choose a by maximising that Jacobian-corrected likelihood. Forgetting the Jacobian term is the classic bug: it biases a toward the boundary and makes the warp look better than it is.

    Prediction has to come back through a non-linear map, and the mean of a log-normal is not the exponential of the mean:

    \[\mathbb{E}[y] = a + e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}, \qquad \mathrm{Var}[y] = e^{2\mu+\sigma^2}\left(e^{\sigma^2}-1\right). \qquad (15)\]

    That σ²/2 is not a detail — it is the difference between a calibrated prediction and one that is biased low by exactly the uncertainty.

    Code walkthrough #

    sqdist is the only geometry primitive in the file, and it uses the expansion ‖a−b‖² = ‖a‖² + ‖b‖² − 2a·b so nothing of size n×m×d is ever allocated. The clip matters: floating point can return -1e-17 for a point against itself, and sqrt of that is nan:

    def sqdist(A: np.ndarray, B: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """`‖a − b‖²` for every pair, without forming the `n×m×d` tensor."""
    d = (A * A).sum(1)[:, None] + (B * B).sum(1)[None, :] - 2.0 * A @ B.T
    return np.maximum(d, 0.0)

    Every kernel is a __call__(A, B) plus a diag, and the diag override is the reason prediction is O(n) per point rather than O(n²): the diagonal of an RBF Gram matrix is the constant σ_f², and computing it as np.diag(self(A, A)) would build the whole matrix to read n entries:

        def __call__(self, A, B):
        r2 = sqdist(A, B) / self.length_scale ** 2
        return self.sigma_f ** 2 * np.exp(-0.5 * r2)

    def diag(self, A):
        return np.full(A.shape[0], self.sigma_f ** 2)

    The Matérn branch is a lookup, but the ν guard is load-bearing: ν = 1 has no closed form without a Bessel function and silently returns the wrong kernel if it is allowed through:

            r = np.sqrt(sqdist(A, B)) / self.length_scale
        if self.nu == 0.5:
            k = np.exp(-r)
        elif self.nu == 1.5:
            s = np.sqrt(3.0) * r
            k = (1.0 + s) * np.exp(-s)
        else:
            s = np.sqrt(5.0) * r
            k = (1.0 + s + s * s / 3.0) * np.exp(-s)

    _solve is the numerical core: one Cholesky, two triangular solves, and the evidence accumulated at the same time. np.log(np.diag(L)).sum() is the log-determinant, and it is the only way to get it without underflow:

        def _solve(self, X: np.ndarray, y: np.ndarray):
        """Cholesky of `K + σ²I`, the linear solve, and the log-likelihood."""
        s2 = self.sigma_n ** 2
        K = self.kernel_(X, X) + (s2 + self.jitter) * np.eye(X.shape[0])
        L = np.linalg.cholesky(K)
        alpha = np.linalg.solve(L.T, np.linalg.solve(L, y))
        ll = (-0.5 * float(y @ alpha) - np.log(np.diag(L)).sum()
              - 0.5 * y.size * np.log(2.0 * np.pi))
        return L, alpha, float(ll)

    Prediction is (4) twice — once for the mean, once for the variance. Solving with L rather than forming K⁻¹ is O(n²) per test point instead of O(n³):

            Ks = self.kernel_(self.X_, X)
        mean = Ks.T @ self._alpha
        v = np.linalg.solve(self._L, Ks)
        var = self.kernel_.diag(X) - (v ** 2).sum(axis=0)
        return mean, np.maximum(var, 0.0)

    The sparse fit is (13) in six lines. A is L_m⁻¹K_mn, B is the m×m matrix the Woodbury identity asks for, and w is (Q+σ²I)⁻¹y computed without ever knowing Q:

            Kmm = self.kernel_(Z, Z) + self.jitter * np.eye(m)
        Lm = np.linalg.cholesky(Kmm)
        # A = Lm⁻¹ K_mn, so that Q = AᵀA = K_nm K_mm⁻¹ K_mn
        A = np.linalg.solve(Lm, self.kernel_(Z, X))              # (m, n)
        B = s2 * np.eye(m) + A @ A.T                             # (m, m)
        c = np.linalg.solve(B, A @ y)                            # (m,)
        w = (y - A.T @ c) / s2                                   # (Q+σ²I)⁻¹ y
        self.inducing_mean_ = np.linalg.solve(Lm.T, A @ w)       # K_mm⁻¹K_mn w

    The warping objective is the marginal likelihood of the transformed data minus Σ log z, the Jacobian of (14). One line of golden-section search over θ recovers the offset, and there is no gradient because there is only one parameter:

            def objective(theta: float) -> float:
            a = ymin - np.exp(theta)
            z = y - a
            if (z <= 0.0).any():
                return -np.inf
            g = np.log(z)
            _, _, ll = self._solve(X, g)
            return ll - float(np.log(z).sum())      # the Jacobian of the warping

    Back-transforming is where a warped GP quietly goes wrong. Both the mean and the variance need the σ²/2 term, and the observation noise enters scaled by (dg/dz)² = e^{2g}:

                scale = np.exp(2.0 * mean)
            ymean = self.warp_a_ + np.exp(mean + 0.5 * var)
            yvar = scale * (np.expm1(var) + self.sigma_n ** 2)
            return (ymean, np.sqrt(yvar)) if return_std else ymean

    Sampling needs the full posterior covariance, not just its diagonal. Drawing N(mean_i, var_i) independently at each point would match the marginals and produce white noise instead of smooth functions, so _posterior_cov forms the Schur complement and sample takes its square root:

        def _posterior_cov(self, X: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        """`K** − K*ᵀ K⁻¹ K*`: the covariance `predict` only reports the diagonal of."""
        Kss = self.kernel_(X, X)
        if self.variant == "sparse_gp":
            Ksm = self.kernel_(X, self.inducing_)                  # (n*, m)
            C = Kss - Ksm @ np.linalg.solve(
                self._Lm.T, np.linalg.solve(self._Lm, Ksm.T))
        else:
            v = np.linalg.solve(self._L, self.kernel_(self.X_, X))
            C = Kss - v.T @ v
        return 0.5 * (C + C.T)

    Complexity #

    n training points, n_* test points, d dimensions, m inducing points.

    Stage Exact GP Sparse GP
    fit O(n³) (Cholesky) O(nm² + m³)
    memory O(n²) O(nm)
    predict mean O(nn_*) O(mn_*)
    predict variance O(n²n_*) O(m²n_*)
    sample O(n³ + n²n_*) O(n_*³)

    The n³ is the wall. A laptop handles n in the low thousands; beyond that the matrix alone does not fit and an approximation is not optional. Kernel composition does not change the asymptotics — a sum of twenty kernels is still one n×n Cholesky — it only changes the constant by the number of terms.

    Failure modes #

    The n³ wall. At n = 10⁴ the K matrix is 800 MB and a Cholesky is 10¹² flops; at n = 10⁵ it is impossible. The diagnostic is simply the size of the data. The fix is inducing points (sparse_gp), a variational bound, or a different model — not a bigger machine.

    The length scale is the model, and it is not fitted here. Hyperparameters are inputs in this file. A length scale much smaller than the typical spacing makes the GP interpolate noise and grow spurious wiggles between points; much larger than the data range makes it a constant. The diagnostic is the evidence from (5) compared across a grid of ℓ, and the fix is an optimiser over it (which is 01-optimization's problem, not this atom's).

    Duplicate points plus a tiny noise floor. Two identical rows make K exactly singular, and cholesky raises LinAlgError rather than producing a bad answer. jitter in the constructor is the fix; the diagnostic is the exception itself, or np.linalg.eigvalsh(K).min() ≤ 0.

    Confident in the wrong direction. Away from the data the variance returns to k(x,x), which looks reassuring, but "away" is measured in the kernel's metric. In high dimensions every test point is far from every training point under ‖·‖, so the GP reports near-prior uncertainty everywhere and still returns a mean that extrapolates linearly. The signal is a predictive band that never shrinks regardless of how much data you add.

    A wrong warp is worse than no warp. warped assumes a specific one-parameter family. If the target is skewed in a way log(y−a) cannot represent, the Jacobian term can make the likelihood prefer a a that flattens the data, and the fit degrades below the plain GP. Compare the two on held-out data — the demo does — rather than trusting the training evidence alone.

    Applications #

    Bayesian optimisation is the flagship: the GP is cheap to update and its variance is exactly the exploration signal an acquisition function needs. Surrogate modelling and computer-experiment emulation are the same use case with a different name — fit a GP to 200 expensive simulations, optimise the cheap surrogate. Kriging in geostatistics is a GP with a Matérn kernel under a different vocabulary.

    Time series with honest error bars, active learning (query where the variance is largest), and calibration of simulation models all lean on the same two formulas. Kernel composition is what makes it practical: sum_kernel of a linear trend and a periodic component is a seasonal regression with a principled uncertainty band.

    Where it is a trap: high-dimensional inputs (d > 20 needs a learned metric or a deep kernel), more than a few thousand points, non-stationary processes (a GP assumes the same smoothness everywhere), and any setting where only the point prediction matters — a gradient-boosted tree will be faster and often more accurate.
