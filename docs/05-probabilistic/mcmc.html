    05 · Probabilistic models/tier 2/7 variants/source

    MCMC

    Intuition #

    A Markov chain that forgets where it started and settles into the distribution you cannot integrate. You never evaluate the posterior — you only compare it at two points, and a ratio of densities is all a Metropolis rule needs.

    \[P(x \to \mathrm{d}x') = q(x \to x')\,a(x, x')\,\mathrm{d}x' \qquad (1)\]

    If a is chosen so that the chain is reversible with respect to \(\pi\),

    \[\pi(x)\,q(x \to x')\,a(x, x') \;=\; \pi(x')\,q(x' \to x)\,a(x', x) \qquad (2)\]

    then \(\pi\) is stationary, and under mild conditions the chain converges to it from any starting point. That single equation generates every sampler in the file: the variants differ only in which proposal q they can afford and how much structure they exploit to make a large.

    The economics are the whole subject. A random-walk proposal is free and accepts often but moves \(O(\sqrt{d})\) per step, so it needs \(O(d)\) steps to decorrelate. A gradient tells you which way is uphill, and following a Hamiltonian trajectory moves \(O(1)\) per step regardless of d — the same posterior for orders of magnitude fewer density evaluations, at the price of a gradient. Everything else is engineering that trade.

    Variants #

    Variant Proposal q Cost per draw Use when
    metropolis_hastings \(N(x, s^2 I)\) 1 density nothing is differentiable, d is small, you need a baseline
    gibbs the exact full conditional 1 conditional per coordinate conditionals are conjugate or one-dimensional
    hmc leapfrog trajectory of length \(L\varepsilon\) \(L\) gradients + \(L\) densities differentiable, d from 10 to 10⁶
    nuts HMC, trajectory length chosen by u-turns 2^depth gradients you do not know the trajectory length — the default choice
    slice_sampling uniform under the density along a line \(O(1)\) densities no gradients, awkward shapes, heavy tails
    adaptive_metropolis \(N(x, s_t^2 I)\) with \(s_t\) tuned 1 density you want Metropolis without a tuning sweep
    diagnostics — (uses the random walk) — always, before you believe any of the above

    diagnostics is not a kernel. It is the three numbers — R-hat, effective sample size, acceptance — that tell you whether the other six produced anything. It lives here because a sampler shipped without them is a random number generator with a story attached.

    gibbs is Metropolis with acceptance probability exactly one applied to a proposal you can only make when the conditional is available. nuts is hmc with the hardest hyper-parameter — trajectory length — removed. slice_sampling is the only kernel here that needs no tuning at all and the only one that is happy with a target whose mean does not exist.

    Derivation #

    Metropolis–Hastings #

    Propose \(x' \sim q(\cdot \mid x)\), accept with

    \[a(x, x') \;=\; \min\left(1, \frac{\pi(x')\,q(x \mid x')}{\pi(x)\,q(x' \mid x)}\right) \;=\; \min\left(1, e^{\ell(x') - \ell(x)}\right) \qquad (3)\]

    for a symmetric proposal. The second equality is why no normalising constant is ever needed: only the difference in log density appears, and it is compared with \(\log U\). Substituting (3) into (2) gives detailed balance in one line — the numerator of one direction is the denominator of the other.

    Gibbs #

    If \(q\) is the full conditional \(p(x_j \mid x_{-j})\), then \(\ell(x') - \ell(x) = \log p(x_j' \mid x_{-j}) - \log p(x_j \mid x_{-j})\), and the Hastings ratio is exactly the inverse of the proposal ratio. The acceptance probability is 1. Gibbs is not an approximation of Metropolis; it is the case where the proposal is already correct.

    The corollary is the failure: if \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are correlated at \(\rho = 0.99\), the conditional for \(x_1\) is a very narrow distribution around \(\rho x_2\), so each sweep moves a distance proportional to \(1 - \rho^2\). Gibbs is a random walk that has quietly chosen a terrible step size.

    Hamiltonian Monte Carlo #

    Add a momentum \(p \sim N(0, M)\) and integrate Hamilton's equations for the augmented density \(\pi(x)N(p; 0, M)\):

    \[H(x, p) \;=\; -\ell(x) + \tfrac{1}{2} p^\top M^{-1} p, \qquad \dot{x} = M^{-1}p, \quad \dot{p} = \nabla \ell(x) \qquad (4)\]

    The leapfrog integrator alternates half-kicks and drifts, is symplectic and reversible, and therefore preserves the stationary distribution exactly up to the Metropolis correction

    \[a \;=\; \min\left(1, e^{H_0 - H_1}\right) \qquad (5)\]

    In the demo, a target with standard deviations \((1, 10)\) and the matching metric \(M = \mathrm{diag}(1, 10^{-2})\) keeps the mean energy error at \(0.05\) and delivers 21× the random walk's effective samples per evaluation on the slow coordinate. With the identity metric the same code would still be correct, and would take a step size small enough for the narrow coordinate that the wide one barely moves — the metric, not the integrator, decides whether HMC works.

    NUTS #

    Trajectory length is the remaining knob, and it is target-dependent. NUTS grows the trajectory by doubling, in a random direction, and stops when either end turns back towards the other:

    \[(x^{+} - x^{-}) \cdot p^{-} < 0 \quad \text{or} \quad (x^{+} - x^{-}) \cdot p^{+} < 0 \qquad (6)\]

    The proposal is drawn from the new subtree with probability proportional to its number of states, which keeps the doubling unbiased. The step size is tuned by dual averaging during warm-up,

    \[\log \varepsilon_{t+1} = \mu - \frac{\sqrt{t}}{\gamma}\,\bar h_t, \qquad \bar h_t = \left(1 - \frac{1}{t+t_0}\right)\bar h_{t-1} + \frac{1}{t+t_0}\left(\delta - \bar\alpha_t\right) \qquad (7)\]

    and initialised by doubling from \(\varepsilon = 1\) until a single leapfrog accepts about half the time. The target \(\delta\) is not 0.234: random-walk optimal scaling says nothing about Hamiltonian dynamics, which want \(\delta \approx 0.8\) because a rejected trajectory has already paid for every leapfrog step.

    Slice sampling #

    Sample uniformly from the region under the density. Draw a level \(y = \ell(x_0) - e\) with \(e \sim \mathrm{Exp}(1)\), then a point uniformly from \(\{x : \ell(x) > y\}\) along a line. The returned point is uniform on the slice, so the kernel is reversible with acceptance 1, and the only tuning is a window width that adapts to the local scale as it steps out. It is the only sampler here that handles a Cauchy target — whose mean does not exist — without any special casing.

    Adaptation and diagnostics #

    Robbins–Monro tunes the proposal during burn-in:

    \[\log s_{t+1} = \log s_t + \gamma_t\,(\alpha_t - \alpha^{*}), \qquad \gamma_t \propto t^{-0.6} \qquad (8)\]

    A decaying gain is not a detail. Adapting forever makes the kernel non-homogeneous and destroys the stationary distribution; decaying to zero makes the adaptation vanish asymptotically, so the chain converges. Freeze the scale after warm-up and the guarantee holds.

    Two chains that disagree have not mixed. Compare between- and within-chain variance:

    \[\hat R = \sqrt{\frac{\frac{n-1}{n}W + \frac{B}{n}}{W}} \qquad (9)\]

    and count how many independent draws the autocorrelation has cost you:

    \[\mathrm{ESS} = \frac{mn}{1 + 2\sum_{t \geq 1}\rho_t} \qquad (10)\]

    with Geyer's initial positive pair sum truncating the series where it stops being informative. Identical chains give \(\hat R = \sqrt{1 - 1/n} = 0.99975\), not 1 — the \(1/n\) factor is visible in the demo, which is a good sign the formula was implemented rather than approximated.

    Code walkthrough #

    The whole atom is one class plus three functions. VARIANTS is the public API; sample dispatches on it.

    VARIANTS = frozenset(
    ["metropolis_hastings", "gibbs", "hmc", "nuts", "slice_sampling",
     "adaptive_metropolis", "diagnostics"]
)

    sample returns (n, d) and records the price of every draw in n_eval_ and n_grad_. Those counters are the reason the HMC comparison in the demo is honest: kernels are compared per evaluation, not per iteration.

            if self.variant == "gibbs":
            chain = self._gibbs(n_samples, x0, rng)
        elif self.variant in ("hmc", "nuts"):
            chain = self._hamiltonian(n_samples, x0, rng)
        elif self.variant == "slice_sampling":
            chain = self._slice(n_samples, x0, rng)
        else:
            chain = self._random_walk(n_samples, x0, rng)

    The Metropolis core is four lines, and the one that would be wrong in a naive implementation is the comparison: it is a log-space test against log U, not U < ratio, because the ratio underflows for any meaningful move in high dimensions.

                prop = x + scale * rng.standard_normal(d)
            lpp = self._lp(prop)
            ok = np.log(rng.random()) < lpp - lp
            if ok:
                x, lp = prop, lpp
                accepted += 1

    Gibbs is the same loop with the proposal replaced by a draw from the conditional, which is why it has no accept step at all.

            for i in range(self.n_warmup + n):
            for j, draw in enumerate(samplers):
                x[j] = draw(x, rng)

    When a closed-form conditional is not available, the same variant slices it. This is the useful trick: Gibbs-with-slice is a valid Gibbs sampler whose conditionals are each one-dimensional, so nothing is rejected and no algebra is required.

            y = float(logp(x0)) - float(rng.exponential())
        left = x0 - self.width * float(rng.random())
        right = left + self.width

    The slice's shrinkage loop is the invariant: every rejected draw moves an endpoint inward, so the interval always contracts around points above the level and cannot wander into the tail.

            for _ in range(1000):
            x1 = left + (right - left) * float(rng.random())
            if float(logp(x1)) > y:
                return x1

    HMC's leapfrog is where the mass matrix must be handled in momentum form. Updating a velocity as if it were a momentum is off by a factor of M, which shows up as energy that is not conserved on an anisotropic target — exactly the bug the demo's |ΔH| = 0.05 assertion catches.

            p = p + 0.5 * eps * g
        x = x + eps * p / m
        lp = self._lp(x)
        g = self._grad(x)
        p = p + 0.5 * eps * g

    The NUTS leaf is the slice test and the Metropolis ratio in Hamiltonian form. Getting the sign of logu wrong makes every tree empty and the chain frozen at its starting point — a bug that produces a plausible-looking trace and zero mixing.

                H1 = -lp1 + self._kinetic(p1)
            n = int(logu <= -H1)
            s = int(logu < -H1 + 1000.0)
            alpha = min(1.0, float(np.exp(H0 - H1)))

    The recursion reuses the same _tree for both directions and joins two subtrees, stopping when the endpoints turn back.

            delta = xp - xm
        no_uturn = int(float(delta @ pm) >= 0.0 and float(delta @ pp) >= 0.0)

    Step-size initialisation is a doubling search, not a constant. Starting dual averaging from an arbitrary step is the difference between converging in 500 warm-up draws and wandering for 5,000.

                if trial == 0:
                direction = 1 if better else -1
            elif better != (direction == 1):
                break
            eps *= 2.0**direction

    The acceptance target is variant-aware, and that is not cosmetic: with the random-walk value 0.234, NUTS settles on a step where most trajectories are rejected and reports a mean tree depth of 1.

            if self.variant in ("hmc", "nuts"):
            return 0.8
        return 0.44 if d == 1 else 0.234

    The ESS estimator sums autocorrelation in pairs and stops at the first non-positive pair. Truncating at a fixed lag either throws away real correlation or adds noise; the pair rule is the standard fix.

        total = 0.0
    lag = 0
    while lag + 1 < rho.size:
        pair = rho[lag] + rho[lag + 1]
        if pair <= 0.0:
            break
        total += pair
        lag += 2

    R-hat is between-chain variance added to within-chain variance:

        W = float(np.mean(chains.var(axis=1, ddof=1)))
    B = float(n * np.var(chains.mean(axis=1), ddof=1))
    var_hat = (n - 1) / n * W + B / n
    return float(np.sqrt(var_hat / W))

    Complexity #

    Per draw, with d the dimension and L the trajectory length:

    Variant Time Memory Density evaluations per draw
    metropolis_hastings O(d) O(n d) 1
    gibbs O(d) per sweep with closed forms, O(d²) to evaluate them O(n d) 0 (conditionals)
    hmc O(L d) O(n d) 2L (plus L gradients)
    nuts O(2^depth d) O(n d) ≤ 2^{depth+1}
    slice_sampling O(d) expected, O(1) per coordinate O(n d) ~3–5 per coordinate
    adaptive_metropolis O(d) O(n d + warmup) 1

    The stored chain dominates memory: 100,000 draws of 1,000 parameters is 800 MB in float64. Nothing here streams; subsample or checkpoint if that matters.

    What actually dominates wall-clock is not the per-draw cost but the number of draws needed, and that is set by the effective sample size. The demo's anisotropic target shows the whole point: the random walk spends 10,000 evaluations to get 39 effective draws of the slow coordinate; HMC spends 160,000 to get 10,000, which is 21× cheaper per effective draw.

    Failure modes #

    The trace looks stationary and the diagnostics lie. R-hat compares chains to each other, so four chains started in the same mode agree perfectly about a multimodal target. The demo constructs the failure deliberately: two chains frozen at ±6 give R-hat = 849, but if both had started at −6 the number would have been 1.000 and the posterior mean wrong by 6. Fix: overdispersed starting points, and plot the trace.

    Acceptance is fine and mixing is not. In the anisotropic demo the random walk accepts 40% of its proposals while the slow coordinate gets 39 effective samples out of 10,000. Acceptance measures the proposal relative to the local scale, not progress through the posterior. Fix: look at ESS per coordinate, not at the acceptance rate.

    Energy explodes instead of decorrelating. A leapfrog step above the stability limit \(2/\omega\) turns a trajectory into a diverging orbit; the symptom is a sudden collapse of acceptance when the step size grows, and a |ΔH| in the tens. The demo's first implementation had exactly this bug — a velocity updated as if it were a momentum, off by the mass matrix — and the assertion energy_error_ < 0.2 is what caught it. Fix: ΔH percentiles per draw; divergences concentrate in the funnel of a hierarchical model.

    Adapting forever. Any tuning that does not decay keeps the kernel non-homogeneous and the chain does not converge to π. Fix: freeze after warm-up, as adaptive_metropolis and nuts do here, and check that the adaptation has settled before the first stored draw.

    Correlated conditionals. Gibbs on a Gaussian with ρ = 0.99 needs O((1-ρ²)^{-1}) sweeps, which is 5,000 per effective draw. Nothing about the code is wrong. Fix: reparameterise, or use HMC on the joint.

    No burn-in is not "none needed". Every kernel here is started from x0, and the first draws are not from π. The demo discards n_warmup; the diagnostics for a chain with n_warmup = 0 and a bad start will show it as a shift, not as an R-hat.

    Applications #

    • Hierarchical and multilevel models. The reason MCMC is in every Bayesian's toolkit: a model with partial pooling has no closed-form posterior, and the funnel geometry is exactly what NUTS was designed to survive.
    • Latent variable and mixture models. Label switching, unknown \(K\), and posteriors over assignments are all sampling problems (see dirichlet_process.py).
    • Gaussian process hyperparameters. The marginal likelihood is available, but its gradient with respect to a handful of kernel parameters is often not; slice sampling or NUTS on the log-hyperparameters is the standard fallback (see gaussian_process.py).
    • Posterior predictive checks. Drawing parameters and then data is the cheapest model criticism that exists, and it requires only a sampler.
    • Calibration of expensive simulators. When the likelihood is a simulation, approximate Bayesian computation wraps a sampler around it.

    Where it is a trap: a conjugate model with three parameters. The posterior is normal, beta, or Dirichlet in closed form, and 10,000 draws buy you Monte Carlo error on a number you could have written down. bayesian_inference.py exists for those cases. MCMC is for the posterior you cannot write down, and its cost is measured in gradients, not in elegance.
