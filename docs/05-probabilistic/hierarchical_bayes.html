    05 · Probabilistic models/tier 2/5 variants/source

    Hierarchical Bayes

    Intuition #

    You have measurements from a dozen groups — schools, stores, patients, A/B test cells — and you want each group's mean. Two obvious estimators, both wrong in an instructive way.

    Pool everything: one grand mean, applied to every group. Unbiased if the groups really are identical, hopeless if they are not. Use each group's own data: the mean of four observations, with a standard error of \(\sigma/2\). Unbiased, and so noisy that the ranking of twelve groups is mostly noise.

    The hierarchical model says: the group means are themselves drawn from a distribution. That single assumption produces a third estimator which is neither, and which is better than both.

    \[y_{ij} = \mu + a_j + \varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad a_j \sim N(0, \tau^2), \qquad \varepsilon_{ij} \sim N(0, \sigma^2) \qquad (1)\]

    \(\tau^2\) is how different the groups really are; \(\sigma^2\) is how noisy one observation is. Given those two numbers, the posterior mean of group \(j\)'s mean is a weighted average of the grand mean and the group's own average:

    \[\hat\theta_j = \lambda_j\,\mu + (1-\lambda_j)\,\bar y_j, \qquad \lambda_j = \frac{\sigma^2/n_j}{\tau^2 + \sigma^2/n_j} \qquad (2)\]

    Read the weight. When the group has lots of data (\(n_j\) large) or the groups really differ (\(\tau^2\) large), \(\lambda_j \to 0\) and you trust the group. When the data are noisy or the groups are nearly identical, \(\lambda_j \to 1\) and you trust the pool. The estimate is shrunk toward the centre by exactly the amount the model says is justified.

    This is the whole atom. The variants are the three special cases and the two ways to fill in \(\tau^2\) and \(\sigma^2\): closed-form shrinkage with the variances known (partial_pooling), the two limits (no_pooling, full_pooling), empirical Bayes by EM (empirical_bayes), and a sampler that puts a prior on the variances too (noncentered).

    The demo's headline is the risk comparison. With twelve groups of four, and a true \(\tau^2 = 0.36\) against \(\sigma^2 = 1\):

    Estimator MSE against the true group means
    no pooling 0.2609
    full pooling 0.1868
    partial pooling (\(\lambda = 0.410\)) 0.1263

    The shrinkage estimator beats both extremes, and it is not a lucky draw: it is the Bayes estimator under (1), so no other function of the data has lower expected squared error when the model is true.

    Variants #

    Variant \(\tau^2, \sigma^2\) Estimate of \(\theta_j\) Use when
    partial_pooling given \(\lambda_j\mu + (1-\lambda_j)\bar y_j\) you know the variance components, or are testing the shrinkage itself
    no_pooling — \(\bar y_j\) groups are genuinely unrelated; the baseline the other two must beat
    full_pooling \(\tau^2 = 0\) \(\bar y\) for every group the groups are exchangeable and identical
    empirical_bayes estimated by EM plug-in shrinkage the standard default: two unknowns, fitted by maximum marginal likelihood
    noncentered sampled posterior mean over draws you want the uncertainty in \(\tau^2\) and \(\sigma^2\), not a point plug-in

    The middle three are one line each and exist to be compared against. The interesting pair is empirical_bayes — which treats \((\tau^2, \sigma^2)\) as fixed at their MLEs and is therefore overconfident, because it ignores its own uncertainty in them — and noncentered, which integrates over them.

    Then there is the parameterisation. noncentered is named for a numerical trick, not a model: write \(a_j = \tau z_j\) with \(z_j \sim N(0, 1)\) and the sampler's geometry stops depending on \(\tau\). Same posterior, but a sampler that can traverse it when \(\tau\) is small. The demo measures the difference in effective sample size: 2839 against 96 for \(\log\tau\) over 3000 draws, at identical cost.

    Derivation #

    1. The shrinkage weight #

    Condition on \(a_j\). The group mean is Gaussian around the group effect,

    \[\bar y_j \mid a_j \;\sim\; N\!\left(\mu + a_j,\; \sigma^2/n_j\right) \qquad (3)\]

    and the prior is \(a_j \sim N(0, \tau^2)\). Two Gaussians make a Gaussian, with precision the sum of precisions:

    \[P_j = \frac{n_j}{\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{\tau^2}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[a_j \mid y] = \frac{n_j(\bar y_j - \mu)/\sigma^2}{P_j}, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(a_j \mid y) = \frac{1}{P_j}\]

    Adding \(\mu\) back and rearranging gives (2). Two identities are worth keeping:

    \[1 - \lambda_j = \frac{n_j/\sigma^2}{P_j}, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(\theta_j \mid y) = \frac{1}{P_j} = (1-\lambda_j)\,\frac{\sigma^2}{n_j}\]

    The second is the one that matters downstream: the posterior variance is the sampling variance discounted by the same factor that shrinks the mean. Borrowing strength buys precision, not just a better point estimate.

    2. Why it beats both extremes #

    Write the risk of an estimator \(\lambda\mu + (1-\lambda)\bar y_j\), averaging over the prior on \(\theta_j\) and the sampling noise. The two error terms are independent, so

    \[R(\lambda) = \lambda^2\tau^2 + (1-\lambda)^2\frac{\sigma^2}{n_j} \qquad (4)\]

    a quadratic in \(\lambda\). Minimise it:

    \[\lambda^\star = \frac{\sigma^2/n_j}{\tau^2 + \sigma^2/n_j}, \qquad R(\lambda^\star) = \frac{\tau^2\sigma^2/n_j}{\tau^2 + \sigma^2/n_j} \;<\; \min\!\left(\tau^2,\; \frac{\sigma^2}{n_j}\right) \qquad (5)\]

    So the optimal weight is exactly the posterior weight (2), and the risk at the optimum is below both endpoints — the harmonic-mean-looking expression is smaller than either argument. That is Stein's phenomenon in its simplest hierarchical form, and it is why the demo's MSE ordering is a theorem rather than a coincidence.

    With the demo's numbers, \(\sigma^2/n = 0.25\) and \(\tau^2 = 0.36\) give \(\lambda = 0.410\): the estimate is 59% the group's own mean, 41% the grand mean.

    Three of the twelve groups had a sample variance below \(\sigma^2\). For those groups no_pooling is more confident than the hierarchical answer, because a single \(s_j^2\) is itself a noisy estimate of \(\sigma^2\) and happened to come out low. The pooled estimate wins on average precisely because it does not chase that noise.

    3. Marginal likelihood and empirical Bayes #

    To fit \(\tau^2\) and \(\sigma^2\), integrate the group effects out — they are Gaussian, so this is exact. Two sufficient statistics survive, and they are independent:

    \[\bar y_j \sim N\!\left(\mu,\; \tau^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{n_j}\right), \qquad \frac{ss_j}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n_j-1}, \qquad ss_j = \sum_i (y_{ij} - \bar y_j)^2 \qquad (6)\]

    so the marginal log-likelihood is a sum of two easy pieces:

    \[\log p(y) = \sum_j\left[-\tfrac12\log\!\left(2\pi(\tau^2 + \sigma^2/n_j)\right) - \frac{(\bar y_j - \mu)^2}{2(\tau^2 + \sigma^2/n_j)}\right] + \sum_j\left[-\tfrac{n_j-1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{ss_j}{2\sigma^2}\right] \qquad (7)\]

    This is the objective empirical_bayes maximises, and it is also the quantity reported as loglik_. The second bracket is the part that is easy to forget — drop it and \(\sigma^2 \to 0\) becomes a free lunch, as Section 4 of the failure modes describes.

    EM alternates the posterior of the missing \(a_j\) with the M-step that maximises the expected complete-data log-likelihood. With \(\theta_j\) the posterior mean of \(a_j\) and \(v_j\) its variance:

    \[\mu = \frac{1}{N}\sum_j n_j(\bar y_j - \theta_j), \qquad \tau^2 = \frac{1}{J}\sum_j\left[(\theta_j - \mu)^2 + v_j\right] \qquad (8)\]
    \[\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_j\left[ss_j + n_j\left((\bar y_j - \mu - \theta_j)^2 + v_j\right)\right] \qquad (9)\]

    Every term has a job. The \(v_j\) in (8) is what stops \(\tau^2\) collapsing to zero: the effects are not observed, and their posterior variance is part of their second moment. The \((\bar y_j - \mu - \theta_j)^2\) in (9) is what stops \(\sigma^2\) being biased low: the residual of the group mean around its fitted value is still a residual. In the demo, EM raises the marginal likelihood from \(-60.497\) to \(-59.917\) monotonically, and lands at \(\tau^2 = 0.259\), \(\sigma^2 = 0.830\) against the truth \(0.360\) and \(1.000\) — both within sampling error at twelve groups.

    4. Centered and non-centered #

    Write the group effect two ways:

    \[a_j \sim N(0, \tau^2) \qquad\text{or}\qquad a_j = \tau z_j, \quad z_j \sim N(0, 1) \qquad (10)\]

    They describe the same distribution, and the joint density in \((\mu, z, \log\tau, \log\sigma)\) is the same function of the data. But the geometry is not. In the centered form the conditional precision of \(a_j\) is

    \[P_j = \frac{n_j}{\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{\tau^2} \qquad (11)\]

    so as \(\tau \to 0\) the coordinate \(a_j\) becomes arbitrarily stiff, while its typical value is of order \(\tau\) — a neck of width \(\tau\) inside a funnel of height \(1/\tau^2\). A leapfrog step small enough for the neck cannot cross the mouth; a step large enough for the mouth overshoots the neck. In the non-centered form \(z_j\) is always \(O(1)\) and \(\tau\) enters only through the likelihood, so the curvature stays bounded as \(\tau \to 0\).

    The demo samples both with the same kernel, the same step size (0.15, sixteen leapfrog steps), the same 500 warm-up draws, and reports the ESS of \(\log\tau\): 2839 non-centered against 96 centered out of 3000 draws. The centered sampler is not wrong — it is exact, just catastrophically autocorrelated, which is what an ESS of 3% means.

    The target includes priors, since \(\tau\) and \(\sigma\) are now parameters:

    \[\mu \sim N(0, 5^2), \qquad \log\tau \sim N(0, 2^2), \qquad \log\sigma \sim N(0, 2^2) \qquad (12)\]

    Weak on purpose. A tight prior on \(\log\tau\) would decide the variance components, and then the comparison between parameterisations would be measuring the prior rather than the sampler.

    5. Checking the sampler against the exact answer #

    The group effects can be integrated out analytically, so the marginal posterior over \((\mu, \log\tau, \log\sigma)\) is a three-dimensional integral of (7) times the priors. The demo evaluates it on a \(61 \times 111 \times 81\) grid and uses it as an independent answer:

    Quantity Sampler (3000 draws) Exact grid
    \(\mathbb{E}[\tau^2]\) 0.097 0.108
    \(\mathbb{E}[\sigma^2]\) 1.710 1.726
    group means — max difference 0.015

    The grid never touches the sampler, so this is evidence rather than a tautology. It also surfaces something a single number would hide: the data put \(\tau^2 \approx 0.11\), not the true \(0.0225\). With ten groups of two observations, \(\tau\) is barely identified — the spread of the group means is dominated by \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) — and that weak identification is exactly why the funnel is severe and why the parameterisation matters.

    Code walkthrough #

    The public surface is one frozenset and a class whose fit dispatches on it.

    VARIANTS = frozenset(
    ["partial_pooling", "full_pooling", "no_pooling", "empirical_bayes", "noncentered"]
)

    Three of the five branches are one-liners, and they are worth reading as the definitions they are: no pooling reports the group mean and its own standard error; full pooling reports the grand mean for everyone, with the variance of that estimate, \(\sigma^2/N\); partial pooling calls the shrinkage routine.

            elif self.variant == "empirical_bayes":
            self._em(ybar, n, ss)
            self._shrink(ybar, n, ss, mu=self.mu_)
        elif self.variant == "noncentered":
            self._hmc(ys, centered=False)
        else:
            self.tau2_, self.sigma2_ = self.tau2, self.sigma2
            self._shrink(ybar, n, ss)

    _shrink is (2) and its variance identity in five lines. The clamp is the \(\tau^2 \to 0\) limit: empirical Bayes can legitimately return zero between-group variance, and the correct answer there is full pooling (\(\lambda = 1\)), not a division by zero.

            tau2 = max(self.tau2_, 1e-12)
        prec = n / self.sigma2_ + 1.0 / tau2
        self.group_mean_ = (n * ybar / self.sigma2_ + self.mu_ / tau2) / prec
        self.group_var_ = 1.0 / prec
        self.lambda_ = (self.sigma2_ / n) / (tau2 + self.sigma2_ / n)

    The EM loop is (8)–(9). The E-step computes the posterior of \(a_j\), not of the group mean: theta is the deviation from \(\mu\), which is why the M-step for \(\mu\) subtracts it rather than averaging it.

                prec = n / sigma2 + 1.0 / tau2
            var = 1.0 / prec
            theta = (n * (ybar - mu) / sigma2) / prec

    The M-step is where the two easily-dropped terms live. Both are in the code, and both have a comment, because each one alone produces a plausible-looking fit that is quietly wrong.

                mu = float(np.sum(n * (ybar - theta)) / N)
            tau2 = float(np.mean((theta - mu) ** 2 + var))
            sigma2 = float(np.sum(ss + n * ((ybar - mu - theta) ** 2 + var)) / N)

    The marginal likelihood is (7), and it is the same function the EM is monotone in — which is what makes the demo's monotonicity check meaningful rather than decorative.

        var = tau2 + sigma2 / n
    out = float(np.sum(-0.5 * (LOG2PI + np.log(var)) - 0.5 * (ybar - mu) ** 2 / var))
    if ss is not None:
        out += float(np.sum(-0.5 * (n - 1.0) * (LOG2PI + math.log(sigma2))
                            - 0.5 * ss / sigma2))

    _model is the sampler's target, and the one line that is not obvious is the normalisation: -N * ls, not -N/2 * ls, because ls is \(\log\sigma\) and there is one factor of \(\sigma\) per observation. The ss_total term is the within-group sum of squares; without it the posterior is improper.

            ll = (-0.5 * N * LOG2PI - N * ls
              - 0.5 * (np.sum(n * resid**2) + ss_total) / sigma2)
        ll += -0.5 * J * LOG2PI - 0.5 * np.sum(eff**2) / tau2
        if centered:
            ll -= J * lt

    The gradient is written out by hand, in both parameterisations, because the whole point is that they differ. In the centered branch the prior contributes \(-a_j/\tau^2\); in the non-centered branch the Jacobian of \(a = \tau z\) cancels the \(-\log\tau\) of the prior, so \(z_j\) is pulled to zero by \(-z_j\) alone and \(\tau\) appears only through the likelihood.

            if centered:
            # `a` is the effect itself; the prior pulls it to 0 with precision
            # 1/τ², so a small τ makes this coordinate stiff — the funnel.
            g[1:1 + J] = n * resid / sigma2 - a / tau2
            g[-2] = -0.25 * lt - J + np.sum(a**2) / tau2
        else:
            # z = a/τ is N(0, 1), so its prior term has no τ in it at all: the
            # log τ from the change of variables cancels the -log τ in N(0, τ²)
            g[1:1 + J] = tau * n * resid / sigma2 - a
            g[-2] = -0.25 * lt + tau * np.sum(n * resid * a) / sigma2

    Both closures are handed to the same kernel, with the same step size. That is what makes the ESS comparison a comparison of geometry and not of tuning.

            sampler = MCMC(log_prob, variant="hmc", grad=grad, step_size=0.15,
                       n_leapfrog=16, n_warmup=self.n_warmup)
        chain = sampler.sample(self.n_samples, x0, rng=np.random.default_rng(self.seed))

    The ESS call has one trap: mcmc.ess wants (n_chains, n_samples), so a single coordinate is passed as chain[None, :, k]. Passing chain[:, k][:, None] instead makes it think there are 3000 chains of length 1 and it returns the number of draws, which looks like perfect mixing.

            self.ess_ = np.array([ess(chain[None, :, k]) for k in range(chain.shape[1])])

    Complexity #

    With \(J\) groups, \(N\) observations, and \(N = \sum_j n_j\):

    Variant Time Memory
    no_pooling \(O(N)\) \(O(J)\)
    full_pooling \(O(N)\) \(O(J)\)
    partial_pooling \(O(N + J)\) \(O(J)\)
    empirical_bayes \(O(IJ)\) for \(I\) EM sweeps \(O(J)\)
    noncentered \(O(T L (J+3))\) for \(T\) draws, \(L\) leapfrogs \(O(T(J+3))\)

    The closed forms are linear in the data and effectively free. EM is linear in the number of groups per sweep and converged in 5 sweeps here, 60 in the hand-rolled monotonicity check that starts from \(\tau^2 = \sigma^2 = 1\) — the number of sweeps is set by how far the start is from the answer, not by \(J\).

    The sampler is the expensive one: 3000 draws plus 500 warm-up, 16 leapfrog steps each, on a parameter of dimension \(J + 3 = 13\), so about 730,000 gradient evaluations per chain and two chains in the demo. That is the price of integrating over \((\tau^2, \sigma^2)\) instead of plugging in their MLEs, and it is why empirical_bayes is the default and noncentered is the honest answer when you can afford it.

    The grid check is \(O(A B C J)\): \(61 \times 111 \times 81 \times 10 \approx 5.5\) million evaluations, vectorised in numpy, about 0.1 s. It is affordable only because the model has three scalar parameters after marginalising the groups; the same trick in a general hierarchical model is what INLA and Laplace approximations are for.

    Failure modes #

    Dropping the within-group sum of squares. The first version of the sampler targeted the group means only, as if the data were one observation per group. That makes the posterior improper: send \(\sigma \to 0\) and every group effect to its group mean, and the log density diverges to \(+\infty\) while the prior penalty stays finite. HMC does exactly what the target says and runs down the ridge, ending at \(\sigma^2 \approx 0.01\) with a 3% acceptance rate. The fix is one term, ss_total, and it is the difference between a proper posterior and a divergence. Diagnostic: the sampler's \(\log\sigma\) quantiles. If they sit far below the plausible noise scale, the target is missing a term, not mis-tuned.

    Dropping the residual in the \(\sigma^2\) M-step. Use \(\sigma^2 = \sum_j(ss_j + n_j v_j)/N\) and the estimate is biased low — \(0.741\) against the correct \(0.830\) on this data — and the EM is no longer monotone in the marginal likelihood it claims to maximise (the demo catches this as a \(-0.004\) step). The missing piece is \(n_j(\bar y_j - \mu - \theta_j)^2\): the group mean is not at its fitted value, and that residual is data.

    Dropping the \(v_j\) in the \(\tau^2\) M-step. Then \(\tau^2\) is the variance of the estimated effects, which already include shrinkage toward zero, so it shrinks further every sweep and collapses. With \(\tau^2 = 0\) the model becomes full pooling and every group mean is the same number. This is the classic empirical-Bayes failure, and the fix is to remember that the missing effects have posterior variance.

    The centered funnel. With a small true \(\tau\), a centered sampler's ESS for \(\log\tau\) can fall to a few percent of the draws while its point estimates still look fine, because the rare accepted moves are large. The demo's 96 out of 3000 is a 3% ESS — and the posterior means it produces are still close, which is exactly why ESS and not the estimate is the diagnostic. Fix: non-centered parameterisation, or a mass matrix adapted to the funnel.

    A single observation in a group. no_pooling raises, because \(s_j^2/(n_j-1)\) is undefined at \(n_j = 1\). Partial pooling is defined: the group contributes its one observation with a wide posterior, and \(\lambda_j\) is large because \(\sigma^2/n_j = \sigma^2\) is large compared with \(\tau^2\). This is the case hierarchical models exist for — small-area estimation is entirely about groups too small to estimate alone.

    The exchangeability assumption. The model treats the group labels as interchangeable draws from one distribution. If the groups are actually two clusters — a treatment and a control, say — the prior is wrong and the shrinkage pulls both toward a mean that neither belongs to. The symptom is a residual pattern by group that no \(\tau^2\) can fix. Diagnostic: plot the group means against the shrunken estimates; structure in the residuals means the prior is missing a covariate, not that \(\tau^2\) is wrong.

    An informative prior on the log-scales. With ten groups and two observations each, the data barely identify \(\tau\). A tight prior on \(\log\tau\) — the default in some software is a half-Cauchy with a small scale — will dominate the posterior, and the "estimate" is then the prior. The demo uses \(N(0, 2^2)\) deliberately and still finds \(\mathbb{E}[\tau^2] \approx 0.11\) against a true \(0.0225\): weak identification, not a bug.

    Applications #

    Small-area estimation. The original use: a survey with 4 respondents in a county and 400 in a city, where the county estimate borrows from the state. The US Census Bureau's SAIPE programme and every "model-based" official statistic is this file with covariates added.

    Meta-analysis. Each study estimates an effect with a standard error; the between-study variance \(\tau^2\) is the heterogeneity. Random-effects meta-analysis is (7) with \(n_j\) replaced by \(1/\mathrm{se}_j^2\), and the shrinkage estimate is the pooled effect for a study of that size. bayesian_inference.py has the single-study conjugate version.

    A/B testing with many cells. Ten variants, each with a few hundred users: ranking the raw conversion rates is mostly noise, and ranking the shrunken ones is not. This is the "empirical Bayes" that makes high-dimensional online experiments work, and it is the same arithmetic as the demo.

    Education and health-care report cards. Value-added models for teachers, hospital mortality rates, and surgeon league tables are all hierarchical: the whole point is that the noisy small-\(n\) units are not ranked on their raw averages. The policy debate about whether to publish shrunken or raw estimates is a debate about (2).

    Genomics. limma's moderated \(t\)-statistic is empirical Bayes on gene-wise variances: each gene's variance is shrunk toward a pooled estimate, exactly as (2) shrinks a group mean, and the degrees of freedom are the \(v_j\) correction in a different guise. Millions of features, one variance component.

    Priors in machine learning. Hierarchical priors are how multilevel models, mixed-effects packages (lme4, brms), and Bayesian deep learning share parameters across groups. The non-centered parameterisation is standard advice in Stan and PyMC for exactly the funnel reason the demo measures.

    Where it is a trap: when the groups are not exchangeable, when \(J\) is very small (fewer than about five groups, \(\tau^2\) is not estimable and a fixed prior is more honest), and when the group means are the quantity of interest and you cannot afford to integrate over the variance components — the plug-in EB answer is then overconfident by exactly the amount it ignores. mcmc.py is the sampler underneath the last variant, and variational_inference.py is the alternative when \(J\) is in the thousands and MCMC is too slow.
