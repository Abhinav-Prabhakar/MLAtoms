    07 · Clustering/tier 1/5 variants/source

    HDBSCAN

    Intuition #

    DBSCAN has one knob that decides everything: eps. It also has a structural weakness — a single eps cannot fit a dense blob and a diffuse cloud at the same time. Set it for the blob and the cloud is all noise; set it for the cloud and the blob merges with its neighbours.

    HDBSCAN dissolves the problem instead of tuning it. Build the clustering at every density, and then decide which clusters are worth keeping:

    1. Core distance. d_core(x) is the distance to the min_samples-th nearest neighbour — a local density estimate at x.
    2. Mutual reachability. Replace d(a,b) by max(d_core(a), d_core(b), d(a,b)). Two points in a sparse region are pushed apart even if they are physically close, so a chain of sparse points cannot bridge two dense clusters.
    3. Minimum spanning tree of that graph. Its edges, sorted by weight, are the entire single-linkage hierarchy: every merge is one edge.
    4. Stability. A cluster earns credit for every point that stays inside it over its λ = 1/distance lifetime.
    5. Excess of mass. Keep a cluster when it earns more credit than its children combined; otherwise keep the children.

    Because the threshold eps is gone, only min_cluster_size (how small a group may be) and min_samples (how smooth the density estimate is) remain, and both are far less sensitive than eps. The demo's three blobs of different density plus a uniform background come out as exactly three clusters, with 0.989 accuracy among labelled points and 0.907 of the true inliers recovered.

    Variants #

    Variant Selection What it shows
    hdbscan excess of mass on the whole hierarchy the reference algorithm
    hdbscan_star one flat DBSCAN* cut at the best level the hierarchy read without stability
    stability rank all nodes by stability, keep the best non-overlapping ones stability as a first-class quantity
    soft_clustering EOM clusters, then inverse-distance memberships graded assignment
    approximate EOM on an MST built from a k-NN graph the scaling trick

    hdbscan is Campello et al.'s excess-of-mass (EOM) rule and is what "HDBSCAN" means without qualification. hdbscan_star is the other classical reading: pick the level λ where the hierarchy has the most valid clusters and take DBSCAN* there. It is cheaper (no stability accumulation) and it labels nearly everything — 35 noise points against 127, with 1.000 inlier coverage — but it also absorbs part of the uniform background, which is why its accuracy is 0.927 rather than 0.989.

    stability ranks the nodes directly: sort every cluster-size-valid node by stability and greedily keep the best ones that do not overlap. On well-separated data it agrees with EOM, which is the point of the demo ([115.1 40.3 118.1] from both), but it exposes the raw numbers and behaves differently when a parent node is genuinely more stable than its children.

    soft_clustering keeps the EOM clusters and replaces the hard label with a membership vector. Each cluster is represented by its densest point, memberships are 1/d²-weighted, and small memberships are zeroed and renormalised. 79 of the 650 points end up split between two clusters — the boundary points whose assignment is a coin flip — while the median point has a maximum membership of 1.00.

    approximate builds the MST from a k-nearest-neighbour graph instead of every pair. On the demo it examines 6383 candidate pairs instead of 210925, returns the same three clusters, and loses nothing in accuracy (0.989).

    Derivation #

    Core distance and mutual reachability. Let k = min_samples and let d_k(x) be the distance from x to its k-th nearest neighbour. Then

    \[d_{\text{core}}(x) = d_k(x), \qquad d_{\text{mreach}}(a,b) = \max\{d_{\text{core}}(a),\, d_{\text{core}}(b),\, d(a,b)\}\]

    The mutual reachability graph is the complete graph with these weights. It has two interpretations that matter:

    • It is the DBSCAN graph at level λ if you keep only edges with d_mreach ≤ 1/λ: two points are connected exactly when both are core at that radius and close enough.
    • It inflates the distance between a core point and a sparse neighbour to the core radius, so the sparse point's attachment to the dense cluster becomes as long as the cluster's own density scale. That is what suppresses single-linkage chaining.

    Why the MST is the hierarchy. In a graph with edge weights, single-linkage clustering at threshold d is the connected components of the edges of weight ≤ d. Kruskal's algorithm processes edges in increasing order and unions components — so it is single-linkage, and the sequence of unions it performs is the dendrogram, at a cost of one MST instead of all n(n−1)/2 edges. Prim's algorithm computes the same MST without materialising the sort. That equivalence is the theoretical core of HDBSCAN: one MST contains every single-linkage clustering of the data.

    Formally, for any threshold d, the components of the mutual reachability graph at d are the components of the subgraph of the MST restricted to edges ≤ d. So the hierarchy can be built from n−1 numbers rather than n².

    λ and the tree. Write λ = 1/d. Small λ is coarse (large distance), large λ is fine. Each internal node c of the dendrogram is created by one merge at level λ_c = 1/w_c and splits at λ = level(c). Its lifetime is the interval (birth(c), level(c)], where birth(c) is the λ of the merge that created its parent. Along any root-to-leaf path these intervals partition the λ axis, so at any λ exactly one node per path is "active" — that is why a flat cut is well defined.

    The value λ_p = 1/d_core(p) is where point p stops being a core point: beyond it, p is too sparse to anchor a cluster.

    Stability. The stability of c is the total λ-lifetime its points spend inside it:

    \[s(c) = \sum_{p \in c}\Big(\min\big(\lambda_p,\, \text{level}(c)\big) - \text{birth}(c)\Big)_+\]

    The min says a point contributes only until it stops being core, and only while c exists; the (·)_+ says points that are already gone when c appears contribute nothing. A tight, long-lived cluster accumulates a large s; a transient merge of two distant blobs accumulates almost nothing.

    Excess of mass. Define the selection recursively:

    \[\text{keep}(c) = \begin{cases} \{c\} & \text{if } s(c) > \sum_{c' \in \text{children}(c)} \text{keep}(c'),\\[4pt] \bigcup_{c'} \text{keep}(c') & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}\]

    with leaves that are smaller than min_cluster_size contributing nothing. A parent is kept only when merging its children adds mass — that is the "excess" in excess of mass. It prefers fewer, larger clusters when the children are unstable fragments of one dense region, and more, smaller clusters when the parent spans a sparse gap.

    The root always wins this comparison, because s(root) = Σ_p min(λ_p, level_root) grows with n while its children's stabilities are bounded by their own short lifetimes. HDBSCAN therefore excludes the root unless allow_single_cluster=True — this is a real branch in the library, and forgetting it silently returns one cluster on every dataset. The demo relies on the excluded-root path.

    Flat cut. At level λ, the active nodes are {c : birth(c) < λ ≤ level(c)}, and they partition the leaves. A DBSCAN* reading of the hierarchy is one such cut. Choosing λ to maximise the number of clusters of size ≥ min_cluster_size (ties broken by covered points) is the greedy optimum over the n candidate levels, which is what _best_flat_cut enumerates. It is cheap and exact, and it needs no stability at all — at the cost of being sensitive to which level wins.

    Soft membership. For selected cluster c with exemplar e_c (its densest point),

    \[\mu_c(x) = \frac{1/\|x-e_c\|^2}{\sum_{c'} 1/\|x-e_{c'}\|^2}\]

    then entries below min_probability are zeroed and the row renormalised. Inverse-square distance is the natural kernel here: it is the harmonic potential of a point mass, so a point twice as far from one exemplar gets a quarter of the weight, and a point sitting on an exemplar gets essentially all of it.

    Outlier score. The GLOSH score of Campello et al. is

    \[\text{score}(p) = 1 - \frac{\lambda_p}{\lambda_{\max}}\]

    with λ_max the largest core-λ in the sample. It is scale-free (it depends only on the density ordering), it needs no labelled anomalies, and it is exactly the quantity the hierarchy already computed. The demo's uniform background scores 0.943 against 0.718 for clustered points.

    Code walkthrough #

    Core distances come from one partition, which is O(n) per row instead of a sort:

        def _core_distances(self, D: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        n = D.shape[0]
        k = min(self.min_samples, n - 1)
        return np.partition(D, k, axis=1)[:, k]

    D[i, i] = 0, so index k in the sorted row is the k-th other point — the neighbour count convention that makes min_samples=1 the nearest neighbour rather than the point itself. np.partition places the k-th smallest value in position k and leaves the rest unordered, which is all this needs; a full sort would be O(n log n) for the same answer. min(self.min_samples, n − 1) guards a dataset smaller than k.

    Prim's algorithm runs over the dense matrix, keeping one best edge per unseen node:

            for _ in range(n):
            masked = np.where(seen, INF, best)
            u = int(np.argmin(masked))
            seen[u] = True
            if parent[u] >= 0:
                edges.append((float(mr[u, parent[u]]), int(parent[u]), u))
            upd = (~seen) & (mr[u] < best)
            best[upd] = mr[u][upd]
            parent[upd] = u

    masked is the standard "argmin over unseen" and is a full O(n) copy each step, so the loop is O(n²) — the same order as the distance matrix it operates on, which is why it is not worth a heap here. best[u] starts at infinity except for node 0, so the tree grows from an arbitrary root and the first iteration adds nothing. The recorded edge weight is the parent's side of the cut, which is the MST edge; taking mr[u, v] for the current u and its old parent would be the same number.

    The k-NN approximation replaces the n² scan with a sparse edge list:

            k = min(self.approx_neighbors or 3 * self.min_samples, n - 1)
        idx = np.argpartition(mr, k, axis=1)[:, :k + 1]
        edges: dict[tuple[int, int], float] = {}
        for i in range(n):
            for j in idx[i]:

    k+1 columns because argpartition includes the point itself. The dict keyed on the sorted pair deduplicates: (i,j) and (j,i) are the same edge and must not be counted twice, or Kruskal would see two edges for one connection and the union-find would silently ignore the duplicate. 3 * min_samples is the usual rule of thumb; raising it makes the graph denser and the result more exact at the cost of the speed-up.

    The tree is built by replaying MST edges in increasing order:

            for w, i, j in sorted(edges):
            ri, rj = find(i), find(j)
            if ri == rj:
                continue
            level = 1.0 / w if w > EPS else INF
            a, b = node_of[ri], node_of[rj]
            nodes.append({
                "level": float(level), "birth": 0.0, "children": [a, b],
                "points": nodes[a]["points"] + nodes[b]["points"],
                "size": nodes[a]["size"] + nodes[b]["size"],
            })
            uf[ri] = rj
            node_of[rj] = len(nodes) - 1

    Each accepted edge creates exactly one internal node, so the tree has 2n−1 nodes and the loop is Kruskal with union by "attach the second root under the first". level is 1/w, and nodes[child]["birth"] is filled in afterwards in one pass, because a node's birth is its parent's level and the parent does not exist yet when the child is created. w = 0 (duplicate points) gives an infinite level, which is correct: a duplicate is infinitely dense and never splits. The union-find is path-halving, which is enough for n operations.

    Stability is the only place the point lists are used as more than bookkeeping:

            for i, node in enumerate(self.nodes):
            lo, hi = node["birth"], node["level"]
            total = 0.0
            for p in node["points"]:
                lp = lam[p]
                if not np.isfinite(lp):
                    lp = 1e300
                total += max(0.0, min(lp, hi) - lo)
            stab[i] = float(total)

    min(lp, hi) is the point's fall-out λ (its own density or the cluster's split, whichever comes first) and lo is when the cluster appeared; the difference is the λ-lifetime the point spends inside. max(0, ...) handles the case lo > λ_p — a point that was already non-core when the cluster was born. The 1e300 substitution for duplicate points (infinite λ) keeps the arithmetic finite without changing any comparison.

    Selection is a five-line recursion:

            picks: list[int] = []
        score = 0.0
        for j in node["children"]:
            sub, s = self._eom(j)
            picks.extend(sub)
            score += s
        if self._stab[i] > score:
            return [i], self._stab[i]
        return picks, score

    The returned pair is (clusters, total stability of those clusters), so the parent's comparison uses the stability of what the children would actually contribute — not their raw stability, which would double count when a child is itself replaced by grandchildren. Sizes below min_cluster_size return ([], 0.0) at the top of the function, which prunes entire subtrees of fragments in one test.

    The flat cut is a sweep over the distinct levels:

            for lam in levels:
            cut = [i for i, n in enumerate(nodes)
                   if n["birth"] < lam <= n["level"]
                   and n["size"] >= self.min_cluster_size]
            if not cut:
                continue
            key = (len(cut), float(sum(nodes[i]["size"] for i in cut)))
            if key > best_key:
                best_key, best = key, cut

    The strict birth < λ ≤ level matters at the boundaries: adjacent nodes share an endpoint (level(child) = birth(parent)), and using ≤ on both sides would put two nested nodes in the same cut. The tuple comparison (count, coverage) means "most clusters first, then the most points covered" — a deliberate choice, since a single huge cluster covers the most points but is useless as a clustering.

    Labels are assigned per selected subtree:

            for idx, c in enumerate(selected):
            node = self.nodes[c]
            level = node["level"]
            for p in node["points"]:
                labels[p] = idx
                proba[p] = 1.0 if level <= 0.0 else min(
                    1.0, float(self._lam[p]) / level)

    proba is the fraction of the cluster's λ-range the point survives: 1 for a point denser than the cluster's split level, less for a point that barely made it in. It is the HDBSCAN membership strength, and it is what separates a core member from a border one without a second membership model.

    Soft memberships use the densest point of each cluster as its exemplar:

            E = np.asarray(exemplars)
        d2 = ((X[:, None, :] - E[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(axis=2)
        w = 1.0 / np.maximum(d2, EPS)
        proba = w / w.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
        proba[proba < self.min_probability] = 0.0
        proba /= np.maximum(proba.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True), EPS)

    The broadcast X[:, None, :] - E[None, :, :] is an n×k×d temporary, which is fine for a handful of clusters and would need blocking for many. Multiplying by 1/d² rather than taking a softmax of −d is deliberate: no temperature to tune, and the weights decay at the same rate as a physical potential. Zeroing then renormalising keeps rows summing to one after the threshold, which is what makes the result a probability vector rather than a truncated one.

    The density score needs only λ:

            lam = np.where(np.isfinite(self._lam), self._lam, np.nan)
        top = float(np.nanmax(lam)) if np.isfinite(lam).any() else 1.0
        self.outlier_scores_ = np.clip(1.0 - np.nan_to_num(lam, nan=0.0) / top,
                                       0.0, 1.0)

    np.isfinite masks the infinite λ of duplicate points before the max, because inf/inf is nan and would poison every score. np.nanmax over the finite mask is the densest point in the sample; the clip keeps the scores in [0,1] when a point is denser than the reference (impossible in-sample, possible for out-of-sample queries).

    Complexity #

    Step Time Memory
    Distance matrix O(n² d) O(n²)
    Core distances O(n²) O(n)
    MST (Prim) O(n²) O(n)
    Tree build O(n log n) sort + O(n α(n)) unions O(n) nodes
    Stability `O(Σ_c c
    EOM selection O(n) O(n)
    Flat cut O(n²) over levels O(n)
    Soft memberships O(n K d) O(n K)

    The O(n²) distance matrix is the bottleneck, and it is the reason HDBSCAN is usually paired with a k-d tree or an approximate nearest-neighbour index: with one, the MST is built from O(n k) edges and the whole pipeline becomes O(n k log n). This file's approximate variant is the graph half of that trick — the demo drops from 210925 candidate pairs to 6383 — but it still computes the full matrix, so the timings stay quadratic. Σ_c |c| is O(n log n) for a balanced tree and O(n²) in the worst case (a caterpillar); it is the price of storing point lists per node.

    Failure modes #

    1. min_cluster_size too small. The hierarchy has a node for every merge, so a tiny threshold turns the tree into noise fragments and EOM selects many small clusters. Set it to the smallest group you would act on (15–50 is usual), not to 1.
    2. min_samples too small. With min_samples = 1 the mutual reachability reduces to plain distance and HDBSCAN becomes single linkage: the demo's uniform background would chain the three blobs into one cluster. min_samples is the knob that buys robustness to chaining.
    3. The root trap. s(root) grows with n, so selecting on the whole tree returns one cluster for every dataset. The allow_single_cluster=False branch is not a nicety; the demo would fail without it.
    4. min_samples larger than the cluster. If a cluster has fewer than min_samples points, none of its members is core there, so its core distances equal the min_samples-th neighbour distance outside the cluster and the whole group looks sparse. Keep min_samples < min_cluster_size.
    5. Duplicate points. d_core = 0 gives λ = ∞ and infinite stability, which can dominate the selection. The code guards the arithmetic (1e300) but not the semantics: deduplicate, or accept that exact duplicates form an unbreakable core.
    6. Flat-cut instability. hdbscan_star picks one level, and the level with the most clusters is not necessarily the level with the best clusters. On the demo it labels 35 noise points and covers everything, absorbing background that EOM rejects — higher recall, lower precision.
    7. Soft memberships ignore density. Inverse-distance memberships are geometric: a sparse background point gets a confident membership if it happens to sit near an exemplar. Combine them with outlier_scores_ or with probabilities_ from the EOM fit when density matters.
    8. GLOSH with one component. The score is relative to the densest point in the sample, so on a single uniform blob every point scores near zero even if the blob is the anomaly relative to incoming data. Fit on reference data and score new points against the fitted λ_max.
    9. Approximate MST disconnect. A k-NN graph can be disconnected, which would leave a forest instead of a tree. The code adds a synthetic root at λ = 0 to keep the selection well-defined, but the resulting hierarchy has a fake top-level merge; check n_edges_ == n − 1 before trusting an approximate fit.
    10. Quadratic memory. n = 100k needs 80 GB for the distance matrix. Use the k-NN path, a spatial index, or Borůvka's algorithm on a sparse graph — HDBSCAN's asymptotics are fine, this file's implementation is not.

    Applications #

    • Anomaly detection without labels. outlier_scores_ ranks points by local density; the demo's background separates at 0.943 against 0.718. Used for intrusion detection, sensor faults, and transaction monitoring.
    • Variable-density clustering. Astronomy (star clusters against a background field), geo-spatial event data, and single-cell expression data all have clusters whose density varies by orders of magnitude, where DBSCAN's single eps fails.
    • Topic discovery in embeddings. Cluster document embeddings without choosing K; the noise label isolates boilerplate and one-off documents.
    • Hierarchical taxonomies. The tree is a dendrogram, so cluster_stability_ and the node structure give a hierarchy rather than a flat partition — useful for browsing and for choosing a granularity later.
    • Pre-labelling for supervision. Cluster the unlabelled pool, label the clusters, and send the noise to human review; the soft memberships say which points are worth a second look.
    • As a stronger baseline than k-means. When someone reaches for k-means on data with unknown K and outliers, HDBSCAN is usually the better first attempt and needs less tuning.
