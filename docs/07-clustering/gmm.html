    07 · Clustering/tier 1/7 variants/source

    Gaussian mixture models

    Intuition #

    k-means says a point belongs to one cluster. A GMM says a point was generated by one of K Gaussians, and we just did not see which. That extra assumption buys three things: soft assignments (r_ik is the probability that point i came from component k), a density you can evaluate at new points, and covariances — so clusters may be elongated, rotated, or of different sizes.

    The price is a harder optimisation. The observed-data likelihood

    \[\log p(X) = \sum_i \log \sum_k \pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x_i\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)\]

    has a log of a sum, so the stationary equations for the parameters couple all components and have no closed form. The fix is EM: guess the latent responsibilities, solve the parameters as if the responsibilities were counts, and repeat. Each iteration provably increases the likelihood, and the fixed point satisfies both sets of stationary equations simultaneously.

    The variants answer the two practical questions a GMM raises. How flexible should Σ_k be? — full, diag, spherical, tied. How do I avoid the likelihood diverging when a component collapses onto one point? — bic_selection chooses K and two Bayesian variants, variational and map_mixture, put priors on the weights and covariances.

    Variants #

    Variant Covariance Extra parameters Why it exists
    full one free d×d per component K·d(d+1)/2 the general model
    diag axis-aligned, independent features K·d high d, few points per component
    spherical σ_k² I K isotropic blobs, k-means-like
    tied one shared Σ d(d+1)/2 components differ in location only
    variational full, Dirichlet prior on π +K prunes unused components
    bic_selection full, refit for each K — chooses the number of components
    map_mixture full, inverse-Wishart prior — keeps collapsing covariances finite

    full is the reference and the most expensive: in d=2 it has three covariance parameters per component, in d=50 it has 1275. diag and spherical are the regularised answers for wide data; they bias the fit but cut the parameter count by an order of magnitude. tied is the right model when the components share a shape and differ only in mean — the demo fits data generated with one shared covariance and the tied model loses only 7.4 log-likelihood units against full, while using a third of the covariance parameters.

    variational and map_mixture are not different models so much as different estimators of the same model. Both add a prior, and both prevent the degenerate solution where one Gaussian shrinks to a single point and its likelihood goes to +∞:

    • variational puts a Dirichlet(α₀) prior on the weights and optimises an ELBO. A component that explains few points gets α_k → α₀, so its weight is driven toward zero instead of exactly zero — automatic pruning. The demo asks for 5 components with α₀ = 0.3 and gets weights [0.247 0.313 0.272 0.045 0.123]: two real components and two small ones.
    • map_mixture puts a Dirichlet prior on the weights and an inverse-Wishart prior on each covariance, then maximises the posterior. The covariance estimate becomes (S_k + ν₀Ψ)/(N_k + ν₀ + d + 1) instead of S_k/N_k, so a component with three points gets a covariance pulled toward the global one. The demo's MAP fit has minimum eigenvalue 0.559 where the unregularised fit has 0.215.

    bic_selection is the frequentist answer to "how many components": fit K = 1…k_max and take the smallest

    \[\text{BIC} = -2\log\hat L + p\log n\]

    where p counts weights, means and covariance parameters. On the demo's three blobs it selects k=3 (scores 5332.4 4746.4 4503.3 4529.3 4561.1 4594.9).

    Derivation #

    The latent variable. Introduce z_i ∈ {1,…,K} with P(z_i=k) = π_k and x_i | z_i=k ~ N(μ_k, Σ_k). Then the joint is p(x,z) = π_z N(x|μ_z,Σ_z) and summing over z gives the mixture. The observed-data log-likelihood is the log of that sum.

    Why EM. For any distribution q(z) over the latent variables,

    \[\log p(x) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\log \frac{p(x,z)}{q(z)}\right]}_{\mathcal{L}(q,\theta)} + \mathrm{KL}\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z|x)\big)\]

    The KL term is non-negative, so L(q,θ) ≤ log p(x) with equality when q(z) = p(z|x). EM alternates:

    • E-step: set q(z_i) = p(z_i|x_i, θ) — the responsibilities r_ik ∝ π_k N(x_i|μ_k,Σ_k). This makes the bound tight, so L = log p(x).
    • M-step: maximise L over θ. Because the bound is tight and the maximisation can only increase it, log p(x) after the M-step is at least its value before the E-step. That is the monotonicity guarantee.

    E-step. Bayes' rule with the mixture prior:

    \[r_{ik} = \frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x_i\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)} {\sum_{j=1}^K \pi_j\,\mathcal{N}(x_i\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)}\]

    In code this is computed in log space and normalised with a log-sum-exp, because N(x|μ,Σ) underflows to zero for a point far from every component.

    M-step. With the responsibilities fixed, the expected complete log-likelihood is

    \[Q(\theta) = \sum_{i,k} r_{ik}\Big[\log \pi_k - \tfrac12\log|\Sigma_k| - \tfrac12(x_i-\mu_k)^\top\Sigma_k^{-1}(x_i-\mu_k)\Big] + \text{const}\]

    Let N_k = Σ_i r_ik. Setting ∂Q/∂μ_k = 0:

    \[\sum_i r_{ik}\,\Sigma_k^{-1}(x_i-\mu_k) = 0 \implies \mu_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i r_{ik}x_i\]

    Setting ∂Q/∂Σ_k = 0 (using ∂log|Σ|/∂Σ = Σ^{-1} and ∂(aᵀΣ^{-1}a)/∂Σ = -Σ^{-1}aaᵀΣ^{-1}):

    \[\Sigma_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i r_{ik}(x_i-\mu_k)(x_i-\mu_k)^\top\]

    And the weights, with the constraint Σπ = 1 enforced by a Lagrange multiplier λ:

    \[\frac{\partial}{\partial\pi_k}\left[\sum_i\sum_j r_{ij}\log\pi_j - \lambda\Big(\sum_j\pi_j-1\Big)\right] = \frac{N_k}{\pi_k} - \lambda = 0 \implies \pi_k = \frac{N_k}{n}\]

    because Σ_k N_k = n. The 1/N_k versus 1/n distinction is the whole difference between a component's covariance and the marginal one: responsibilities shrink N_k below the cluster's row count, which is why EM covariances are slightly larger than the hard-assignment covariances of k-means.

    Covariance constraints. The three constrained variants are the same M-step followed by a projection onto the allowed set:

    • diag: keep diag(Σ_k), so the projection is Σ ↦ diag(Σ).
    • spherical: keep the mean eigenvalue, Σ ↦ (tr Σ / d) I.
    • tied: one shared Σ = (Σ_k N_k (x−μ_k)(x−μ_k)ᵀ)/(Σ_k N_k) using every component's residuals, then broadcast to all K.

    Each projection is the EM step for the constrained model: maximising Q over the constrained set is exactly "unconstrained M-step, then project", because the constraint set is convex and the projection is the closest point in it. _project_cov adds reg_covar·I first so the result is positive definite and the Cholesky factor exists.

    Variational EM. Put π ~ Dirichlet(α₀) and approximate the posterior with a mean-field family q(z)q(π). The optimal q(π) is Dirichlet(α) with α_k = α₀ + Σ_i r_ik, and the optimal q(z) uses the expected log weight

    \[\mathbb{E}[\log \pi_k] = \psi(\alpha_k) - \psi\Big(\sum_j\alpha_j\Big)\]

    in place of log π_k. The ELBO becomes

    \[\mathcal{L} = \sum_{i,k} r_{ik}\big[\log \mathcal{N}(x_i|\mu_k,\Sigma_k) + \mathbb{E}\log\pi_k\big] - \mathrm{KL}\big(\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha)\,\|\,\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_0)\big)\]

    with the closed-form Dirichlet KL

    \[\mathrm{KL} = \log B(\alpha_0) - \log B(\alpha) + \sum_k(\alpha_k-\alpha_{0k})\,\mathbb{E}\log\pi_k\]

    _elbo is exactly this. The demo asserts the bound is monotone (np.diff(bound_history_) >= -1e-6), which is the practical check that the E- and M-steps are consistent — a sign error in the KL or a mismatched E[log π] breaks monotonicity immediately.

    MAP. Replace the M-step with the posterior mode. For the weights, the Dirichlet prior gives

    \[\pi_k = \frac{N_k + \alpha_0 - 1}{n + K(\alpha_0 - 1)}\]

    which differs from N_k/n by pseudo-counts; that is why α₀ = 1 recovers the ML estimate. For the covariances, the inverse-Wishart prior IW(ν₀, Ψ) with Ψ = prior_cov gives the mode

    \[\Sigma_k = \frac{S_k + \nu_0\Psi}{N_k + \nu_0 + d + 1}\]

    The d+1 is the mode's degrees-of-freedom correction; dropping it is the most common MAP-GMM bug, and it makes the estimate slightly too small in high dimensions. This is the same family of update as the bayesian_inference atom's conjugate posteriors.

    BIC. For a model with p free parameters, BIC = −2 log L̂ + p log n. The penalty is a first-order Laplace approximation to the marginal likelihood: it counts each parameter as ½ log n bits of evidence, so a component must improve the fit by more than p log n to be worth adding. For a GMM, p = (K−1) + K d + K·cov_params, which is what _cov_params returns per component.

    Code walkthrough #

    The normal density is evaluated through the Cholesky factor, never through an explicit inverse:

    def _log_gauss(X: np.ndarray, mean: np.ndarray, cov: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Log density of a multivariate normal, via Cholesky."""
    d = X.shape[1]
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(cov)
    diff = (X - mean).T
    sol = np.linalg.solve(L, diff)
    return -0.5 * (d * np.log(2.0 * np.pi) + 2.0 * np.log(np.diag(L)).sum()
                   + (sol * sol).sum(axis=0))

    cov = LLᵀ, so log|Σ| = 2 Σ log L_ii and the quadratic form is ||L⁻¹(x−μ)||². Solving the triangular system is O(d²) and numerically stable; forming Σ⁻¹ would square the condition number. The 2.0 * np.log(np.diag(L)).sum() is per-component and scalar, so the whole expression returns a vector over rows.

    Seeding reuses the k-means++ D² rule, then gives every component the global covariance:

            means = X[idx].copy()
        global_cov = np.atleast_2d(np.cov(X.T, bias=True))
        global_cov = global_cov + self.reg_covar * np.eye(d)
        covs = np.repeat(global_cov[None], k, axis=0)
        weights = np.full(k, 1.0 / k)

    Starting every covariance at the global one is a deliberate choice: a component that starts with a tiny covariance can collapse before it has seen enough points, and the responsibilities would then reinforce the collapse. bias=True divides by n rather than n−1, matching the M-step's 1/N_k. np.atleast_2d keeps the d=1 case from producing a scalar.

    The E-step is where the log of the sum is tamed:

            if self.variant == "variational":
            alpha = getattr(self, "_alpha", None)
            if alpha is None or alpha.size != k:
                alpha = np.full(k, self.alpha0)
            Elogpi = _digamma(alpha) - _digamma(alpha.sum())
            log_prob = log_prob + Elogpi[None, :]
        else:
            log_prob = log_prob + np.log(np.maximum(weights, EPS))[None, :]
        lse = _logsumexp(log_prob)
        resp = np.exp(log_prob - lse[:, None])

    log_prob is n×K and lse is its per-row log-sum-exp, so the subtraction turns the log posterior into a normalised probability row without ever exponentiating a large number. The variational branch swaps log π_k for E[log π_k] — a one-line change that turns EM into variational EM. getattr(self, "_alpha", None) matters because predict_proba calls _e_step on a fitted model that may or may not carry the posterior.

    The weight update is a single division, and the MAP branch is an affine correction:

            if self.variant == "map_mixture":
            prior = self.alpha0
            weights = (Nk + prior - 1.0) / (n + k * (prior - 1.0))
            weights = np.maximum(weights, EPS)
            weights /= weights.sum()
        else:
            weights = Nk / n

    The renormalisation at the end keeps the weights a distribution even when the MAP denominator is odd. With α₀ < 1 the MAP weights can go negative — the maximum clips them — which is the signal that the prior is being used as a sparsity device rather than a proper weak prior.

    Means and covariances are accumulated as weighted sums, which is why the whole M-step is two matrix products:

            for j in range(k):
            r = resp[:, j]
            diff = X - means[j]
            S = (r[:, None] * diff).T @ diff
            nk = float(Nk[j])
            if self.variant == "map_mixture":
                cov = (S + self.nu0 * prior_cov) / (nk + self.nu0 + d + 1.0)
            else:
                cov = S / max(nk, EPS)
            cov = self._project_cov(cov)
            covs[j] = cov

    (r[:, None] * diff).T @ diff is Σ_i r_ik (x_i−μ_k)(x_i−μ_k)ᵀ in one d×n by n×d product. prior_cov is the global covariance and sets the scale the MAP estimate is pulled toward. _project_cov then enforces the variant's structure and adds reg_covar, so every covs[j] is positive definite by the time _log_gauss sees it.

    The tied shared covariance is accumulated after the per-component loop:

            if self.covariance_type == "tied":
            total = np.zeros((d, d))
            for j in range(k):
                r = resp[:, j]
                diff = X - means[j]
                total += (r[:, None] * diff).T @ diff
            shared = total / max(float(Nk.sum()), EPS)
            shared = self._project_cov(shared)
            covs[:] = shared

    The denominator is Σ_k N_k, which is n in exact arithmetic, but computing it from the responsibilities keeps the estimate consistent if a caller passes a normalised resp that does not sum to n. covs[:] = shared broadcasts one array to all components — they must be the same object conceptually, and copying would let a later _project_cov diverge them.

    The variational loop is the only place the M-step is split in two, because the weights come from the Dirichlet posterior rather than from N_k/n:

                    alpha = self.alpha0 + resp.sum(axis=0)
                self._alpha = alpha
                Elogpi = _digamma(alpha) - _digamma(alpha.sum())
                log_joint = _log_gauss_matrix(X, means, covs) + Elogpi[None, :]
                resp = np.exp(log_joint - _logsumexp(log_joint)[:, None])
                resp /= np.maximum(resp.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True), EPS)
                weights, means, covs = self._m_step(X, resp, weights, means, covs)
                weights = alpha / alpha.sum()

    The responsibilities are recomputed after α is updated, so the E-step uses the posterior implied by the current responsibilities; the M-step then uses the refreshed ones. weights = alpha / alpha.sum() is the posterior mean of the Dirichlet, and it overwrites the N_k/n that _m_step just wrote — that is the only line where variational differs from the MAP path in the loop body.

    _digamma is a hand-written implementation because the file is numpy-only:

    def _digamma(x: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Digamma by recurrence + asymptotic series; no scipy dependency."""
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float).copy()
    out = np.zeros_like(x)
    while np.any(x < 6.0):
        small = x < 6.0
        out[small] -= 1.0 / x[small]
        x[small] += 1.0

    ψ(x+1) = ψ(x) + 1/x shifts the argument into the region where the asymptotic series ψ(x) ≈ log x − 1/(2x) − 1/(12x²) + … is accurate; the while runs at most a handful of times because every step adds 1. The vectorised x[small] += 1.0 is what keeps it O(K) rather than a Python loop over components.

    Model selection is a loop over K, keeping the best BIC:

                for k in range(1, self.k_max + 1):
                res = max(
                    (self._fit_once(X, k) for _ in range(self.n_init)),
                    key=lambda r: r["bound"],
                )
                scores[k] = res["bic"]
                if best is None or res["bic"] < best[1]["bic"]:
                    best = (k, res)

    max(..., key=bound) picks the best of the restarts by likelihood, and the BIC comparison happens across K. Mixing those up — selecting the restart by BIC — would bias toward the restart with the fewest effective components. scores is a plain dict during the loop and only becomes the list bic_scores_ at the end, so the public attribute is indexable by k−1.

    Complexity #

    Step Time Memory
    One E-step, full covariance O(n K d³) (Cholesky per component) + O(n K d²) O(n K)
    One M-step O(n K d²) O(K d²)
    One EM iteration O(n K d³) O(n K + K d²)
    n_init restarts × n_init same
    bic_selection × k_max, plus the parameter scan same
    Prediction, one point O(K d³ + K d²) O(K d²)

    The d³ is the Cholesky factorisation, recomputed per component on every E-step even though the covariance only changes in the M-step. Caching the factor would halve the constant, at the cost of invalidating it whenever a caller mutates covariances_. For diag and spherical the factorisation is O(d), which is why those variants scale to high dimensions. Number of iterations is usually 10–100; the demo's full fit converges in 10.

    Failure modes #

    1. Singular covariance. A component that captures one point has S_k = 0, the Cholesky fails, and the likelihood diverges. reg_covar is the floor; map_mixture and variational are the principled fixes. The demo shows the difference directly: minimum eigenvalue 0.559 (MAP) versus 0.215 (unregularised) on the same 5-component fit.
    2. Local optima. EM is a local method, and a bad seed can merge two true components and split another. n_init restarts are not optional; the demo uses 3–5 everywhere and the reference fit still needs 5 to reach 0.995 accuracy.
    3. Choosing K. The likelihood is non-decreasing in K, so it cannot select the model. BIC works when n is large relative to p; with n < 10p the penalty is too weak and BIC over-selects. Use AIC, cross-validated likelihood, or a Dirichlet prior instead.
    4. Label switching. Component indices are arbitrary, so means_[0] from two runs need not be the same cluster. Compare fitted models by likelihood or by a permutation-invariant metric; never compare labels_ directly.
    5. Identifiability. Two components with the same mean and covariance are indistinguishable; the likelihood is flat in that direction and the optimiser wanders. The Dirichlet prior in variational breaks the tie by shrinking one weight.
    6. diag on correlated features. The model cannot represent rotation, so it approximates an elongated cluster with a staircase of axis-aligned ones. It is a bias-variance trade, not a bug — but check whether full is affordable before accepting it.
    7. tied when variances differ. One shared covariance forces equal spread. On data where one component is tight and another is wide, tied will split the wide one to compensate. The demo's shared-covariance data is the case where it is correct.
    8. Initialisation with far outliers. k-means++ seeding can place a centre on an outlier, and a component that starts there may never recover. Trim or winsorise before fitting, or use a robust mixture (t-distribution components).
    9. bic_selection cost. It fits k_max models with n_init restarts each, so the cost is k_max·n_init times a single fit. On large n that dominates; use a warm start from the K−1 solution, which is what production libraries do.
    10. Cholesky failure in predict. If covariances_ is mutated after fitting (for example by a user shrinking it), _log_gauss raises LinAlgError. Re-fit or call _project_cov before predicting.

    Applications #

    • Density estimation and anomaly detection. score is a log-density; a point in the bottom percentile of score is an outlier. This is the model behind many network-intrusion and fraud detectors.
    • Soft clustering. The responsibilities give a graded membership, which is what topic models, customer segmentation and speaker diarisation need — a document can be 70% sports and 30% finance.
    • Speaker recognition. Each speaker is a GMM over MFCC frames; the classical UBM–MAP pipeline is exactly the map_mixture update with a universal background model as the prior.
    • Image segmentation and background modelling. Pixel colours or features as a mixture; spherical and diag are the usual choices for speed.
    • Missing-data imputation. Fit the mixture on complete rows, then impute each missing value by its conditional expectation under the mixture posterior.
    • As a generative classifier. One GMM per class and a class prior gives p(y|x) ∝ π_y p(x|y), which is quadratic discriminant analysis when each class is a single Gaussian.
    • Initialisation for larger models. A fitted GMM is the standard init for normalising flows, VAEs' priors, and HMM emission models.
