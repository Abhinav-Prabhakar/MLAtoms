    07 · Clustering/tier 1/8 variants/source

    Agglomerative clustering

    Intuition #

    Divisive methods start with everything and split. Agglomerative methods do the opposite: every point is its own cluster, and the two closest clusters are merged, over and over, until one remains. The sequence of merges is the answer — a tree (a dendrogram) whose leaves are points and whose internal nodes are merges, each labelled with the distance at which it happened. Cutting the tree at any height gives a flat clustering, so one run answers every K.

    Everything interesting is in the phrase "closest". Two clusters have many point pairs between them, and the linkage rule decides which of those distances counts:

    • single uses the closest pair, so a thin chain of points is enough to join two dense blobs. That is chaining: sometimes a feature (a filament, a coastline), sometimes a bug.
    • complete uses the farthest pair, so an elongated cluster is expensive to join and the result is compact and roughly equal-sized.
    • average uses the mean over all cross pairs, which is the compromise and the phylogenetic standard (UPGMA).
    • ward merges the pair whose union increases the total within-cluster sum of squares the least — the "keep the variance low" rule.

    On the demo's two blobs joined by twelve collinear bridge points, single linkage joins them at height 0.44 and complete linkage only at 7.45: same data, same greedy loop, two completely different clusters. That gap is the whole reason the linkage rule exists.

    The other two variants answer different questions. connectivity_constrained restricts merges to edges of a k-NN graph, so clusters must be connected regions rather than merely close ones. cut_height replaces "how many clusters?" with "how far apart?", and lets small leftover groups become noise.

    Variants #

    Variant Merge distance Monotone Character
    single min over cross pairs yes chains, arbitrary shapes, K from gaps
    complete max over cross pairs yes compact, equal-sized, sensitive to outliers
    average mean over cross pairs yes size-weighted compromise, UPGMA
    ward least increase in within-cluster SSE yes balanced, variance-minimising
    centroid distance between centroids no size-weighted centre distance
    median distance between centroids, unweighted no WPGMC, no size bias
    connectivity_constrained ward, only along a mutual k-NN graph yes connected regions, manifold-shaped
    cut_height single, stopped at height yes discovers K, marks noise

    All six linkages are the same loop with different Lance–Williams coefficients, so the file has one merge implementation and a six-branch distance update. The three "geometric" ones (ward, centroid, median) run on squared distances and report a square root as the merge height; the three "set" ones (single, complete, average) run on plain distances.

    The differences that matter in practice:

    • single and complete are the two extremes, and both are exact: they are the minimum spanning tree (single) and the maximum spanning tree (complete) read as a hierarchy. Everything between them is a compromise, which is what average is.
    • ward is the one to reach for on compact blobs, and the demo shows why: on unequal blobs it produces the lowest within-cluster spread (1.36 against 1.96 for centroid). It is also the one that can fail badly on unequal cluster sizes, because its cost n_A n_B/(n_A+n_B) grows with balance: the same demo's ward accuracy is 0.722 while centroid gets 0.922. Ward prefers balanced clusters, so it will split a large true cluster to join a small one.
    • centroid and median are the ones that can invert. A merge that moves a centroid toward an unrelated cluster can make the next merge cheaper than the previous one, so merge heights are not monotone. On the chain data that happens 7 times for centroid and 3 times for median, and zero times for the other four. An inverted hierarchy cannot be drawn as a tree with increasing heights, and cutting it at a height is meaningless — the code still reports n_inversions_ so you notice.
    • connectivity_constrained keeps ward's merge rule but only allows merges along a mutual k-NN graph. On two moons, plain ward cuts the crescents arbitrarily (0.770) because "close" is not the same as "connected"; the constrained version scores 1.000 and never has to merge across the gap between the manifolds.
    • cut_height ignores K entirely: merges stop when the next one is above height. On three blobs with uniform background, a cut at 0.5 returns exactly the three blobs plus 8 noise points (min_cluster_size=20 deletes the singletons); a cut at 5.0 returns one cluster. It is DBSCAN's idea built out of a dendrogram.

    Derivation #

    The greedy loop. Start with n singletons and a symmetric distance matrix. Merge the pair with the smallest distance, replace the two rows by one, repeat n−1 times. The number of merges is fixed, so the only freedom is which two clusters are closest — which is the linkage rule.

    Lance–Williams. Recomputing d(A∪B, C) from scratch costs O(|A||B|) per pair. The Lance–Williams recursion does it from the three existing entries in O(1):

    \[d(A\cup B, C) = \alpha_A\, d(A,C) + \alpha_B\, d(B,C) + \beta\, d(A,B) + \gamma\,\bigl|d(A,C) - d(B,C)\bigr|\]

    The identities max(x,y) = ½(x+y) + ½|x−y| and min(x,y) = ½(x+y) − ½|x−y| give

    Linkage α_A α_B β γ
    single ½ ½ 0 −½
    complete ½ ½ 0 +½
    average n_A/(n_A+n_B) n_B/(n_A+n_B) 0 0
    ward (n_A+n_C)/T (n_B+n_C)/T −n_C/T 0
    centroid n_A/(n_A+n_B) n_B/(n_A+n_B) −n_A n_B/(n_A+n_B)² 0
    median ½ ½ −¼ 0

    with T = n_A + n_B + n_C. Single and complete are the min/max identities applied to d(A,C) and d(B,C) — the β·d(A,B) term is zero because the new cluster's own diameter does not affect its distance to a third cluster.

    Average (UPGMA). The mean over all cross pairs:

    \[d(A\cup B,C) = \frac{\sum_{a\in A\cup B}\sum_{c\in C} d(a,c)}{|A\cup B|\,|C|} = \frac{n_A\,d(A,C) + n_B\,d(B,C)}{n_A+n_B}\]

    The coefficients are proportions, so a large cluster's average dominates the merged distance. That is the UPGMA "size weighting": a big cluster is hard to pull apart because its internal averaging is inertially stable.

    Centroid (UPGMC). Work with squared Euclidean distances, so the singleton distances are ‖x_i − x_j‖² and a cluster is represented by its mean μ. Expanding the variance decomposition around μ_{A∪B} = (n_A μ_A + n_B μ_B)/(n_A+n_B):

    \[\|\mu_{A\cup B} - \mu_C\|^2 = \frac{n_A\|\mu_A-\mu_C\|^2 + n_B\|\mu_B-\mu_C\|^2}{n_A+n_B} - \frac{n_A n_B}{(n_A+n_B)^2}\|\mu_A-\mu_B\|^2\]

    which is the centroid row above. The negative β is the "centroid moved toward C" correction, and it is exactly the term that allows inversions.

    Median (WPGMC). Replace the weighted mean by the unweighted midpoint (μ_A + μ_B)/2. The same expansion with ½ weights gives α = ½, β = −¼, and the merged cluster's size no longer enters the update. That removes the size weighting but not the non-monotonicity: the midpoint can still jump toward C. The difference on unequal data is visible in the demo — median differs from centroid while both stay non-monotone.

    Ward. Ward's objective is the total within-cluster sum of squares, SSE = Σ_c Σ_{x∈c} ‖x − μ_c‖², and each greedy step merges the pair that increases it the least. For a union,

    \[\Delta(A,B) = \mathrm{SSE}(A\cup B) - \mathrm{SSE}(A) - \mathrm{SSE}(B) = \frac{n_A n_B}{n_A+n_B}\,\|\mu_A-\mu_B\|^2\]

    Because Δ ≥ 0, Ward is automatically monotone. The stored distance is 2Δ = 2 n_A n_B/(n_A+n_B)·‖μ_A − μ_B‖², and it satisfies the Lance–Williams recursion with the coefficients in the table (this is the standard derivation; substituting the centroid identity above and multiplying by 2(n_A+n_B)n_C/(n_A+n_B+n_C) produces it term by term). The merge height reported is √(2Δ), matching the convention of scipy.cluster.hierarchy.

    The cost n_A n_B/(n_A+n_B) is maximised at n_A = n_B for fixed total, which is the formal statement of Ward's balance bias: for a fixed centroid separation, merging two medium clusters costs more than absorbing a small cluster into a large one, so Ward prefers to keep sizes similar — even when the truth is lopsided.

    Why single and complete are MSTs. At threshold d, single-linkage clusters are the connected components of the graph with edges of weight ≤ d. Kruskal's algorithm builds exactly those components while adding edges in increasing order, so single linkage is Kruskal on the complete graph — equivalently, the minimum spanning tree read as a dendrogram. Complete linkage is the same argument with the maximum spanning tree. That is why SLINK and CLINK can do in O(n²) time what the naive loop does in O(n³): they never look at all pairs again.

    Connectivity constraint. Let G be a graph on the points (here: the mutual k-NN graph). A constrained agglomerative clustering only merges A and B when some edge of G crosses between them, and after merging, the new cluster inherits the union of its parents' edges. Clusters are then connected subgraphs of G by construction, which is the discrete analogue of "a cluster is a connected region of the data manifold". The rule is soft: when no allowed pair remains the code falls back to the unrestricted minimum, so a disconnected G still produces a complete hierarchy instead of deadlocking.

    Cutting. For a monotone hierarchy the merges happen at non-decreasing heights, so "stop after n − k merges" and "cut at the k-th smallest height" are the same thing, and every cut yields a well-defined partition. cut_height uses the height form: it stops as soon as the next merge exceeds height, then drops clusters smaller than min_cluster_size to noise. A dendrogram for the demo's chained data looks like

    height
 7.45 |                              ┌──────── both blobs ────────┐   complete
 0.44 |  ┌──── blob A ────┐── chain ──┌──── blob B ────┐              single
 0.00 |  ·  ·  ·  ·  ·  ·  ·  ·  ·  ·  ·  ·  ·  ·  ·  ·

    The same picture under single linkage collapses to one long chain: the first merge above the chain's spacing already contains every point.

    Code walkthrough #

    All six linkages are one function of (name, d_i, d_j, d_ij, n_i, n_j, n_k), vectorised over every live cluster k:

            if name == "single":
            return 0.5 * di + 0.5 * dj - 0.5 * np.abs(di - dj)
        if name == "complete":
            return 0.5 * di + 0.5 * dj + 0.5 * np.abs(di - dj)
        if name == "average":
            w = ni + nj
            return (ni * di + nj * dj) / w
        if name == "ward":
            w = ni + nj + nk
            return ((ni + nk) * di + (nj + nk) * dj - nk * dij) / w
        if name == "centroid":
            w = ni + nj
            return (ni * di + nj * dj) / w - (ni * nj / (w * w)) * dij
        if name == "median":
            return 0.5 * di + 0.5 * dj - 0.25 * dij
        raise ValueError(name)

    di and dj are entire rows (d(A,C) and d(B,C) for every live C), so one call rewrites the whole merged row — no Python loop over clusters. dij is a scalar, the distance being consumed by this merge. nk is an array, so ni + nk broadcasts; that is what makes ward and centroid size-weighted while median is not. The two abs forms are the min/max identities; writing np.minimum(di, dj) would be equivalent and marginally slower.

    Whether the working numbers are distances or squared distances is decided once, at the top of fit:

            working = d * d if self.linkage in SQUARED else d

        cap = 2 * n - 1
        dist = np.full((cap, cap), np.inf)
        dist[:n, :n] = working
        np.fill_diagonal(dist, np.inf)

    SQUARED is {ward, centroid, median} — the three whose algebra is derived on squared distances. Squaring single/complete would be harmless (min and max commute with x²) but squaring average would not: the mean of squares is not the square of the mean, and the linkage would silently become something else. cap = 2n − 1 is the number of nodes in the full dendrogram, so cluster n + m is the result of merge m and no index arithmetic is needed. Pre-filling with inf and setting the diagonal to inf means an argmin over the matrix can never pick a dead row or a self-distance.

    The merge loop is the naive O(n³) version, which is the clearest form:

            for _ in range(n - 1):
            live = alive[:, None] & alive[None, :]
            cand = np.where(live, dist, np.inf)
            if self.variant == "connectivity_constrained":
                cand = np.where(allow, cand, np.inf)
            if not np.isfinite(cand).any():
                cand = np.where(live, dist, np.inf)   # drop the constraint, keep going
            i, j = divmod(int(np.argmin(cand)), cap)
            if i > j:
                i, j = j, i
            value = float(cand[i, j])
            h = self._height(value)

    live masks the matrix down to pairs of live clusters and np.argmin on the flattened array gives the pair; divmod recovers (i, j) from the flat index because the matrix is cap × cap. The if i > j swap makes the pair canonical, which keeps children_ and the label propagation deterministic. The not np.isfinite(...).any() check is the constraint's escape hatch: a disconnected k-NN graph would otherwise leave the loop with nothing to merge and no way to finish the hierarchy, so the restriction is dropped rather than raising.

    The update of the new row is one Lance–Williams call plus a symmetric write:

                ks = np.nonzero(alive)[0]
            ks = ks[(ks != i) & (ks != j)]
            new = self._lance_williams(
                self.linkage, dist[i, ks], dist[j, ks], value,
                float(size[i]), float(size[j]), size[ks].astype(float))
            dist[nxt, ks] = new
            dist[ks, nxt] = new

    ks is selected before i and j are marked dead, so the filter has to exclude them by hand. Writing both dist[nxt, ks] and dist[ks, nxt] keeps the matrix symmetric, which matters because the next iteration's argmin scans both triangles. value (not dist[i, j]) is passed as dij because under cut_height the loop may break before the merge, and the height conversion happened already; passing the raw entry would be the same number here but would break if a threshold ever modified it.

    The constraint graph is a mutual k-NN graph, and the constraint is propagated by union:

            idx = np.argpartition(d, k, axis=1)[:, :k + 1]
        adj = np.zeros((n, n), dtype=bool)
        adj[np.arange(n)[:, None], idx] = True
        adj &= adj.T
        np.fill_diagonal(adj, False)

    argpartition picks the k nearest without sorting, and k+1 columns include the point itself, which fill_diagonal then removes. The single character that matters is &= rather than |=: a mutual edge needs both endpoints to name each other, so a point sitting near a different manifold cannot drag its cluster across on its own. The union graph is the looser alternative and gives visibly worse results on the moons.

    After a merge, the new cluster's allowed set is the union of its parents' sets:

                if self.variant == "connectivity_constrained":
                link = allow[i, ks] | allow[j, ks]
                allow[nxt, ks] = link
                allow[ks, nxt] = link

    | here, not &: once A and B are one cluster, an edge from A to C is an edge from A∪B to C. Using & would require both parents to touch C, which shrinks the graph with every merge until nothing can merge at all.

    The flat labels come from replaying merges into a union-find:

            for idx, (a, b, _, _) in enumerate(merges):
            if idx >= keep:
                break
            ra, rb = find(a), find(b)
            parent[ra] = n + idx
            parent[rb] = n + idx

        roots = np.array([find(p) for p in range(n)])

    Stopping at keep merges is the cut: n − k for a fixed k, or every performed merge for cut_height. parent[ra] = n + idx records that merge idx created node n + idx, so find on any leaf walks up to the last merge that contained it. Path halving in find keeps the walk short — the tree can be a caterpillar with n levels, and without compression the label step would be quadratic. After the roots are known, sizes come from np.bincount and clusters smaller than min_cluster_size become -1.

    The height reported for the squared linkages is undone at the last moment:

        def _height(self, value: float) -> float:
        return float(np.sqrt(max(value, 0.0))) if self.linkage in SQUARED else float(value)

    max(value, 0.0) is not paranoia: centroid and median have negative β, so a merged distance can come out slightly below zero from rounding, and np.sqrt of a negative number is nan. Returning the square root keeps distances_ in the same units as the input, which is what makes the demo's 0.44 versus 7.45 comparison meaningful.

    Complexity #

    Step Time Memory
    Initial distance matrix O(n² d) O(n²)
    One merge: argmin over the matrix O(n²) —
    n−1 merges O(n³) O(n²)
    Lance–Williams update O(n) per merge —
    Labels O(n α(n)) O(n)
    n_clusters variants, all linkages same loop, different constants same
    Constraint graph O(n²) + O(n k) O(n²)

    The O(n³) is the cost of rescanning the whole matrix for each merge, and it is why real libraries do not do this. Nearest-neighbour-chain (nn_chain) reduces it to O(n²) in time and memory; a priority queue of candidate pairs gives O(n² log n); SLINK and CLINK achieve O(n²) time with O(n) memory for the two extreme linkages. All of them change the bookkeeping, not the result. The memory is the other wall: the distance matrix is 8n² bytes, so n = 50k needs 20 GB. For larger data the usual answers are sparse constraints (only merge along a k-NN graph, O(nk) edges) or a subsample.

    Failure modes #

    1. Chaining under single. One bridge of points merges two dense blobs at a tiny height — the demo's 0.44 versus 7.45. Real causes: a stray line of points, a duplicated axis, or a genuinely filamentary cluster. Use average/complete, or a connectivity constraint, and inspect the merge heights rather than only the labels.
    2. Ward's balance bias. Ward prefers equal-sized clusters, so on a 300/20 split it scores 0.722 while centroid scores 0.922. If cluster sizes are very unequal and the small ones matter, Ward is the wrong default.
    3. Inversions in centroid/median. Merge heights can decrease, so the "tree" has crossing branches and a height cut is ambiguous. The demo counts 7 and 3 inversions against 0 for the monotone rules. If you need a threshold, do not use these two linkages.
    4. Greedy irreversibility. A merge is never undone, so an early mistake propagates upward. The classic case is complete linkage merging two outlier points into a spurious cluster that then attracts everything else. There is no restart and no objective that later repairs it.
    5. K is a choice, not a result. n_clusters decides where to cut, and different cuts of the same tree are all "valid". Use the merge-height gap (largest jump in distances_), a silhouette scan, or domain knowledge — the tree only tells you the candidate cuts.
    6. No noise model. Every point ends in a cluster, including outliers, which is why complete linkage's accuracy collapses on data with background points. Only cut_height with min_cluster_size can say "this is noise"; single/complete/average cannot.
    7. Feature scale. All linkages use Euclidean distance, so a feature measured in thousands dominates one measured in fractions. Standardise, or use a domain metric — the algorithm itself has no notion of units.
    8. average's size weighting. The n_A/(n_A+n_B) coefficients mean a large cluster barely moves when it absorbs a point, so UPGMA tends to produce one big cluster plus satellites rather than balanced ones.
    9. Disconnected constraint graph. The mutual k-NN graph is not always connected, and the fallback silently drops the constraint and merges across the gap. Check that the graph has one component (or accept that the hierarchy is partly unconstrained); the n_neighbors value controls how likely this is.
    10. Quadratic memory. O(n²) floats is the practical ceiling, well before the O(n³) time becomes painful. Use k-NN constraints, sparse linkage algorithms, or cluster a subsample and assign the rest to the nearest centroid.

    Applications #

    • Phylogenetics and taxonomy. UPGMA (average) on genetic distances is the classical tree-building method, and single/complete correspond to the minimax and maximum distance criteria. The dendrogram is the deliverable.
    • Document and topic hierarchies. Agglomerative clustering over TF-IDF or embedding vectors gives a browsable tree, which is more useful than a flat partition when the consumer is a human looking for the right level of granularity.
    • Genomics and single-cell data. Hierarchical clustering of expression profiles with Ward linkage and a correlation-derived distance is standard; the dendrogram's ordering is used to permute the heatmap.
    • Image segmentation. Region merging on a graph of superpixels, with a connectivity constraint so segments stay spatially contiguous.
    • Anomaly-adjacent workflows. Cutting low and marking small clusters as noise (cut_height) gives a cheap alternative to DBSCAN when a hierarchy is wanted anyway.
    • Customer and product taxonomies. Ward on standardised behavioural features, cut at several heights to get segments, sub-segments and micro-segments from one fit.
    • As a sanity check for K. Running single and complete on the same data and comparing the top merge heights says a lot about whether the clusters are genuinely separated or just an artefact of one linkage's bias.
