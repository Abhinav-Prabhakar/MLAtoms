    07 · Clustering/tier 1/5 variants/source

    Spectral clustering

    Intuition #

    k-means and GMMs both assume that a cluster is a blob around a centre. Spectral clustering assumes something weaker: points that belong together are connected by many short paths, and points in different clusters are connected by few long ones. All the geometry lives in a graph, and the clustering is a graph cut.

    The construction is:

    1. Build an affinity W, large when two points are similar. Here it is a k-NN graph with RBF weights.
    2. Form the graph Laplacian L = D − W, where D is the diagonal of weighted degrees.
    3. Take the k eigenvectors of L belonging to its smallest eigenvalues. Each row of that n×k matrix is a new coordinate vector for one point.
    4. Run k-means on those coordinates.

    Step 3 is the trick. Minimising the cut between two groups is NP-hard, but the relaxed version is an eigenproblem, and its solution embeds the graph so that a good cut becomes a small distance in R^k. On the demo's two interleaving moons, k-means on the raw coordinates scores 0.793 because the moons are not linearly separable, while spectral clustering on the same coordinates scores 1.000 — the graph already knows which points are neighbours along each moon.

    The variants differ in which Laplacian is used, how the embedding is normalised, how the affinity scale is chosen, and how k clusters are extracted.

    Variants #

    Variant Laplacian Embedding Why it exists
    normalized L_rw = I − D⁻¹W rows of V = D^{-1/2}U Shi–Malik; the standard choice
    unnormalized L = D − W rows of U the original; minimises RatioCut
    ng_jordan_weiss L_sym = I − D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2} U with rows scaled to unit norm Ng–Jordan–Weiss; best-conditioned
    self_tuning L_sym on a locally scaled affinity unit rows no global σ to choose
    multiway L_sym on each subgraph Fiedler vector, recursively avoids k-means entirely

    The first three are the same algorithm with different normalisations, and on easy data they agree; the demo's circles give 1.000 for both unnormalized and ng_jordan_weiss. Their differences show up in the quality of the relaxation:

    • unnormalized minimises the ratio cut, which divides by the number of points in each side. It therefore prefers splitting off small groups, which is a bias toward unbalanced clusters.
    • normalized minimises the normalised cut, which divides by the volume of each side. It is scale-free and prefers balanced clusters.
    • ng_jordan_weiss uses the symmetric Laplacian and then projects each row onto the unit sphere. The row normalisation removes the effect of the degree from the embedding, so k-means sees directions rather than magnitudes; it is the most numerically robust of the three and the one to reach for by default.

    self_tuning replaces the single σ by a per-point scale σ_i (the distance to the i-th neighbour) and uses exp(−d²/(σ_iσ_j)). On the moons, a full RBF graph with σ = 1 scores 0.777, because one global width that large blurs the two moons into a single dense graph; local scaling scores 1.000 with no width parameter at all.

    multiway never runs k-means. It splits the graph in two using the Fiedler vector (the eigenvector of the second-smallest eigenvalue), then recursively splits the largest piece until there are k clusters. Three concentric rings come out as 1.000 after exactly 2 Fiedler solves for k = 3.

    Derivation #

    The cut objective. For a partition of the vertices into A and Ā, the cut is

    \[\text{cut}(A,\bar A) = \sum_{i\in A,\,j\in\bar A} W_{ij}\]

    Minimising cut alone has a trivial optimum: isolate one vertex. The two standard fixes are

    \[\text{RatioCut}(A,\bar A) = \frac{\text{cut}(A,\bar A)}{|A|} + \frac{\text{cut}(A,\bar A)}{|\bar A|}, \qquad \text{Ncut}(A,\bar A) = \frac{\text{cut}(A,\bar A)}{\text{vol}(A)} + \frac{\text{cut}(A,\bar A)}{\text{vol}(\bar A)}\]

    with vol(A) = Σ_{i∈A} d_i.

    Relaxation. Encode the partition by an indicator vector f ∈ {−1,+1}ⁿ (or {0,1}). For the unnormalised case,

    \[f^\top L f = \tfrac12\sum_{i,j} W_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2 = \text{cut}(A,\bar A)\cdot 4\]

    because (f_i − f_j)² = 4 exactly when the pair straddles the cut. So minimising fᵀLf subject to fᵀ1 = 0 (balanced sizes) and ‖f‖ = √n is RatioCut in relaxed form. L is symmetric positive semi-definite with 1 as its eigenvector for eigenvalue 0, so the minimum over the orthogonal complement is the second smallest eigenvalue, attained at the Fiedler vector. Dropping the integrality of f is the relaxation; rounding the Fiedler vector's sign is the rounding step.

    For Ncut the substitution g = D^{1/2}f gives

    \[\min_f \frac{f^\top L f}{f^\top D f} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \min_g \frac{g^\top D^{-1/2} L D^{-1/2} g}{g^\top g}\]

    so the relaxed Ncut solution is the second eigenvector of the normalised Laplacian L_sym = I − D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}. The random-walk form is similar: L_rw = D⁻¹L = I − D⁻¹W has the same eigenvalues as L_sym, and its eigenvectors are v = D^{-1/2}u. Working with L_rw and then normalising rows by D^{-1/2} is the Shi–Malik recipe.

    Why k eigenvectors for k clusters. The same argument applies to a k-way cut with an n×k indicator matrix F whose columns are the cluster indicators. The relaxation is min tr(FᵀLF) subject to FᵀF = I, whose solution is the matrix of the k smallest eigenvectors. k-means on the rows is a discrete approximation of that solution: it finds k centroids in R^k, i.e. an n×k indicator matrix of the required form. That is why the algorithm is "spectral embedding, then k-means" rather than "spectral cut, then rounding".

    Row normalisation (NJW). The theorem in Ng–Jordan–Weiss is about the matrix U of the first k eigenvectors of L_sym. Their algorithm normalises each row to unit norm before k-means, which makes the embedding invariant to the degree and turns the problem into clustering directions. The theoretical guarantee is stated for that normalised matrix: under a model with k well-separated clusters, the rows of T = U/‖U‖ are close to k orthogonal vectors, so k-means recovers the partition.

    Local scaling. Zelnik-Manor and Perona's affinity is

    \[W_{ij} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{d^2(x_i,x_j)}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}\right), \qquad \sigma_i = d(x_i, x_{K_i})\]

    The product σ_iσ_j is the geometric mean of the two local scales, so a pair inside a dense region is compared at a short scale and a pair inside a sparse region at a long one. The affinity between a dense and a sparse point is the geometric mean of the two, which is exactly the regime where a global σ fails: too small and the sparse region disconnects, too large and the dense region merges with everything.

    Multiway / Fiedler splitting. For a subgraph with Laplacian L_sym, the Fiedler vector v₂ minimises the relaxed Ncut of that subgraph, and its sign gives the split. Recursion approximates the k-way optimum by k−1 binary decisions, which is cheaper than a full k-eigenvector decomposition on a large graph (O(m log n) per split with a Lanczos solver) and produces a hierarchy as a by-product.

    Code walkthrough #

    The affinity is a k-NN mask over an RBF kernel, with the mask taking the union of both directions:

            idx = np.argpartition(d2, k, axis=1)[:, :k + 1]
        mask = np.zeros((n, n), dtype=bool)
        mask[np.arange(n)[:, None], idx] = True
        mask |= mask.T
        np.fill_diagonal(mask, False)

    argpartition finds the k nearest without sorting, and k+1 columns include the point itself. mask |= mask.T symmetrises the graph, not just the weights: if i chose j but j did not choose i, the edge still exists. That is the standard "union" k-NN graph, and it matters for spectral clustering because a one-sided edge would make W asymmetric and the Laplacian's eigenvectors complex. fill_diagonal(False) removes self-loops, which would only shift every degree by one.

    The self-tuning branch needs the distance to the local-scale neighbour, not the graph neighbour:

                order = np.argsort(d2, axis=1)
            scale = np.sqrt(np.maximum(
                d2[np.arange(n), order[:, self.local_scale_neighbors]], EPS))
            denom = scale[:, None] * scale[None, :]
            W = np.exp(-d2 / np.maximum(denom, EPS))

    order[:, j] is the index of the j-th nearest point for each row, and taking d2 at that index gives the squared distance without a second sort. The outer product scale[:, None] * scale[None, :] is the σ_iσ_j in one expression — no loop over pairs. full_graph is forced on for this variant because the local scales are what make a dense affinity well behaved; sparsifying it would throw away the long-range edges that survive the scaling.

    The Laplacians are three lines each because eigh does the work:

            dinv = 1.0 / np.sqrt(np.maximum(d, EPS))
        L = np.eye(n) - dinv[:, None] * W * dinv[None, :]
        vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(L)

    dinv[:, None] * W * dinv[None, :] is D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2} as two broadcast multiplies, never a matrix product with a diagonal matrix. np.linalg.eigh is the symmetric eigensolver: it returns eigenvalues in ascending order, so vecs[:, :k] is already the k smallest. np.maximum(d, EPS) protects isolated vertices — a point with no edges has degree 0, and without the guard the embedding row would be nan and poison k-means.

    The random-walk embedding is a rescale of the symmetric one:

            V = dinv[:, None] * vecs[:, :k]
        return vals, V

    Because L_rw = D^{-1/2}L_symD^{1/2}, its eigenvectors are D^{-1/2}u. This is exactly why normalized and ng_jordan_weiss differ only in the row scaling: they use the same eigendecomposition, and the variant decides whether rows are left as V or normalised to unit length.

    The Fiedler solve for a subgraph reuses the same construction on the principal submatrix:

            sub = W[np.ix_(idx, idx)]
        d = sub.sum(axis=1)
        dinv = 1.0 / np.sqrt(np.maximum(d, EPS))
        L = np.eye(idx.size) - dinv[:, None] * sub * dinv[None, :]
        _, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(L)
        self.n_eig_ += 1
        return dinv * vecs[:, 1] if idx.size > 1 else vecs[:, 0]

    np.ix_(idx, idx) extracts the principal submatrix in one operation, which is the whole "run the algorithm on a subset" step. vecs[:, 1] is the second eigenvector because the first is constant. The dinv * converts it to the random-walk eigenvector, which is the correct object for the normalised cut; using the symmetric eigenvector directly would still split reasonably but would bias the split toward low-degree vertices.

    The recursion always splits the biggest cluster:

            while len(clusters) < self.k:
            j = max(range(len(clusters)), key=lambda i: clusters[i].size)
            idx = clusters.pop(j)
            v = self._fiedler(W, idx)
            left = v > 0.0
            if left.all() or not left.any():
                left = v > float(np.median(v))

    Splitting the largest cluster balances the recursion tree and is what makes "k−1 splits" meaningful — splitting the smallest would spend solves on already-small pieces. The sign test v > 0 fails when the vector is one-signed, which happens on a connected subgraph whose Fiedler value is tiny; the median fallback guarantees a non-empty split and keeps the loop from spinning. A truly unsplittable cluster (idx.size < 2) is put back and the loop breaks, so k is an upper bound rather than a promise.

    The main dispatch is three lines:

            if self.variant == "multiway":
            self.labels_ = self._multiway(self.affinity_)
            self.embedding_ = None
            self.eigenvalues_ = None
        else:
            self.eigenvalues_, self.embedding_ = self._laplacian_eigen(
                self.affinity_, self.k)

    Setting embedding_ and eigenvalues_ to None for multiway is deliberate: there is no single embedding, and returning a stale one from a previous fit would be worse than returning nothing.

    k-means on the embedding is a sibling import, not a reimplementation:

                km = KMeans("lloyd", n_clusters=self.k, n_init=10,
                        random_state=int(self.rng.integers(2**31 - 1)))
            self.labels_ = km.fit_predict(self.embedding_)

    n_init=10 because the embedding can have clusters of very different sizes, and a single Lloyd run on a bad seed merges two of them. The RNG is seeded from this model's generator so the whole pipeline is reproducible from random_state alone.

    Complexity #

    Step Time Memory
    Affinity matrix O(n² d ) (dense) O(n²)
    k-NN selection O(n²) with argpartition O(n²)
    Eigendecomposition O(n³) dense eigh O(n²)
    k-means on n×k O(n k² i) O(n k)
    Multiway, k−1 splits O(k n³) worst case O(n²)

    The dense eigendecomposition is the wall: O(n³) with a small constant, fine to a few thousand points and hopeless beyond that. Real implementations never build W: they use a sparse k-NN graph and a Lanczos/LOBPCG solver for the few smallest eigenvectors, which is O(m k) per iteration with m ≈ n·n_neighbors edges. The affinity matrix is also the memory wall: n = 100k needs 80 GB. multiway looks worse in the worst case but each sub-solve is on a smaller matrix, so in practice it is competitive and uses less peak memory.

    Failure modes #

    1. Choosing k. The eigengap heuristic — pick k where λ_k jumps — is the standard tool, and it fails when the graph has no clear gap. The demo's moons have λ₀, λ₁ ≈ 0 and λ₂ = 0.0025: a gap, but a small absolute one. Try a few k and validate on a downstream task rather than trusting the spectrum alone.
    2. The affinity width. With a dense RBF graph, σ decides everything; on the moons, σ = 1 scores 0.777 while σ from the median neighbour distance scores 1.000. With a k-NN graph the width matters much less because the edges are fixed by the neighbour count and σ only reweights them — which is why the k-NN graph is the default here.
    3. Disconnected graphs. If the k-NN graph has more than k components, the first several eigenvalues are all zero and k-means splits arbitrary components. That is usually a correct answer (the data really is disconnected), but it is reported without warning. Check λ_{k-1} ≈ 0 and whether the clusters are meaningful.
    4. Very unbalanced cluster sizes. unnormalized minimises RatioCut, which prefers isolating small groups; on data with one dominant cluster it will carve off slivers. Use the normalised variants, which divide by volume.
    5. Degree-0 vertices. An isolated point has d = 0 and an undefined normalised Laplacian. The EPS guard gives it a zero row, so it lands wherever k-means puts the origin — usually noise. Drop isolated vertices before fitting if they matter.
    6. Outliers. A far outlier is its own connected component in the k-NN graph, so it becomes a cluster of one and consumes a slot of k. Trim first, or increase n_neighbors so the outlier attaches to something.
    7. Large n. The dense path is O(n²) memory and O(n³) time. Use a sparse graph and a Lanczos solver, or Nyström approximation; the algorithm's structure is fine, this implementation is the limit.
    8. multiway split degeneracy. A one-signed Fiedler vector on a well-connected subgraph forces the median fallback, which is an arbitrary split. The recursion then produces a cluster that does not correspond to a real cut. Prefer the k-eigenvector method when the subgraphs are not clearly separable.
    9. Sign ambiguity of eigenvectors. v and −v are both valid, so labels can flip between runs. Cluster labels must be compared with a permutation-invariant metric; the demo's accuracy helper does exactly that.

    Applications #

    • Manifold-shaped clusters. Two moons, concentric circles, spirals: spectral clustering is the standard answer when k-means visibly fails, and the demo's 1.000 vs 0.793 is the canonical comparison.
    • Image segmentation. Pixels as vertices, colour/position similarity as edges; the normalised cut is the original Shi–Malik application.
    • Speaker diarisation. Segments as vertices, similarity of speaker embeddings as weights; the number of speakers is k, and the eigengap suggests it.
    • Community detection. Graph clustering with the normalised Laplacian is the spectral counterpart of modularity methods, and it scales when the graph is sparse.
    • Constrained and semi-supervised clustering. Setting W_ij = 0 for must-not-link pairs and W_ij = large for must-link pairs turns constraints into graph edits, with no change to the algorithm.
    • As a feature map. The k eigenvectors are a non-linear embedding usable by any downstream model, not only by k-means.

    • Shi & Malik, Normalized Cuts and Image Segmentation, 2000, IEEE TPAMI — the normalised cut, the random-walk Laplacian and the algorithm this file's normalized variant follows.
    • Ng, Jordan & Weiss, On Spectral Clustering: Analysis and an Algorithm, 2002, NeurIPS — the symmetric Laplacian with row normalisation and its guarantee.
    • Zelnik-Manor & Perona, Self-Tuning Spectral Clustering, 2004, NeurIPS — local scaling and the self_tuning variant.
    • von Luxburg, A Tutorial on Spectral Clustering, 2007, Statistics and Computing — the clearest derivation of the relaxation, the three Laplacians and their equivalences.
