    Schedules

    Intuition #

    A schedule is a statement about how much you trust the current gradient. Early in training the gradient is informative but the model is far from anything good, so you take large steps (after a brief warmup). Late in training the gradient is dominated by noise, and a large step means you never settle.

    So a schedule has two jobs: get through the transient safely (warmup) and shrink the noise floor (decay). Everything else — restarts, cycles, plateau — is a bet about where the transient ends.

    Variants #

    Variant Shape Signal it uses Typical use
    constant flat nothing debugging, RL, very short runs
    step γ^(t//k) step count classic CNN training (30/60/90)
    multistep drops at milestones step count hand-tuned vision recipes
    exponential γ^t step count smooth, rarely optimal
    cosine ½(1+cos πt/T) total budget transformer default
    cosine_restarts periodic cosine cycle length SGDR, ensembling snapshots
    linear_warmup ramp to 1, then flat warmup length prefix for any other shape
    one_cycle up then cosine down to a floor total budget fast single-run training
    cyclical triangle cycle length exploring several minima
    plateau ×γ when the metric stalls validation metric classical, small-data
    polynomial (1−t/T)^p total budget BERT-style, p=1
    inv_sqrt 1/√t step count the SGD theory default
    wsd flat then 1−√ decay total budget LLM pretraining

    Derivation #

    Why 1/√t is the theoretical default. For stochastic convex optimisation with O(1/√T) convergence, the optimal averaged iterate uses η_t ∝ 1/√t. The regret bound of online gradient descent is O(GD√T) with η = D/(G√T), and the anytime version is η_t = η₀/√t. Cosine and step decays are heuristics that beat this in practice because deep learning is not convex; 1/√t remains the shape to compare against.

    Why warmup. Two separate reasons, and conflating them causes bugs:

    1. Adam's variance estimate is unreliable for the first few steps. v̂_t is computed from t samples; its reciprocal is biased upward, so the first adaptive steps are too large. Bias correction helps but cannot fix the variance.
    2. At initialisation the loss surface is often sharp. With random weights, the largest curvature can be orders of magnitude above the bulk, so the stability threshold 2/L is tiny. Warmup keeps η below it until the iterate moves into a flatter region.

    linear_warmup here is a prefix: it ramps to 1 and then applies whichever shape you asked for to the remaining budget. That composition is the one thing every implementation must get right — the cosine should be computed over total − warmup, not over total.

    Cosine. η(t) = η_min + (η_max − η_min)·½(1 + cos(πt/T)). Its appeal is that it spends most of the budget near the top (the derivative is flat at t=0), then decelerates smoothly to the floor. It needs T up front, which is why t_total-dependent schedules and checkpoint resumption interact badly: resuming with a different T silently changes the whole curve.

    Restarts (SGDR). Run cosine on a cycle of length T_i, then reset to η_max. Each restart kicks the iterate out of a narrow minimum; the snapshots at cycle ends are diverse and ensemble well. T_i is usually multiplied by 2 each cycle.

    1cycle. Warm up from η_max/25 to η_max over the first 30% of the budget, then cosine-anneal to η_max/10⁴. The large initial LR acts as regularisation (Smith's "super-convergence"): the model spends the high-LR phase exploring and the long anneal phase settling. It is the fastest known recipe for a fixed compute budget and a poor choice for anything you might extend.

    WSD (warmup–stable–decay). Keep the LR flat for 80% of the run, then decay with 1−√((t−t_s)/(T−t_s)). The flat phase means any checkpoint in it is a valid model, so you can decide to stop late; the short decay recovers the quality that a cosine schedule would have reached. This is why most LLM pretraining now uses it.

    Plateau. The only non-anticipative schedule: it observes the validation metric and multiplies by γ after patience evaluations without improvement. It cannot be precomputed, it depends on the metric's noise level, and it is the right choice when you do not know T — which is the normal case for small-data problems.

    Code walkthrough #

    lr_multiplier is a pure function of the step; Plateau is a small state machine. The pure part begins with the composition rule that every implementation has to get right:

        if warmup_steps and step < warmup_steps:
        return (step + 1) / warmup_steps
    t = max(step - warmup_steps, 0)
    T = max(total_steps - warmup_steps, 1)

    Warmup is a prefix, not an alternative shape: the selected schedule is evaluated on the remaining T = total − warmup steps via the shifted t. Two details carry the meaning. The (step + 1)/warmup_steps makes step 0 return 1/warmup_steps instead of exactly zero — a zero first LR wastes a step and breaks optimisers that divide by it. And T is recomputed after the shift; a cosine over total_steps instead of total_steps − warmup_steps would finish its half-cosine early and then sit at the floor for the warmup duration.

    The discrete schedules index the global step, not the shifted t:

        if variant == "step":
        return gamma ** (step // step_size)
    if variant == "multistep":
        return gamma ** sum(1 for m in milestones if step >= m)

    That is deliberate: milestones are absolute step numbers in every published recipe ("drop at 30/60/90 epochs"), so shifting them by the warmup would silently move the drops. The continuous, budget-based schedules below use t/T, because their shape depends on how much budget remains, not on an absolute calendar.

    Cosine is written with a clamp on the ratio:

            return min_ratio + (1.0 - min_ratio) * 0.5 * (1.0 + np.cos(np.pi * min(t / T, 1.0)))

    Without min(t/T, 1.0), evaluating past the budget lets cos turn around and the LR starts increasing again — which happens whenever a run is extended past its planned total_steps. The clamp makes "past the end" mean "stay at the floor". min_ratio is what keeps the final LR non-zero; the demo checks the endpoints at 1.0 and 0.0 for the default min_ratio=0.

    cosine_restarts is the same formula on t mod period:

            period = cycle_steps or T
        t_cur = t % period
        return min_ratio + (1.0 - min_ratio) * 0.5 * (1.0 + np.cos(np.pi * t_cur / period))

    Every cycle has the same length here; SGDR's doubling (T_i = T_0·2^i) is a one-line change to period, and the demo asserts the simple version returns to exactly 1.0 at each multiple of cycle_steps.

    wsd is the LLM schedule and the shape is entirely in the branch point:

            decay_start = int(T * 0.8)
        if t < decay_start:
            return 1.0
        frac = (t - decay_start) / max(T - decay_start, 1)
        return max(1.0 - np.sqrt(frac), 0.0)

    Flat for 80%, then 1 − √frac. The max(·, 0) is needed because frac can exceed 1 by one step when T is not divisible; without it the multiplier goes negative and the optimiser climbs. The demo counts strictly decreasing steps and expects roughly 20% of T — that is the invariant the schedule is sold on.

    cyclical is a triangle, and the period is 2·cycle_steps, not cycle_steps:

            s = cycle_steps or max(step_size, 1)
        phase = (t % (2 * s)) / s
        tri = 1.0 - abs(phase - 1.0)

    At t=0, phase=0, tri=0 → min_ratio; at t=s, phase=1, tri=1 → peak; at t=2s it wraps. Using t % s instead gives a sawtooth that jumps from the peak straight back to the floor, which is a different (and much worse) schedule.

    one_cycle is two pieces joined at pct_start:

            up = int(T * pct_start)
        if t < up:
            return start + (peak - start) * (t / max(up, 1))
        frac = (t - up) / max(T - up, 1)
        return end + (peak - end) * 0.5 * (1.0 + np.cos(np.pi * frac))

    start = 1/div_factor, end = 1/final_div_factor. The first piece is linear from 1/25 to 1; the second is cosine from 1 to 1e-4. The max(up, 1) and max(T − up, 1) guard degenerate budgets (pct_start=0, or T=1), which is the kind of input a unit test uses and a real run never does.

    Plateau is the only stateful schedule:

        def step(self, metric: float) -> float:
        if metric < self.best - self.threshold:
            self.best = metric
            self.bad = 0
        else:
            self.bad += 1
            if self.bad >= self.patience:
                self.lr = max(self.lr * self.factor, self.min_lr)
                self.bad = 0
                self.reductions += 1
        return self.lr

    The strict < best − threshold means an improvement smaller than threshold counts as a stall — that is the noise filter, and setting threshold=0 makes it fire on every wobble. bad resets to zero after a reduction, so the schedule needs another full patience window before dropping again; without that reset it would collapse to min_lr in patience consecutive bad evaluations. min_lr is a floor on the multiplied value, not on the factor, so factor=0.1 can never take it below it.

    Complexity #

    O(1) time and memory per step: every schedule is a closed-form function of the step count (plateau is a few scalars of state). The real cost is that schedules constrain batching: to hit a target T you must fix the number of steps, hence the batch size and the dataset passes.

    Failure modes #

    1. Warmup shorter than the sharp phase. Loss spikes at step ~200 and never recovers. Symptom: grad norm spikes first, then the loss. Fix: longer warmup, or gradient clipping while you diagnose.
    2. Cosine to exactly zero. The final steps have η ≈ 0, which is fine for a loss but not for BatchNorm running statistics or for an EMA that needs to track a moving model. Keep a floor of 1–5% of peak.
    3. Step decay with the wrong total_steps. The drops land at the wrong place; accuracy is 1–2 points below the tuned recipe. If you change the budget, re-tune the milestones or switch to cosine.
    4. Restarts without resetting momentum. The optimiser's velocity still points into the old minimum; the first steps of the new cycle are wasted. Reset the momentum buffer at a restart.
    5. Plateau on a noisy metric. Validation loss jitters by more than threshold, so the schedule fires constantly and the LR collapses to min_lr in the first third of training. Smooth the metric or raise patience.
    6. Schedule/optimiser mismatch. one_cycle assumes SGD-like dynamics; with Adam, the peak LR is effectively multiplied again by the adaptive denominator and the run diverges. Scale the peak down by ~10× for Adam.

    Applications #

    • LLM pretraining: linear warmup (0.1–1% of steps) + cosine or WSD; η_peak from a small sweep, often 3e-4 for ~1B parameters.
    • Vision CNNs: step decay at 50/75% of the budget, or cosine; SGD + momentum.
    • Small tabular data: plateau with patience=5–10, factor=0.3, because the number of useful epochs is unknown.
    • Fine-tuning: short warmup (~5% of steps) then linear or cosine decay to a small floor; never restart from a large LR.
    • Snapshot ensembles: cosine restarts, keep the checkpoint at each cycle end.
