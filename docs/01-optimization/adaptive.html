    01 · Optimization/tier 1/7 variants/source

    Adaptive methods

    Intuition #

    Adam asked "how big have this coordinate's gradients been?" and divided by the answer. Every method here is a different answer to the same question, and the differences are not cosmetic:

    • How long is the memory? Adagrad never forgets; RMSProp forgets at rate β.
    • Mean or second moment? Centred RMSProp divides by the variance, not the raw second moment.
    • What is the increment? Yogi adds (1−β₂)|g|² only when |g|² exceeds the accumulator — an absolute, not relative, update.
    • Do you need a denominator at all? SignSGD says no: the sign is the whole normalisation.

    Variants #

    Variant Denominator State per parameter Good at Bad at
    adagrad √(Σg²) 1 sparse features, convex problems long runs (LR → 0)
    adadelta RMS[Δ] / RMS[g] 2 no LR to tune slow first steps
    rmsprop √EMA[g²] 1 RNNs, non-stationary data no bias correction
    rmsprop_centered √(EMA[g²] − EMA[g]²) 2 stochastic gradients constant gradients (denominator → 0)
    yogi √v with an additive v 2 avoiding LR blow-ups after a spike little theory beyond the original paper
    signsgd sign(g) 0 huge batches, communication-efficient training fine convergence, small batches
    sm3 min over factorised accumulators Σdᵢ giant embedding tables accuracy of the denominator

    Derivation #

    Adagrad is a regret bound. For a convex problem with G = max‖g‖, the AdaGrad regret is O(G·√T) against the best fixed point, which is optimal for online convex optimisation. The proof requires η_t = η/√t, and the accumulator Σ g² implements exactly that when gradients are stationary: after t steps, the denominator is √t·σ, so the step is η/(σ√t). The price is that the LR decays monotonically even when the problem does not need it.

    RMSProp forgets. Replace the sum with an EMA:

    \[v_t = \beta v_{t-1} + (1-\beta)g_t^2, \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\frac{g_t}{\sqrt{v_t}+\epsilon}\]

    which tracks a local scale. There is no bias correction, so the first steps are too large by 1/√(1−β) — irrelevant in practice, because warmup covers it.

    Centred RMSProp divides by the variance. v − m² estimates Var[g]; with independent noise, dividing by the variance rather than the second moment gives a step that grows as the noise shrinks. On a deterministic gradient Var[g] → 0, so the denominator collapses to ε and the step explodes — see the demo, where the centred variant travels 35× further after 100 unit steps. That is not a bug in the demonstration; it is the reason centred RMSProp is a niche choice.

    Adadelta is dimensionless. The numerator is the RMS of recent updates, the denominator the RMS of recent gradients:

    \[\Delta_t = \frac{\sqrt{E[\Delta^2]_{t-1}+\epsilon}}{\sqrt{E[g^2]_t+\epsilon}}\,g_t\]

    Both sides carry gradient units, so their ratio is scale-free: changing the units of the loss does not change the trajectory. That is why lr=1 converges in the demo, and why the first steps are tiny (the numerator starts at √ε).

    Yogi is additive. v_t = v_{t−1} + (1−β₂)\,g_t^2\,\text{sign}(g_t^2 − v_{t−1}). When gradients are large the accumulator grows by (1−β₂)g²; when they are small it shrinks by the same absolute amount, so it cannot collapse geometrically the way Adam's can. In the demo, after one large gradient followed by 200 tiny ones, Adam's v sits at 10⁻⁴ (it has adapted to the quiet regime) while Yogi's stays at 0.098 — Adam's effective LR has grown 31×, Yogi's has not.

    SignSGD. θ ← θ − η·sign(g) moves every coordinate by exactly η. For convex problems with bounded gradient noise it matches SGD's rate up to constants and is O(d) cheaper to communicate (1 bit per coordinate). It cannot converge to better than O(η) from the optimum, which is why it is used with large batches, momentum, and a decaying LR.

    SM3. A full accumulator for an m×n tensor costs mn; SM3 keeps m+n, one per mode, and sets v̂_ij = min(rowᵢ, colⱼ). Since rowᵢ = Σ_j g_ij² ≥ g_ij², the minimum is still an upper bound — a valid, slightly conservative denominator. The memory saving is the whole point: a 10⁶ × 10³ embedding table goes from 10⁹ floats to 10⁶+10³.

    Code walkthrough #

    One class, seven branches inside step. Nothing is shared except the parameter list and the optional momentum buffer, because the methods disagree about what state is needed — that is the point of the file.

    The simplest branch is SignSGD:

                if self.variant == "signsgd":
                d = np.sign(g)

    d is the direction that the rest of the method multiplies by lr. Every branch computes a d, and p -= self.lr * d at the bottom is common; keeping that shape makes the differences comparable line by line. Note np.sign(0) = 0, so a coordinate with a zero gradient freezes rather than dithering.

    Adagrad accumulates, RMSProp forgets:

                elif self.variant == "adagrad":
                self.acc[i] += g * g
                d = g / (np.sqrt(self.acc[i]) + self.eps)

                elif self.variant == "rmsprop":
                self.acc[i] = self.beta * self.acc[i] + (1 - self.beta) * g * g
                d = g / (np.sqrt(self.acc[i]) + self.eps)

    The += is what makes Adagrad's rate η/√t under a constant gradient and what makes it stall eventually; the EMA is what makes RMSProp stationary. They differ by one operator, and the demo's 2√T + ζ(½) check is what distinguishes them quantitatively rather than by eye.

    Adadelta's ordering is the algorithm:

                    self.acc[i] = self.rho * self.acc[i] + (1 - self.rho) * g * g
                rms_d = np.sqrt(self.delta[i] + self.eps)
                rms_g = np.sqrt(self.acc[i] + self.eps)
                d = (rms_d / rms_g) * g
                self.delta[i] = self.rho * self.delta[i] + (1 - self.rho) * d * d

    rms_d is read from the previous delta, before this step's d is known. That is not a bug or a lag: the method is defined with the previous update RMS, which is what makes it implicit and removes the learning rate. Updating delta before computing d (the "obvious" reordering) gives a different, unstable method.

    Centred RMSProp subtracts the squared mean and needs the clamp:

                    var = np.maximum(self.acc[i] - self.acc2[i] ** 2, 0.0)
                d = g / (np.sqrt(var) + self.eps)

    E[g²] − E[g]² is non-negative in exact arithmetic but can be −1e-17 after two EMAs, and sqrt of that is nan. The maximum(·, 0) is not defensive programming; it is required for the method to run at all. When the gradient is constant, var → 0 and d ≈ g/ε, which is the 35× overshoot the doc describes — a real property, not a numerical artefact.

    Yogi's update is written in the sign form, which is the part people misread:

                    self.acc[i] += (1 - self.beta2) * np.sign(g * g - self.acc[i]) * g * g

    If g² > v, this adds (1−β)g²; if g² < v, it subtracts (1−β)g². It is not (1−β)(g² − v) — that would be an EMA and would decay geometrically. The additive form is what keeps v near the largest recent scale instead of collapsing to the latest quiet one. The first moment is a separate EMA and is bias-corrected:

                    mhat = self.acc2[i] / (1 - self.beta ** self.t)
                vhat = self.acc[i] / (1 - self.beta2 ** self.t)
                d = mhat / (np.sqrt(vhat) + self.eps)

    acc2 is reused from the centred-RMSProp branch; the two variants are mutually exclusive, so one buffer serves both.

    SM3 is the only branch that does not have a full-size accumulator:

            accs = self.sm3[i]
        for k, acc in enumerate(accs):
            axes = tuple(ax for ax in range(g.ndim) if ax != k)
            acc += (g * g).sum(axis=axes)
        v = None
        for k, acc in enumerate(accs):
            shape = [1] * g.ndim
            shape[k] = g.shape[k]
            shaped = acc.reshape(shape)
            v = shaped if v is None else np.minimum(v, shaped)

    For a matrix, mode 0 accumulates over rows into a (cols,) vector and mode 1 into a (rows,) vector. Reducing over ax != k is the non-obvious indexing: it keeps the k-th dimension and sums everything else, so acc has length g.shape[k]. The broadcast then reshapes acc to (1, cols) or (rows, 1) and takes the elementwise minimum. Since row_i = Σ_j g_ij² ≥ g_ij², the min is still an upper bound on the true second moment — conservative, never optimistic. The memory is Σ dᵢ instead of Π dᵢ, which is the entire reason the method exists; for a 2×3 tensor the demo checks 5 < 6, and the ratio improves with every added dimension.

    Finally, momentum acts on the normalised direction, not on the raw gradient:

                if self.momentum:
                self.vel[i] = self.momentum * self.vel[i] + d
                d = self.vel[i]
            p -= self.lr * d

    For Adam-family methods this is the correct place (momentum of the adaptive update), and moving it above the branch — v ← μv + g — changes the method to one with two coupled scales. The demo does not use momentum, so this line is only exercised by a reader who passes the flag; it is still the one that decides whether "Adam with momentum" means what they think.

    Complexity #

    Variant Extra memory Time per step
    adagrad, rmsprop P O(P)
    adadelta, rmsprop_centered, yogi 2P O(P)
    signsgd 0 (or P with momentum) O(P)
    sm3 Σ dᵢ O(P) for the min and the update

    All are elementwise, so the cost is memory bandwidth. The memory column, not the time column, is what decides whether a model fits.

    Failure modes #

    1. Adagrad stalls. After 10⁵ steps the accumulated denominator is huge and the LR is effectively zero. It is a convex/online method; for deep nets use Adagrad only with a schedule that resets the accumulator, or switch to RMSProp.
    2. Centred RMSProp on clean gradients. The variance goes to zero and the step explodes. Keep a floor on the denominator, or use plain RMSProp.
    3. SignSGD with small batches. The sign of a noisy gradient is not a descent direction; the theory needs large batches or variance reduction. With batch size 8 it often oscillates instead of descending.
    4. SM3 on dense, correlated gradients. min(row, col) overestimates the per-coordinate second moment when the gradient matrix is dense, so the effective LR is smaller than intended.
    5. Mixing ε conventions. √(v+ε) vs √v+ε changes behaviour by orders of magnitude when v is tiny; pick one and keep it consistent with the optimizer state you load.

    Applications #

    • Adagrad: sparse bag-of-words, CTR models, any problem where most features appear rarely — the per-feature LR is exactly what you want and the total step count is modest.
    • RMSProp: RNNs, RL (where the value loss is non-stationary), and as the denominator inside RMSProp-style optimisers in JAX/Optax.
    • Adadelta: quickly trying a model without tuning the LR; it is a reasonable default when you have no time for a sweep.
    • SignSGD: federated learning and distributed training where upload bandwidth dominates, combined with error feedback to recover accuracy.
    • SM3: recommendation models with vocabulary in the millions, where a full Adam state would dominate memory.
