    02 · Linear models/tier 1/9 variants/source

    Generalised linear models

    Intuition #

    A linear model makes two assumptions that are usually wrong together: the mean is linear in the features, and the noise is Gaussian with constant variance. A GLM keeps the first and generalises the second:

    \[\mathbb{E}[y] = \mu = g^{-1}(x^\top\beta + \text{offset}), \qquad \mathrm{Var}[y] = \phi\,V(\mu)\]

    The link g says how the mean depends on the linear predictor; the variance function V(μ) says how the noise scales with the mean. Counts have V(μ)=μ (Poisson), positive skewed data has V(μ)=μ² (Gamma), proportions have V(μ)=μ(1−μ) (Binomial). Once those two functions are fixed, one algorithm — iteratively reweighted least squares — fits the model, and one formula gives the standard errors.

    That is the reason this is one atom and not six: Poisson, Gamma, Binomial, Tweedie and negative binomial differ only in V, and log/identity/logit differ only in g. The demo's Tweedie variant reproduces Poisson and Gamma exactly at p=1,2, which is the API proving the point.

            ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
        │  η = Xβ + offset                            │
        │      │                                      │
        │      g⁻¹                                    │
        │      ▼                                      │
        │  μ ──► V(μ), g'(μ) ──► w, z ──► WLS ──► β'  │
        │      ▲                                      │
        │      └──────────────────────────────────────┘
        └─────────────────────────────────────────────┘

    Variants #

    Variant Family Link φ The data it is for
    poisson Poisson log (canonical) fixed 1 counts, rates, event totals
    gamma Gamma log estimated positive continuous, right-skewed (cost, rainfall)
    binomial Binomial logit (canonical) fixed 1 binary outcomes, proportions
    tweedie Tweedie V=μ^p log estimated zero-inflated positive data (insurance claims)
    negative_binomial NB2 V=μ+μ²/θ log fixed 1 overdispersed counts
    log_link Gaussian log estimated positive continuous with multiplicative effects
    identity_link Gaussian identity estimated exactly OLS, as a sanity check
    offsets Poisson log fixed 1 rates with a known exposure
    dispersion quasi-Poisson log estimated counts whose SEs must survive overdispersion

    The naming is deliberately operational, in the same style as logistic_regression: poisson and gamma are families, offsets and dispersion are modelling situations, and log_link/identity_link are link choices on a Gaussian family. The table is the map; identity_link and ols agree to machine precision, and the demo asserts it.

    Derivation #

    Exponential family. Write the density as

    \[f(y;\theta,\phi) = \exp\!\left(\frac{y\theta - b(\theta)}{\phi} + c(y,\phi)\right)\]

    Then μ = b'(θ) and Var[y] = φ b''(θ) = φ V(μ). Every family above is this form with a particular b: Poisson b(θ)=e^θ, Gamma b(θ)=−log(−θ), Binomial b(θ)=log(1+e^θ), Gaussian b(θ)=θ²/2, Tweedie b(θ) given by the Jørgensen parameterisation, and NB2 as a Poisson–Gamma mixture.

    Score. With η_i = x_iᵀβ + o_i, μ_i = g^{-1}(η_i) and dμ/dη = 1/g'(μ),

    \[\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \beta} = \sum_i \frac{y_i-\mu_i}{\phi}\,\frac{1}{g'(\mu_i)}\,x_i\]

    For the canonical link (θ = η), dθ/dη = 1, so the score simplifies to

    \[\nabla\ell = \frac{1}{\phi}X^\top(y-\mu)\]

    which is zero when Xᵀ(y−μ)=0 — the residual is orthogonal to every column, exactly as in OLS. The demo checks this identity for Poisson and binomial, and the analogous Xᵀ(μ(μ−y))=0 for a Gaussian log link.

    Fisher information. The expected negative Hessian is

    \[\mathcal{I}(\beta) = \frac{1}{\phi}X^\top W X, \qquad W = \mathrm{diag}\!\left(\frac{(d\mu_i/d\eta_i)^2}{V(\mu_i)}\right) = \mathrm{diag}\!\left(\frac{1}{V(\mu_i)\,g'(\mu_i)^2}\right)\]

    IRLS. Fisher scoring is β ← β + I⁻¹∇ℓ, which rearranges into a weighted least squares problem:

    \[\beta_{\text{new}} = \arg\min_\beta \sum_i w_i\big(z_i - x_i^\top\beta - o_i\big)^2, \qquad z_i = \eta_i + (y_i-\mu_i)\,g'(\mu_i)\]

    z is the working response: a first-order Taylor expansion of g(y) around μ. For the identity link z=y and the loop converges in one step, which is why identity_link is OLS. For a canonical link IRLS is Newton's method and converges quadratically; for any other link it is Fisher scoring and converges linearly, which is why the code backtracks.

    Deviance. The fit is compared to the saturated model μ=y:

    \[D = 2\phi\big[\ell(y) - \ell(\mu)\big]\]

    which gives the four formulas in the code:

    Family D/2
    Poisson Σ [y log(y/μ) − (y−μ)]
    Gamma Σ [−log(y/μ) + (y−μ)/μ]
    Binomial Σ [y log(y/μ) + (1−y) log((1−y)/(1−μ))]
    NB2 Σ [y log(y/μ) − (y+θ) log((y+θ)/(μ+θ))]
    Gaussian Σ (y−μ)² / 2
    Tweedie p∉{1,2} Σ [y^{2−p}/((1−p)(2−p)) − yμ^{1−p}/(1−p) + μ^{2−p}/(2−p)]

    Dispersion. When φ is unknown, the Pearson estimator is

    \[\hat\phi = \frac{1}{n-\mathrm{rank}(X)}\sum_i \frac{(y_i-\mu_i)^2}{V(\mu_i)}\]

    and Cov(β̂) = φ̂ (XᵀWX)⁻¹. Poisson and binomial fix φ=1; the dispersion variant estimates it, which leaves the coefficients unchanged and multiplies every standard error by √φ̂.

    Tweedie and NB. Tweedie interpolates: p=1 is Poisson, p=2 is Gamma, and 1<p<2 is a compound Poisson–Gamma with a point mass at zero — the shape of an insurance portfolio (many zero claims, positive skewed payouts). NB2 is a Poisson whose rate is Gamma-distributed, giving V(μ)=μ+μ²/θ; θ→∞ recovers Poisson, which the demo checks to 7.5e-9.

    Why Gamma uses a log link. The canonical link is g(μ)=1/μ, which is unstable: η near zero sends μ to infinity and the working weights blow up. The log link is non-canonical but numerically tame and multiplicative, which is how cost and rate models are actually specified. That choice is why gamma is fitted by Fisher scoring with a line search rather than by pure Newton.

    Code walkthrough #

    The file is one model plus a handful of family kernels. The variant table is the only place where names are resolved:

        if variant not in VARIANTS:
        raise ValueError(f"unknown variant {variant!r}")
    family, link = {
        "poisson": ("poisson", "log"),
        "gamma": ("gamma", "log"),
        ...
        "dispersion": ("poisson", "log"),
    }[variant]
    phi_known = family in ("poisson", "binomial", "negative_binomial")
    if variant == "dispersion":
        phi_known = False

    Two things are decided here and nowhere else: which (family, link) pair the variant means, and whether φ is known. dispersion deliberately starts from Poisson and then unfixes φ, so its coefficients are identical to poisson and only the covariance changes — the demo asserts both halves of that statement.

    The stable sigmoid is the same branchwise form used in logistic_regression.py:

        out = np.empty_like(z)
    pos = z >= 0
    out[pos] = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-z[pos]))
    ez = np.exp(z[~pos])
    out[~pos] = ez / (1.0 + ez)

    1/(1+e^{-z}) is safe for z ≥ 0 and e^z/(1+e^z) for z < 0; evaluating either formula on the whole array produces inf/inf = nan in one tail, and a nan probability makes every weight and the deviance nan from the first iteration. This is not cosmetic: it is what lets the binomial demo use logits of magnitude 5 without a guard.

    The initial value is a smoothed response pushed through the link:

        mu0 = _clip_mu(0.5 * (y + float(np.mean(y))), family)
    beta = np.linalg.lstsq(Xa, _link(mu0, link) - off, rcond=None)[0]

    (y+ȳ)/2 is strictly interior for binary y (hence a finite logit) and strictly positive for counts; using y directly makes the logit infinite for every 0/1 row. The regression is on η₀ − offset, because the offset is a known additive term and must not be absorbed into β. For the identity link this start is already a scaled OLS solution, and the first IRLS step lands exactly on OLS.

    The loop body is IRLS in six lines:

            eta = Xa @ beta + off
        mu = _clip_mu(_inv_link(eta, link), family)
        gp = _link_grad(mu, link)
        w = sw / (_variance(mu, family, power, theta) * gp * gp)
        w = np.maximum(w, 0.0)
        z = eta - off + (y - mu) * gp          # working response for beta only
        sqrt_w = np.sqrt(w)
        beta_new = np.linalg.lstsq(Xa * sqrt_w[:, None], z * sqrt_w, rcond=None)[0]

    w = 1/(V·g'²) is Fisher information per observation, and z is the linearised response. The subtraction of off is the offset's whole implementation: eta contains it, but the regression being solved is for Xβ, so the target must not. Scaling rows by √w turns weighted least squares into ordinary least squares on (√w X, √w z); using lstsq instead of forming XᵀWX avoids squaring the condition number, and it returns the minimum-norm solution when the design is rank-deficient.

    Non-canonical links can overshoot, so every step is checked against the deviance:

            step = 1.0
        for _ in range(40):
            cand = beta + step * (beta_new - beta)
            mu_c = _clip_mu(_inv_link(Xa @ cand + off, link), family)
            dev_c = _deviance(y, mu_c, family, power, theta)
            if np.isfinite(dev_c) and dev_c <= dev + 1e-12 * abs(dev):
                break
            step *= 0.5
        else:
            cand, dev_c = beta, dev

    The 1e-12 * abs(dev) slack is not a fudge: an accepted full step can raise the deviance by one ulp, and without the slack the loop halves the step forever and never converges. The for ... else runs only when no step was accepted, in which case the old β is kept — Python's else on a loop is the cleanest way to express "no break happened" and avoids a separate accepted flag.

    The deviance dispatch handles Tweedie's boundaries before touching the generic formula:

        if power == 1.0:
        return _deviance(y, mu, "poisson", power, theta)
    if power == 2.0:
        return _deviance(y, mu, "gamma", power, theta)

    The generic Tweedie expression divides by (1−p)(2−p), so at exactly p=1 or p=2 it is 0/0. Delegating at the boundary is what makes tweedie(power=1) produce bit-identical coefficients to poisson — the demo prints 0.0e+00, not "close enough". A tolerance-based comparison would hide the fact that the variance and deviance functions must agree exactly at the limit.

    NB2's deviance is written with log1p rather than a ratio of logs:

            t = (np.where(y > 0.0, y * np.log(np.maximum(y, EPS) / mu), 0.0)
             - (y + theta) * np.log1p((y - mu) / (mu + theta)))

    For large θ, (y+θ)/(μ+θ) → 1 and log of that ratio loses most of its significant digits; log1p((y−μ)/(μ+θ)) is exact to first order in the small quantity. Without it, the θ=1e7 limit does not reproduce Poisson to 1e-8, and that is precisely the limit the demo uses to test the family.

    Inference is separated from fitting and uses the rank, not the column count:

        rank = int(np.linalg.matrix_rank(Xa))
    pearson = float(np.sum(sw * (y - mu) ** 2 / var))
    dispersion = 1.0 if phi_known else pearson / max(n - rank, 1)
    cov = dispersion * np.linalg.pinv(info)

    n − rank is the correct residual degrees of freedom: a duplicated column consumes a parameter but carries no information, and dividing by n − d would make φ̂ too large. pinv keeps the covariance finite for a rank-deficient design, where some directions genuinely have no estimable variance. For the dispersion variant the demo's overdispersed counts give φ̂ = 1.659, and every standard error is exactly √1.659 = 1.288 times the Poisson one — the assertion compares the ratio to √φ̂ to 1e-8, which is the entire meaning of quasi-Poisson.

    Complexity #

    Step Cost
    One IRLS iteration O(nd²) for the weighted least squares, O(nd) for μ, w, z
    Covariance O(nd² + d³) once, at the end
    Iterations 4–10 canonical links; 5–30 non-canonical

    Memory is O(nd) for the design plus O(d²) for the covariance. The loop never forms XᵀWX; lstsq works on the n×d weighted design, which is the same asymptotic cost with better conditioning. For d in the thousands and n in the millions, the design is the memory bottleneck, not the iterations.

    Failure modes #

    1. The response is outside the family's support. Negative counts, y=0 for Gamma, or a binomial proportion above 1 produce nan in the deviance on the first iteration. Validate y before fitting; the fit itself cannot infer the support from an array.
    2. Overdispersion. Poisson assumes Var=μ. Real counts often have Var>μ, so the Poisson SEs are too small and every p-value is optimistic. The dispersion variant estimates φ and fixes the inference; NB2 fixes both the mean and the variance model, which is stronger and usually better.
    3. Separation in the binomial model. If a hyperplane separates successes from failures, μ tends to 0/1, the weights μ(1−μ) vanish, and β diverges. The likelihood has no finite maximiser; add an L2 penalty (as logistic_regression.separable_fix does) or use Firth's method.
    4. A non-canonical link with a bad start. Fisher scoring can step to a negative μ for the log link. The code's backtracking rejects such a step, but a custom loop without it will produce log(negative) = nan. This is the reason step-halving is in the loop rather than in a comment.
    5. Offsets on the wrong scale. offset must be log(exposure), not exposure. Passing exposure directly fits η = Xβ + E, which is a different model with a bizarre intercept. The demo's unoffset fit absorbs 0.6·x₀ into β₀ and moves the slope by 0.607; that is the size of the mistake.
    6. Reading φ as a goodness-of-fit statistic. For Poisson and binomial the code fixes φ=1 by design; a deviance/(n−d) ratio near 1 is a coincidence, not evidence that the model fits. Use Pearson residuals or a formal overdispersion test.
    7. Tweedie power outside (1,2). p=0 is Gaussian, p=1 Poisson, p=2 Gamma, but the generic deviance formula is only valid for p∉{0,1,2} and requires y>0 for p>2. Do not extrapolate the parameter without checking the support.
    8. Ignoring uncertainty in θ. NB2 treats the size as known; estimating it (profile likelihood or a grid) and then fixing it understates the standard errors. The same applies to the Tweedie power.
    9. Interpreting the intercept under an offset. With η = β₀ + Xβ + log E, the intercept is a log rate at E=1, not a log count. Report rates, or state the exposure explicitly.
    10. Weights versus IRLS weights. The weights argument is a prior weight (e.g. inverse variance, trials). It enters w multiplicatively; it is not the same object as V(μ), and passing one where the other is meant changes the estimand, not just the precision.

    Applications #

    • Epidemiology and public health. Poisson regression with log(person-years) as an offset is the standard incidence-rate model; the offsets variant is exactly that. Binomial with logit gives odds ratios for risk factors.
    • Insurance pricing. Tweedie with 1<p<2 models aggregate claim cost: a point mass at zero plus a Gamma severity, one GLM instead of a two-part model. Claim counts use Poisson or NB2.
    • Ecology and genomics. Counts of organisms, reads, or mutations are almost always overdispersed; NB2 or quasi-Poisson is the default, and the dispersion estimate is reported alongside the coefficients.
    • Customer spend and demand. Gamma with a log link for positive continuous amounts (revenue per user, rainfall, time-to-event proxies); coefficients are multiplicative effects on the mean.
    • Credit and conversion. Binomial logit for default and conversion; the GLM framing makes the linear predictor, the link, and the calibration explicit, and standard errors are available without a separate bootstrap.
    • A/B testing. Rate metrics are Poisson/binomial GLMs with an offset for exposure; the coefficient on the treatment indicator is the log rate ratio, and its SE is the one the dispersion variant corrects.
    • As an OLS sanity check. identity_link is OLS; if it does not match ols.py, the IRLS loop is wrong, not the statistics.
