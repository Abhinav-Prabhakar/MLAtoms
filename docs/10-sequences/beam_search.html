    Beam search — choosing a sequence from next-token scores

    Intuition #

    A language model answers one question: given the tokens so far, how likely is each possible next token? Everything about generation is a second question, and it is a search problem. A sequence of length \(T\) over a vocabulary of size \(V\) has \(V^T\) candidates, and the model only ever scores one step at a time.

     step 1        step 2                 step 3
   a 0.6        a a 0.30               a a a 0.15
   b 0.4        a b 0.30               a a b 0.15
                b a 0.04               a b a 0.27
                b b 0.36               a b b 0.03
                                       b a ·  ...
                                       b b a 0.32
                                       b b b 0.04

    Greedy decoding follows the boldest branch at every step. It is optimal at each step and not optimal overall, because the best first token can lead to a poor second one. The table in the figure is exactly that: taking a pays \(0.6\) and then splits evenly, while taking b pays \(0.4\) and then offers \(0.9\). Greedy returns a a at \(0.30\); the better sequence b b is worth \(0.36\).

    Beam search keeps the \(B\) best prefixes at each step and extends all of them. It is still not exact — a prefix that looks mediocre now can recover later — but it fixes the failure above, and \(B\) is the knob that trades compute for quality. The rest of the atom is about the other knobs: how to draw a sample instead of taking an argmax, how to cut the tail of the distribution before drawing, and how to bias the scores away from repetitions and from generic text.

    Temperature rescales the logits before the softmax. Dividing by \(T<1\) sharpens the distribution, multiplying by \(T>1\) flattens it, and the exact statement is that the new probability of a token is its old probability to the power \(1/T\), renormalised. Top-\(k\) keeps the \(k\) best tokens and renormalises; top-\(p\) keeps the smallest set whose mass reaches \(p\). Both are truncation, and truncation before sampling is what removes the long tail of nonsense without removing the variety.

    Repetition penalty divides the logits of tokens already in the context, which pushes a model out of a loop. Contrastive decoding subtracts the log-probabilities of a smaller amateur model from those of the expert: a token that both models like is probably generic, and a token that only the expert likes is probably a response to the context. Both are score transformations, and both compose with any of the searches.

    The demos use exact tables, so every claim is checkable. A beam of two turns the \(0.30\) greedy answer into \(0.36\). A beam wide enough to keep all \(27\) prefixes of a three-token vocabulary matches brute force to the last digit, while a beam of one ends \(0.34\) behind. Top-\(p\) at \(0.7\) keeps exactly the two tokens holding \(0.75\) of the mass. A penalty of two sends a model that would repeat one token through four before it locks. Contrastive decoding flips a choice the expert alone makes, and an end token that greedy never emits wins the beam comparison.

    Variants #

    variant what it does draw or argmax cost
    greedy best token at each step argmax \(O(TV)\)
    beam best \(B\) prefixes at each step argmax \(O(TBV)\)
    temperature logits divided by \(T\) draw \(O(TV)\)
    top_k keep the \(k\) best tokens draw \(O(TV\log V)\)
    top_p keep the smallest set with mass \(p\) draw \(O(TV\log V)\)
    repetition_penalty divide scores of used tokens argmax \(O(TV)\)
    contrastive subtract an amateur's log-probabilities argmax \(O(TV)\) 
    graph TD
  A[decoder] --> B[search]
  A --> C[sampling]

    greedy takes the argmax at each step. It is the cheapest option and the baseline every other variant is measured against, and it never recovers from a bad early token.

    beam keeps the \(B\) best prefixes and extends each by every token. It finds better sequences at \(B\) times the cost, and it is exact when \(B\) is at least the number of distinct prefixes at every step.

    temperature samples from \(\mathrm{softmax}(z/T)\). It is the standard way to control how surprising a sample is, and it is a no-op at \(T=1\).

    top_k samples from the \(k\) best tokens only. The count is fixed, so a peaked distribution and a flat one get the same amount of variety.

    top_p samples from the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability reaches \(p\). The set shrinks when the model is confident and grows when it is not, which is the property top-\(k\) lacks.

    repetition_penalty divides positive logits and multiplies negative ones for every token in the context. It is the lowest-cost fix for looping, and it is a blunt instrument because it cannot tell a useful repetition from a degenerate one.

    contrastive subtracts \(\alpha\) times the amateur's log-probabilities from the expert's. It needs a second model, and it targets text that is likely for reasons unrelated to the prompt.

    Derivation #

    The sequence score #

    An autoregressive model factorises the probability of a sequence into one conditional per token,

    \[p(y_1,\ldots,y_T)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(y_t\mid y_{

    The search never sees the product: it sees one row of log-probabilities at a time, indexed by the prefix. Both searches below are the same recursion over prefixes, differing only in how many prefixes they keep.

    Greedy #

    Greedy maximises (1) one factor at a time:

    \[y_t=\arg\max_{v}\ \log p(v\mid y_{

    It is exact when the best continuation is independent of the choice made now, and wrong otherwise. In the figure it picks a because \(0.6>0.4\) and then has to settle for \(0.5\), so its final score is \(\log 0.3\); the sequence b b scores \(\log 0.36\). The difference is \(0.18\) in log space, and it is entirely due to the first step.

    Beam search #

    Let \(B_t\) be the set of \(B\) highest-scoring prefixes of length \(t\). The recursion is

    \[B_0=\{()\},\qquad B_t=\mathrm{top}_B\Big\{\big(y_{

    where the score of an item is its prefix score plus \(\log p(v\mid y_{. Every prefix in \(B_{t-1}\) is extended by every token, all \(B V\) candidates are scored, and the best \(B\) survive. With \(B\ge V^t\) no prefix is ever pruned and the search is exhaustive, which is how the demo builds an exact reference for a vocabulary of three.

    The cost is the \(B V\) scores per step. The reason to prefer a beam over exhaustive search is that the model's distribution is usually concentrated: the number of plausible prefixes grows much more slowly than \(V^t\).

    Stopping and the end token #

    Extending a prefix can only lower its score, since \(\log p(y_t\mid y_{. So the best score any active prefix can still reach is its current score. That gives the bound behind the stopping rule:

    \[\max_{y_{

    The code only applies it once at least \(B\) sequences have finished, because a single early ending is not enough evidence that the search is done. At the end the finished sequences and the surviving active prefixes are compared together: an active prefix that never emitted the end token is still a valid continuation when the length limit is reached.

    Temperature #

    With logits \(z\) and temperature \(T>0\), the shaped distribution is

    \[p_T(v)=\frac{\exp(z_v/T)}{\sum_u \exp(z_u/T)} =\frac{p(v)^{1/T}}{\sum_u p(u)^{1/T}}. \qquad (5)\]

    The second form is the useful one: raising every probability to \(1/T\) and renormalising is the same operation as dividing the logits. As \(T\to0\) the mass concentrates on the argmax; as \(T\to\infty\) it approaches uniform. The demo checks the identity against a direct softmax of \(z/T\) for three temperatures.

    Truncation #

    Top-\(k\) keeps the \(k\) largest logits and sets the rest to \(-\infty\) before renormalising:

    \[S_k=\mathrm{topk}(p,k),\qquad p_k(v)=\frac{p(v)\mathbf{1}[v\in S_k]}{\sum_{u\in S_k}p(u)}. \qquad (6)\]

    Top-\(p\) keeps the shortest prefix of the sorted probabilities whose cumulative mass reaches \(p\):

    \[S_p=\Big\{v_1,\ldots,v_m\Big\},\qquad m=\min\Big\{j:\sum_{i=1}^{j}p_{(i)}\ge p\Big\}, \qquad (7)\]

    where \(p_{(1)}\ge p_{(2)}\ge\cdots\) are the sorted probabilities. The demo's distribution is \(0.5,0.25,0.15,0.1\): at \(p=0.7\) the first two already hold \(0.75\), so \(m=2\), while top-\(k\) with \(k=2\) happens to agree and with \(k=3\) does not. On a flat distribution top-\(p\) keeps many tokens; on a peaked one it keeps few, and that is the whole point.

    Both are applied before the draw, so the sample is drawn from the renormalised truncated distribution rather than from the full one.

    Repetition penalty #

    For a token \(v\) that already appears in the context, the CTRL-style penalty is

    \[z'_v=\begin{cases}z_v/\theta & z_v>0,\\ \theta\,z_v & z_v\le 0,\end{cases} \qquad \theta\ge 1, \qquad (8)\]

    which moves the logit toward zero from either side. A positive logit shrinks by the factor \(\theta\), a negative one grows in magnitude, and the ranking of used tokens relative to unused ones drops. The demo uses four logits \(4,3,2.5,2.2\): with \(\theta=2\) the model visits \(0,1,2,3\) and then locks onto \(0\), because after every token has been used the ranking is the original one again, scaled. The penalty reads the set of tokens in the context, not their recency, which is why it cannot produce a long cycle.

    Contrastive decoding #

    Let \(p_e\) be the expert and \(p_a\) the amateur, and let \(\alpha\ge0\). Contrastive decoding scores a token by

    \[\log \tilde p(v)=(1+\alpha)\log p_e(v\mid y_{

    and samples or takes the argmax of \(\tilde p\). The same expression is the pointwise mutual information between the token and the context under the two models:

    \[\log\tilde p(v)=\log p_e(v\mid y_{

    The first term is what the expert wants; the second rewards tokens the expert likes more than the amateur does. A token both models love has a ratio near one and gets no bonus, and a token the amateur loves more is actively suppressed. In the demo the expert's logits are \(1.0,0.9,0.9\) and the amateur's are \(3,0,0\): the expert alone picks token \(0\), and at \(\alpha=0.5\) the amateur's preference for the same token outweighs the expert's \(0.1\) margin, so the choice moves to token \(1\). With \(\alpha=0\) the variant reduces to the expert and the choice returns.

    Sampling #

    The three sampling variants share one loop: shape the logits, exponentiate and renormalise, draw one token, append it. With a seeded generator the draw is deterministic, which is what lets the demo compare runs. The categorical draw is

    \[v\sim\mathrm{Categorical}\Big(\frac{\exp z'}{\sum_u\exp z'_u}\Big), \qquad (11)\]

    and the loop stops at the end token or at the length limit, whichever comes first. Sampling is not an approximation to the argmax: it explores the distribution on purpose, which is why the same model produces different sentences on different seeds.

    Why beam is not exact #

    The recursion in (3) prunes by current score, and a prefix that is behind now can still win later. A beam of width \(B\) is exact only if the pruned mass is zero, which in practice means never. The demo makes the gap concrete: on a random table over three tokens and three steps, a beam of one is \(0.34\) behind the brute-force optimum, while a beam of \(27\) is exact. Beam search is a good heuristic with a knob, not an algorithm with a guarantee.

    Code walkthrough #

    Truncating the tail #

    def _masked_top_k(logits: np.ndarray, k: int) -> np.ndarray:
    """Keep the k largest logits, set the rest to -inf."""
    if k >= logits.size:
        return logits
    order = np.argsort(-logits)
    output = np.full_like(logits, -np.inf)
    output[order[:k]] = logits[order[:k]]
    return output

    def _masked_top_p(logits: np.ndarray, p: float) -> np.ndarray:
    """Keep the smallest set of tokens whose probability reaches p."""
    probabilities = np.exp(_log_softmax(logits))
    order = np.argsort(-probabilities)
    cumulative = np.cumsum(probabilities[order])
    keep = int(np.searchsorted(cumulative, p) + 1)
    keep = min(max(keep, 1), logits.size)
    output = np.full_like(logits, -np.inf)
    output[order[:keep]] = logits[order[:keep]]
    return output

    Both functions do the same two things: decide a set, then set everything outside it to \(-\infty\). The \(-\infty\) entries survive the later log-softmax as exact zeros, so the draw can never pick a pruned token. searchsorted finds the first index where the cumulative mass reaches \(p\); the +1 converts that index to a count, and the clamp keeps at least one token alive even when \(p\) is smaller than the top probability.

    The penalty #

            output = logits.copy()
        for token in set(context):
            if 0 <= token < output.size:
                output[token] = (output[token] / self.penalty if output[token] > 0.0
                                 else output[token] * self.penalty)
        return output

    set(context) is the key detail: the penalty depends on whether a token has appeared at all, not on how often or how recently. Dividing a positive logit and multiplying a negative one both move it toward zero, which is what makes the rule symmetric around zero and monotone in the penalty. Guarding the index keeps a prompt from a larger vocabulary from raising an IndexError.

    Shaping the distribution #

            shaped = self._penalise(logits, context)
        if self.variant == "contrastive":
            if amateur is None:
                raise ValueError("contrastive decoding needs an amateur model")
            expert = _log_softmax(shaped)
            weak = _log_softmax(self._logits(amateur, context))
            shaped = (1.0 + self.alpha) * expert - self.alpha * weak
            return _log_softmax(shaped)
        if self.variant == "temperature":
            shaped = shaped / self.temperature
        elif self.variant == "top_k":
            shaped = _masked_top_k(shaped, self.k)
        elif self.variant == "top_p":
            shaped = _masked_top_p(shaped, self.p)
        return _log_softmax(shaped)

    Every variant funnels through one function, which is why they compose with any of the searches: the search asks for log-probabilities and does not care how they were produced. Contrastive is the exception that returns early, because it works in log-probability space rather than on logits — the subtraction in (9) is only meaningful after both models have been normalised.

    Greedy #

        def _argmax(self, scores, prompt: tuple, eos, amateur) -> tuple:
        tokens = prompt
        logprob = 0.0
        for _ in range(self.max_length):
            logits = self._logits(scores, tokens)
            log_probs = self._distribution(logits, tokens, amateur)
            token = int(log_probs.argmax())
            logprob += float(log_probs[token])
            tokens = tokens + (token,)
            if eos is not None and token == eos:
                break
        return tokens, logprob

    The prefix is a tuple, so extending it is \(O(1)\) amortised and hashing it for a dictionary costs the length of the prefix. logprob accumulates the model's own log-probability of the tokens it chose, and it is what logprob_ reports after a call. The eos check comes after the append, so the end token is part of the returned sequence.

    Beam extension #

            for _ in range(self.max_length):
            candidates = []
            for tokens, logprob in beams:
                log_probs = self._distribution(self._logits(scores, tokens), tokens,
                                               amateur)
                for token in range(log_probs.size):
                    value = logprob + float(log_probs[token])
                    if not np.isfinite(value):
                        continue
                    if eos is not None and token == eos:
                        finished.append((tokens + (token,), value))
                    else:
                        candidates.append((tokens + (token,), value))

    This is (3) written out. Each prefix is scored once, not once per extension, so the model call count is \(|B|\) per step rather than \(|B|V\). A token whose log-probability is \(-\infty\) — pruned by top-\(k\) or top-\(p\) — is skipped, which is what stops a truncated variant from resurrecting the tail inside the beam. The branch on eos is the only structural difference between the finished pool and the active pool.

    Stopping and the final choice #

                candidates.sort(key=lambda item: -item[1])
            beams = candidates[:self.beam]
            if len(finished) >= self.beam:
                best_finished = max(score for _, score in finished)
                if candidates[0][1] <= best_finished:
                    break
        pool = finished + beams
        best = max(pool, key=lambda item: item[1])
        return best[0], best[1]

    The sort is by negative score, so candidates[0] is the best active prefix and the comparison is against the best finished sequence. Because scores only decrease, candidates[0][1] is an upper bound on anything the active set can still produce, and the break is safe rather than heuristic. The final pool concatenates both lists; in the demo the early end token wins that comparison, which greedy never even considers.

    The sampling loop #

                probabilities = np.exp(log_probs)
            probabilities = probabilities / probabilities.sum()
            token = int(rng.choice(probabilities.size, p=probabilities))

    Renormalising after exp is not redundant: the log-probabilities are already normalised, but truncation and contrastive shaping happen in log space and the exponentiated rows can carry rounding error. Dividing by the sum makes rng.choice receive a valid probability vector, and doing it explicitly keeps the dependency visible. The generator is seeded per call, so two calls with the same seed return the same sequence.

    Dispatch #

            if self.variant == "beam":
            tokens, logprob = self._beam(scores, prompt, eos, amateur)
        elif self.variant in ("top_k", "top_p", "temperature"):
            tokens, logprob = self._sample(scores, prompt, eos, amateur)
        else:
            tokens, logprob = self._argmax(scores, prompt, eos, amateur)

    Three searches for seven variants: the sampling variants differ only in how they shape the distribution, and the shaping variants differ only in which search consumes them. decode also validates the prompt and the end token before any model call, so a bad argument fails before the expensive part.

    Complexity #

    Let \(T\) be the generated length, \(V\) the vocabulary, \(B\) the beam width, \(P\) the model's cost per call and \(N\) the prompt length.

    operation model calls extra work memory
    greedy \(T\) \(O(TV)\) \(O(N+T)\)
    beam \(TB\) \(O(TBV\log(BV))\) \(O(BV)\) candidates
    temperature sample \(T\) \(O(TV)\) \(O(N+T)\)
    top-\(k\) sample \(T\) \(O(TV\log V)\) \(O(V)\)
    top-\(p\) sample \(T\) \(O(TV\log V)\) \(O(V)\)
    repetition penalty \(T\) \(O(T(N+T))\) \(O(V)\)
    contrastive \(2T\) \(O(TV)\) \(O(V)\)

    The model call dominates everything else in practice, so the honest comparison is in calls: greedy and the sampling variants make \(T\), beam makes \(T B\), and contrastive doubles the count because it evaluates the amateur as well. The sorts are over \(V\) or \(BV\) items and are negligible next to a transformer forward pass.

    Memory is the other difference. Greedy keeps one prefix and one distribution; beam keeps \(B V\) scored candidates per step, which is small compared with the KV cache a real model keeps for the prompt and the generated tokens. The cost of a wide beam is latency, not memory.

    Failure modes #

    Greedy treated as optimal. The demo's two-step table is the smallest possible counterexample, and it is not contrived: any model whose first-token preference disagrees with the joint preference fails the same way. A greedy decoder is a baseline, not a guarantee.

    A beam that is too narrow. A beam of one is greedy, and a beam of four can still prune the eventual winner in the first step. The demo's random table puts a beam of one \(0.34\) behind the optimum; widening it to \(27\) closes the gap exactly. Beam width is a quality knob with a cost, and there is no width that is correct for every input.

    Beam collapse. With a beam and a likelihood objective, the highest-scoring sequence is often short and repetitive, because the end token and common tokens are cheap. The classic symptom is a beam whose candidates are all the same sentence with different lengths. Length normalisation or a coverage penalty is the usual fix.

    Unnormalised scores after shaping. Truncation sets entries to \(-\infty\) and contrastive subtraction does not preserve normalisation, so the row must be renormalised before a draw. Skipping the division still produces a valid-looking sample because rng.choice tolerates small errors, and the bug shows up as a slightly wrong distribution rather than as a crash.

    A temperature of zero. Division by zero is the obvious failure, and the constructor refuses it. The subtler one is a temperature so small that the softmax underflows to a one-hot vector, after which sampling is greedy and the seeds stop mattering.

    Top-\(p\) with a tiny \(p\). The clamp to one token keeps the distribution valid, but it silently turns top-\(p\) into greedy. A caller who wanted variety and got none should check \(p\) before blaming the model.

    A penalty that cannot see recency. The rule in (8) reads the set of tokens in the context. Once every token has been used, the ranking returns to the original one and the loop resumes, as the demo shows. Frequency penalties and no-repeat-ngram constraints exist because the static penalty cannot express "not again so soon".

    Contrastive with a badly matched amateur. The amateur must share the expert's tokeniser and be weaker in the same domain; otherwise the subtraction suppresses the right tokens for the wrong reason. An amateur that is stronger than the expert inverts the objective, and an amateur with a different vocabulary makes the log-probabilities incomparable.

    The stopping rule with a length limit. The bound in (4) is about finished sequences. When the length limit is what stops the search, the comparison is between truncated continuations of different lengths, and the scores are not directly comparable without a length normalisation. The demo sidesteps this by using a short limit; a production decoder has to choose a policy.

    An end token inside a truncated set. If top-\(k\) or top-\(p\) removes the end token, the beam can never finish and always runs to the length limit. The failure is quiet: the sequences look fine, they are just longer than they should be.

    Applications #

    Machine translation. Beam search was the default decoder for encoder-decoder translation for years, usually with a length penalty because the raw likelihood favours short outputs. Widths of four to eight were standard, and the quality difference against greedy was large enough to justify the cost.

    Speech recognition. A beam over the CTC lattice or over an attention decoder is how acoustic models become transcripts, often with an external language model shaping the scores. The ctc atom implements the lattice version of the same search.

    Summarisation and captioning. Beam search with a length penalty and a coverage term is still common for tasks judged on ROUGE or CIDEr, where the reference is one particular sentence rather than a distribution.

    Code generation. Sampling with a low temperature and top-\(p\) is the default for code assistants, because a single greedy completion cannot express the several valid ways to write the same function. The truncation keeps the sample inside the syntax the model is confident about.

    Creative writing and dialogue. Temperature and top-\(p\) are the knobs users actually feel. A high temperature increases diversity and the rate of incoherence together; top-\(p\) is the usual compromise because it adapts the candidate set to the model's confidence.

    Constrained generation. The same beam machinery carries hard constraints: a grammar mask sets the logits of illegal tokens to \(-\infty\), and the search then only ever emits valid syntax. JSON mode in production APIs is this idea at the logit level.

    Retrieval-augmented and tool-using models. Contrastive decoding and classifier-free guidance are the same subtraction with different references, and they are used to make a model attend to the retrieved context instead of falling back on its prior.
