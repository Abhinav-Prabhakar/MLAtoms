    10 · Sequences/tier 1/7 variants/source

    Dynamic time warping — align sequences that do not share a clock

    Intuition #

    Two recordings of the same word are not the same length, and no single shift makes them line up. One speaker stretched the vowels, the other paused in the middle, and the samples therefore sit at different times. Comparing them point by point measures the clock, not the content. Dynamic time warping measures the content by allowing a monotone reparameterisation of time: point \(i\) of one sequence may correspond to point \(j\) of the other, as long as the correspondence never runs backwards.

    The object is a grid. Rows are the points of the first sequence, columns are the points of the second, and each cell holds the cost of pairing those two points. A path from the top-left corner to the bottom-right corner, moving only right, up or diagonally, is a valid alignment; the cost of the alignment is the sum of the cells it visits. DTW is the minimum over paths, and it is found by the same dynamic program as any shortest path on a grid.

            y:  0    1    2    3    4
      x ┌────┬────┬────┬────┬────┐
      0 │ 0  │ 1  │ 4  │ 9  │ 16 │
      1 │ 1  │ 0  │ 1  │ 4  │ 9  │
      2 │ 4  │ 1  │ 0  │ 1  │ 4  │
      3 │ 9  │ 4  │ 1  │ 0  │ 1  │
        └────┴────┴────┴────┴────┘
             cost of pairing x_i with y_j

    That grid is the whole algorithm. Everything else is a decision about which cells are legal. The unconstrained grid allows a path to match the beginning of one sequence with the end of the other, which is sometimes right (a query inside a reference) and usually wrong (two recordings of the same word). A band forbids the far cells, a parallelogram forbids the steep slopes, free endpoints on one axis allow the subsequence case, and a soft minimum replaces the hard choice with a differentiable one.

    The variants are those decisions, plus two operations built on top of the grid: averaging a set of sequences in the warped geometry, and approximating the grid on a coarser one.

    The demos make the trade-offs numerical. On a shape sampled through a known monotone warp, the recovered path pairs points that are a hundredth of a cycle apart at worst, and the distance is \(0.0675\). On a stronger warp, a Sakoe–Chiba band of three cells costs \(7.8\) times the unconstrained distance, while a band of ten costs nothing because it contains the optimal path. Soft DTW's gradient matches finite differences to \(5\times10^{-10}\), and as the smoothing shrinks the soft distance converges to the hard one. The barycenter of twelve warped copies of a shape recovers it to a correlation of \(0.99987\) and beats the pointwise mean, \(0.2577\) against \(0.2839\) in total distance.

    The one number to carry away is the cost of a wrong constraint. On the strongly warped pair the unconstrained path is worth \(0.100\); a band of three returns \(0.778\). A band is not an optimisation. It is a statement about which pairings are physically possible, and it is the part of the model that has to be right.

    Variants #

    variant grid endpoints returns
    standard all cells fixed exact path and cost
    sakoe_chiba band of radius \(r\) fixed exact path and cost
    itakura parallelogram fixed exact path and cost
    subsequence all cells free on the reference best matching interval
    soft_dtw all cells fixed smoothed cost and gradient
    dtw_barycenter all cells fixed an average sequence
    fast_dtw progressively refined band fixed approximate path and cost

    standard is the classical algorithm: squared Euclidean local cost, all cells legal, both endpoints fixed. It is exact for its model and the reference against which the others are measured.

    sakoe_chiba keeps a band of half-width radius around the diagonal. It is the right constraint when the two sequences are known to be roughly aligned — same gesture, same word, same heartbeat — and it reduces the work from \(nm\) to about \(n\,r\).

    itakura keeps a parallelogram defined by a maximum local slope. Instead of a fixed width, it allows the path to drift in the middle and forces it back at both ends, which is a different statement: the alignment may be locally steep but the global clocks cannot diverge.

    subsequence frees the start and the end on the reference axis. The query must be matched completely, but it may begin and end anywhere in the longer sequence, which is the query-by-example problem: find this word in that recording, this pattern in that trace.

    soft_dtw replaces min with a soft minimum controlled by a temperature \(\gamma\). The result is a smooth, differentiable function of both sequences, which is what makes it usable as a loss or a layer. It is not a distance: it is negative for large \(\gamma\) and it depends on the path length.

    dtw_barycenter computes DTW barycenter averaging: align every member to a running average, then average the points that landed at the same index. It is the correct notion of mean when the samples are misaligned in time.

    fast_dtw aligns a coarsened copy of the pair, projects the coarse path back as a band, and refines. It is approximate, and its distance is never below the exact one because the band is a restriction of the grid.

    Derivation #

    The objective and the recursion #

    Let \(x_{0:n-1}\) and \(y_{0:m-1}\) be the two sequences, and let \(C(i,j)=\lVert x_i-y_j\rVert^2\) be the local cost. A warping path is a sequence of cells \(\pi=(\pi_0,\dots,\pi_L)\) with \(\pi_0=(0,0)\), \(\pi_L=(n-1,m-1)\), and each step in \(\{(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)\}\). The distance is

    \[D(n-1,m-1) = \min_{\pi}\ \sum_{(i,j)\in\pi} C(i,j). \qquad (1)\]

    The path set is exponential, but the objective is additive and the constraint is local, so the principle of optimality applies: the best path to \((i,j)\) ends with the best path to one of its three predecessors. Hence

    \[D(i,j) = C(i,j) + \min\big(D(i-1,j),\,D(i,j-1),\,D(i-1,j-1)\big), \qquad (2)\]

    with \(D(0,0)=C(0,0)\) and the first row and column filled by a single predecessor each. The table is \(n\times m\), every cell is \(O(1)\), and the backtracking pass recovers the path that achieved the minimum.

    The code is (2) with the mask folded in, and with the free-start case handled by initialising the whole first row instead of its first cell:

            if free_start:
            table[0] = np.where(mask[0], cost[0], np.inf)
        else:
            table[0, 0] = cost[0, 0]
        for i in range(1, n):
            for j in range(m):
                if not mask[i, j]:
                    continue
                best, source = np.inf, (-1, -1)
                for di, dj in STEPS:
                    pi, pj = i - di, j - dj
                    if pi < 0 or pj < 0:
                        continue
                    if table[pi, pj] < best:
                        best, source = table[pi, pj], (pi, pj)
                if np.isfinite(best):
                    table[i, j] = cost[i, j] + best
                    back[i, j] = source

    The np.isfinite(best) test is not defensive noise: a cell whose three predecessors are all outside the band must stay at infinity, and adding the cost to infinity would poison the comparison. The stored source is what makes backtracking possible without re-deriving the argmin.

    Bands and parallelograms #

    The Sakoe–Chiba band of half-width \(r\) keeps the cells within \(r\) of the diagonal after accounting for the length ratio:

    \[|j - i\,(m-1)/(n-1)| \le r. \qquad (3)\]

    In code the diagonal is rounded to the nearest integer cell and the row is filled in one slice:

            slope = (m - 1) / max(n - 1, 1)
        for i in range(n):
            centre = int(round(i * slope))
            low = max(0, centre - radius)
            high = min(m - 1, centre + radius)
            mask[i, low:high + 1] = True

    The Itakura parallelogram is a different shape. In normalised coordinates \(u=i/(n-1)\), \(v=j/(m-1)\), it requires the local slope to lie between \(1/s\) and \(s\) at both ends of the path:

    \[v \le s u, \qquad v \ge u/s, \qquad 1-v \le s(1-u), \qquad 1-v \ge (1-u)/s.\]

    The first two bound the path near the start, the last two near the end, and their intersection is the parallelogram. It allows a band that is wide in the middle and narrow at the corners, which is the shape you want when the two clocks are allowed to drift but not to swap order.

    Free endpoints #

    For the subsequence variant, the query \(x\) must be matched in full and the reference \(y\) may be entered and left anywhere. That is one line of initialisation and one line at the end:

    \[D(0,j) = C(0,j)\ \ \forall j, \qquad \text{distance} = \min_j D(n-1,j). \qquad (4)\]

    Freeing the whole first row lets the path start at any reference index; taking the minimum over the last row lets it stop at any reference index. The path's first cell carries the start index, which is the answer to "where does this pattern begin". The suffix of the reference after the match is ignored, which is the point; the prefix before it is skipped by construction.

    The implementation keeps the start in the backtracked path rather than in a separate variable, so the interval is read off the alignment:

            end = int(np.argmin(ends))
        path = self._backtrack(back, (x.shape[0] - 1, end), free_start=True)

    Soft DTW #

    Hard DTW is a minimum, and a minimum is piecewise constant in the inputs, so its gradient is zero almost everywhere. Soft DTW replaces the minimum with the smooth minimum

    \[\mathrm{softmin}_\gamma(a_1,\dots,a_k) = -\gamma\log\sum_i e^{-a_i/\gamma},\]

    which tends to \(\min_i a_i\) as \(\gamma\to0\) and to the mean as \(\gamma\to\infty\). The recursion is otherwise identical:

    \[D(i,j) = C(i,j) + \mathrm{softmin}_\gamma\big(D(i-1,j), D(i,j-1), D(i-1,j-1)\big). \qquad (5)\]

    The implementation subtracts the smallest predecessor before exponentiating, which is the same log-sum-exp guard as everywhere else in this chapter:

                    previous = table[i - 1, j], table[i, j - 1], table[i - 1, j - 1]
                lowest = min(previous)
                total = sum(math.exp(-(p - lowest) / gamma) for p in previous)
                table[i, j] = cost[i, j] + lowest - gamma * math.log(total)

    The gradient follows from the same recursion run backwards. Differentiating (5) with respect to each predecessor gives the softmax of the negative predecessor values, so a reverse sweep accumulates an adjoint at each cell and the derivative with respect to a point of \(x\) is twice the adjoint row times the residual:

    \[\frac{\partial D}{\partial x_i} = 2\sum_j \bar D(i,j)\,(x_i-y_j), \qquad \bar D(p) = \sum_{q} \bar D(q)\,\frac{\partial D(q)}{\partial D(p)}.\]

    That is the entire backward pass, and the demo checks it against finite differences.

    Barycenters #

    The mean of a set of sequences is not their pointwise mean unless they are already aligned. DTW barycenter averaging alternates two steps: align every member to the current average, then set each average point to the mean of the member points that were warped onto it:

    \[\bar z_j = \frac{1}{|S_j|}\sum_{s\in S_j} z^{(s)}_i, \qquad S_j = \{(s,i) : (i,j)\in\pi^{(s)}\}. \qquad (6)\]

    The length of the barycenter is inherited from the initial average, which is the medoid — the member with the smallest total distance to the others. The iteration is not guaranteed to decrease the total distance monotonically, but it does in practice on well-behaved data, and the demo's history is non-increasing.

    FastDTW #

    The exact table is \(O(nm)\), which is too much for sequences of ten thousand points. FastDTW exploits the fact that alignments are smooth at coarse scales: halve both sequences by averaging adjacent points, align the short pair, project the coarse path back to a band of radius \(r\), and run the exact recursion inside that band. Recurse until the sequences fit in a handful of points.

    Each level costs \(O(n_i r)\) and the lengths halve, so the total is \(O(nr)\) for a fixed radius, and the result is exact whenever the true path stays inside the projected band. When it does not, the answer is an upper bound, because the band is a subset of the grid.

    Code walkthrough #

    Backtracking #

        @staticmethod
    def _backtrack(back: np.ndarray, end: tuple, free_start: bool) -> np.ndarray:
        path = [end]
        while True:
            i, j = path[-1]
            if free_start and i == 0:
                break
            if not free_start and path[-1] == (0, 0):
                break
            pi, pj = back[i, j]
            if pi < 0:
                raise ValueError("the backtracking table is incomplete")
            path.append((int(pi), int(pj)))
        path.reverse()
        return np.asarray(path, dtype=int)

    The two stopping conditions are the two endpoint conventions: a fixed-start path ends at the origin, a free-start path ends anywhere in the first row. The pi < 0 guard catches the case where the table was filled with an inconsistent mask, which would otherwise walk to (-1, -1) and index out of bounds.

    The band mask #

            mask = np.zeros((n, m), dtype=bool)
        slope = (m - 1) / max(n - 1, 1)
        for i in range(n):
            centre = int(round(i * slope))

    The mask is built once per pair and passed to the same _fill routine that the unconstrained variant uses. That separation is what keeps seven variants from becoming seven copies of the recursion: only the mask, the endpoint convention, and the min-versus-softmin choice change.

    The coarse pass #

            coarse_x = self._reduce(x)
        coarse_y = self._reduce(y)
        coarse_mask = self._fast_mask(coarse_x, coarse_y, radius)
        coarse_cost = self._costs(coarse_x, coarse_y)
        coarse_path, _ = self._solve(coarse_cost, coarse_mask)

    _reduce averages adjacent pairs and duplicates the last point when the length is odd, so every level halves the length exactly. The recursion returns a mask, not a distance: the distance is only ever computed at full resolution, which is what keeps the approximation consistent with the exact algorithm's cost model.

    Accumulating a barycenter #

                    path, value = self._exact(member, average)
                total += value
                for i, j in path:
                    accumulator[j] += member[i]
                    counts[j] += 1.0

    Every cell of the path contributes one member point to one average index, and the division by counts is the average. The np.maximum(counts, 1.0) in the next line protects an index that no path visited, which happens when the average is longer than the members; those indices keep their previous value instead of becoming NaN.

    Complexity #

    Let \(n,m\) be the sequence lengths, \(r\) the band radius, \(L\) the path length, \(S\) the number of members in a barycenter, and \(I\) the DBA iterations.

    operation time memory
    standard, subsequence \(O(nm)\) \(O(nm)\)
    sakoe_chiba \(O(nr)\) \(O(nr)\)
    itakura \(O(nm)\) worst case \(O(nm)\)
    soft_dtw \(O(nm)\) \(O(nm)\)
    soft_dtw gradient \(O(nm)\) extra \(O(nm)\)
    dtw_barycenter \(O(I\,S\,nm)\) \(O(nm)\)
    fast_dtw \(O(nr)\) \(O(nr)\)

    The table is the memory bottleneck, and for long sequences it is the reason the banded variants exist: a radius of ten on a pair of length ten thousand is four orders of magnitude less work than the full grid. Both the table and the backpointer array are materialised here because backtracking needs them; if only the distance is wanted, two rows suffice and the memory drops to \(O(m)\).

    Failure modes #

    Unconstrained warping can match anything. With no band, a path may map one point of \(x\) to a long stretch of \(y\) — or the reverse — whenever that lowers the sum. On sequences with slowly varying amplitude this produces alignments that are optimal under (1) and meaningless as correspondences. The constraint is the model; if the domain says the clocks cannot diverge by more than ten percent, the band should say so too.

    A constraint can be infeasible on the integer grid. The Itakura parallelogram with a maximum slope close to the length ratio leaves less than one cell per row, and the rounding can then leave no monotone path at all. The code raises no monotone path satisfies the constraint rather than returning infinity. In the demo, a slope of \(1.3\) on a \(60\times80\) pair is refused while \(1.5\) is accepted, and this is a property of the grid, not a bug.

    The band can be tight enough to be wrong. The same demo's band of three costs \(7.8\) times the unconstrained distance. A band is a bias-variance choice like any other: wide enough to contain the true path, narrow enough to exclude the spurious ones, and there is no way to know which without a validation set.

    FastDTW is approximate, and coarsening is a low-pass filter. On smooth data a radius of two reproduces the exact answer and a radius of one is already \(3.5\) times worse. On piecewise-constant data the coarse path is actively wrong: \(75\) against an exact \(50\), a \(50\%\) error. Averaging neighbours destroys the feature the alignment depends on. Use it when the sequences are smooth and the grid is large, and check it against exact DTW on a sample.

    Soft DTW is not a distance. It is below the hard value for every positive \(\gamma\), it can be negative, and it grows with the path length, so two pairs of different lengths are not comparable. The demo's \(\gamma=10\) value is \(-1159\) on a pair whose hard distance is \(0.100\). Normalise by length, or compare only at matched lengths and a fixed \(\gamma\).

    Squared Euclidean cost is scale-sensitive. A feature measured in thousands dominates one measured in fractions, and the alignment will be driven entirely by the first. Standardise the features before warping, or use a cost that is invariant to the units; there is no such thing as an unscaled DTW distance across heterogeneous channels.

    DTW is not a metric. It is symmetric and non-negative, but the triangle inequality fails: \(d(x,z)\) can exceed \(d(x,y)+d(y,z)\) because the optimal alignment of \(x\) to \(z\) need not pass through the alignment of \(y\). Anything that assumes a metric — vantage-point trees, Lipschitz bounds, some clustering guarantees — must use the lower bound (LB_Keogh) or a different representation instead.

    The barycenter depends on its starting length and its members. DBA cannot change the length of the average, so a set of sequences of very different lengths produces an average stuck at the medoid's length. It is also a local method: the medoid and the iteration order matter, and a bad initial average can trap the iteration. The demo's history is monotone, but that is a property of this data, not a guarantee.

    Subsequence matching finds the best interval, not all of them. If the query occurs twice, only the cheaper occurrence is returned. Repeated patterns need either a sliding-window scan or a different formulation; the single-interval answer is what (4) optimises.

    Applications #

    Speech recognition before HMMs. Isolated-word recognisers aligned a test utterance to a stored template with DTW and classified by the nearest template. The sakoe_chiba and itakura constraints come from that literature, and the hmm atom in this chapter is what replaced them once enough data existed to estimate a generative model.

    Handwriting and shape matching. Online handwriting is a sequence of pen positions, and two copies of the same character differ by a monotone speed change, which is exactly the DTW model. The same machinery matches contours and skeleton curves in shape retrieval.

    Human activity and gesture recognition. Accelerometer streams from wearables are warped by speed and style; DTW with a band is the classical nearest-neighbour classifier, and the barycenter is how prototypes are averaged.

    Time-series classification and clustering. One-nearest-neighbour with DTW is a famously strong baseline on the UCR archive, and DTW barycenters are the centroids in DTW k-means. The fast_dtw variant exists because the archive's sequences are long enough for the quadratic cost to matter.

    Query by humming and music retrieval. A hummed melody is a warped, noisy version of a score; subsequence DTW finds where it occurs. The subsequence variant is the direct implementation of that query.

    Biomedical signal alignment. ECG beats, EEG epochs and gait cycles are compared after warping, both for clustering patients and for aligning trials before averaging evoked responses. The barycenter is the template that a patient is compared against.

    Imitation learning and trajectory matching. A demonstrated trajectory and a reproduction are compared under DTW to score the imitation without penalising a different speed; the gradient of soft DTW is what lets the score be optimised through.
