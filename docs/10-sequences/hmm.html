    Hidden Markov models — the chain you cannot see

    Intuition #

    In a Markov chain you watch the state. Here you watch what the state emits. The state sequence is latent, the observations are conditionally independent given it, and every question you can ask splits into two halves: what the chain did, and what each state sounds like.

    That split is what makes the model useful. Weather is a two-state chain you cannot observe directly; what you observe is whether people carry umbrellas. Speech is a chain of phonemes you cannot observe directly; what you observe is a waveform. A genome is a chain of CpG islands and background you cannot observe directly; what you observe is a sequence of nucleotides. In each case the interesting object is hidden and the observed object is noisy, and an HMM is the smallest model that respects both facts.

    Everything follows from three sets of numbers: where the chain starts, how it moves, and what each state emits. Given those numbers, two matrix recursions answer the inference questions — the forward pass for the likelihood and the smoothed posteriors, the Viterbi pass for the single best path. Given only observations, one algorithm estimates the numbers: Baum–Welch, which is expectation–maximisation applied to the latent path.

              hidden:   ●───────●───────●───────●
                    │       │       │       │
                    ▼       ▼       ▼       ▼
          observed: x₁      x₂      x₃      x₄

          A: state → state       π: where it starts
          b: state → observation  the only part you can see

    The seven variants slice the same object along two axes. Three of them name the emission model: symbols (discrete), a diagonal Gaussian per state (gaussian), or a mixture of Gaussians per state (gmm_hmm). Four of them name the algorithm on the surface: exact posterior marginals (forward_backward), maximum-probability paths (viterbi), multi-restart training with a likelihood trace (baum_welch), or the scaled linear-domain recursions (scaling). The axes compose, because none of the algorithms cares what the emission model is.

    What the demos establish, beyond the usual parameter recovery, is that the three claims in the file are true rather than plausible. On a seven-symbol sequence the forward recursion agrees with enumeration over all \(128\) state paths to ten digits. On a ten-symbol sequence Viterbi agrees with the best of \(1024\) enumerated paths exactly. And the scaled fit reaches the same likelihood as the log-space fit to \(8\times10^{-11}\), while the unscaled linear recursion returns exactly zero.

    The number worth remembering from the fits is \(352\). That is how many nats a two-component mixture per state gains over a single Gaussian per state on four well-separated clusters. Choosing the emission model is not a detail; it is the difference between modelling the data and modelling its average.

    Variants #

    variant emission fit surface
    discrete categorical symbols Baum–Welch score
    gaussian diagonal Gaussian Baum–Welch score
    gmm_hmm Gaussian mixture per state Baum–Welch score
    forward_backward inferred from the data Baum–Welch posterior_marginals, pairwise_posteriors
    viterbi inferred from the data Baum–Welch predict, viterbi_score_
    baum_welch inferred from the data multi-restart EM history_, n_iter_
    scaling inferred from the data scaled linear EM scaling_factors_

    discrete is the classical HMM of the speech and bioinformatics literature: a finite alphabet, a table of emission probabilities per state, and counting as the estimator. Its M-step is the simplest in the file: no responsibilities, no variance floor, no moments.

    gaussian replaces the table with one mean and one variance per state per feature, so the M-step becomes weighted moments. It is the workhorse for real-valued sequences and the model to try first when the state means are what distinguish the states.

    gmm_hmm gives every state a mixture instead of a single Gaussian, which is the right model when a state is itself multimodal — a phoneme with several allophones, a regime with two operating points. It costs a set of mixture responsibilities per state and turns the M-step into a weighted soft-clustering.

    forward_backward exposes the smoothing recursions: the posterior probability of each state at each time, and of each transition. These are what you plot, what you threshold, and what the other variants use internally.

    viterbi exposes the max-product recursion and the score of the path it returns. It answers "what is the single most likely explanation", which is a different question from "what is each state's marginal" and can have a different answer.

    baum_welch is the training variant: it allows several random restarts, keeps the best, and exposes the likelihood after every iteration. The trace is the model's own convergence diagnostic and the restarts are the only defence against local optima.

    scaling runs the same EM in the linear domain with one normalising factor per step, the technique that predates log-space arithmetic and is still how most production implementations work. It is also the clearest demonstration of why the factors are necessary.

    Derivation #

    The model and the forward recursion #

    Let \(z_{1:T}\) be the hidden states and \(x_{1:T}\) the observations. The chain is first-order and the emissions are conditionally independent given the state:

    \[p(z_{1:T}, x_{1:T}) = \pi_{z_1} b_{z_1}(x_1)\prod_{t=2}^{T} A_{z_{t-1}z_t}b_{z_t}(x_t).\]

    Summing over paths is exponential, but the sum factorises. Define the forward variable \(\alpha_t(k) = p(x_{1:t}, z_t = k)\); then

    \[\alpha_t(k) = b_k(x_t)\sum_{j} \alpha_{t-1}(j) A_{jk}, \qquad (1)\]

    with \(\alpha_1(k)=\pi_k b_k(x_1)\) and \(p(x_{1:T})=\sum_k \alpha_T(k)\). The recursion has the shape of the chain: one \(K\times K\) matrix product per observation, \(O(TK^2)\) in total, and the exponential disappears.

    In log space the sum becomes a log-sum-exp, and the code is (1) with that substitution:

            log_alpha = np.empty((x.shape[0], self.n_states))
        log_alpha[0] = log_pi + log_b[0]
        for t in range(1, x.shape[0]):
            log_alpha[t] = log_b[t] + _logsumexp(log_alpha[t - 1][:, None] + log_a,
                                                 axis=0)

    _logsumexp subtracts the row maximum before exponentiating. Without that, a log-probability of \(-2000\) underflows to zero and the sum is wrong; with it, the only quantities that ever get exponentiated are at most one in magnitude.

    The backward variable is the same idea run in reverse, \(\beta_t(j) = p(x_{t+1:T}\mid z_t=j)\):

    \[\beta_t(j) = \sum_k A_{jk} b_k(x_{t+1}) \beta_{t+1}(k), \qquad (2)\]

    with \(\beta_T(k)=1\). It does not depend on the past, which is why it can be computed once and reused for every time step:

            log_beta = np.zeros((x.shape[0], self.n_states))
        for t in range(x.shape[0] - 2, -1, -1):
            log_beta[t] = _logsumexp(log_a + (log_b[t + 1] + log_beta[t + 1])[None, :],
                                     axis=1)

    Posteriors #

    Multiplying the two messages and normalising gives the smoothed state probability, and adding the transition in the middle gives the smoothed transition probability:

    \[\gamma_t(k) = \frac{\alpha_t(k)\beta_t(k)}{p(x)}, \qquad \xi_t(j,k) = \frac{\alpha_t(j)A_{jk}b_k(x_{t+1})\beta_{t+1}(k)}{p(x)}. \qquad (3)\]

    Both are exact under the model: no sampling, no approximation, no iteration. The same quantities computed from the scaled recursions are what every EM step consumes.

    The vectorised computation is worth showing because it removes a loop over the sequence, which is where a naive implementation spends most of its time:

            future = log_b[1:] + log_beta[1:]
        log_xi = (log_alpha[:-1][:, :, None] + log_a[None, :, :]
                  + future[:, None, :] - likelihood)
        xi = np.exp(log_xi)

    The three factors broadcast against each other into a \((T-1)\times K\times K\) array, one slice per transition. Memory is the price: \(O(TK^2)\) floats, which is fine for a few thousand observations and a handful of states and is the reason long-sequence implementations use the forward-only formulation of the M-step instead.

    The M-step #

    Baum–Welch maximises the expected complete-data log-likelihood. The expectation is over the posterior of the hidden path, which (3) gives exactly, and the maximisation separates because the complete-data likelihood is a sum of independent terms. The result is counting with soft assignments:

    \[\pi_k = \gamma_1(k), \qquad A_{jk} = \frac{\sum_t \xi_t(j,k)}{\sum_t \gamma_t(j)}, \qquad (4)\]

    and for symbols \(b_k(v) = \sum_{t:x_t=v}\gamma_t(k) \big/ \sum_t \gamma_t(k)\). The Gaussian and mixture updates are the same weighted moments with \(\gamma_t(k)\) as the weight; for the mixture, an extra responsibility \(r_t(k,m)\) splits each state's mass between its components.

    Two implementation details make (4) survive real data. A row with no mass would divide by zero, so it falls back to the uniform distribution — a state that died is given no information rather than a NaN. And the emission estimate is a weighted count, which the categorical branch computes directly:

                counts = np.zeros_like(self.emission_)
            for symbol in np.unique(values):
                counts[:, symbol] = gamma[values == symbol].sum(axis=0)
            self.emission_ = _normalise_rows(np.maximum(counts, 1e-300))

    _normalise_rows is the guard in one place:

        totals = matrix.sum(axis=1)
    out = np.full_like(matrix, 1.0 / matrix.shape[1])
    live = totals > 0.0
    out[live] = matrix[live] / totals[live, None]
    return out

    The small floor on the counts is not there to avoid the division — _normalise_rows masks empty rows — but to keep every entry strictly positive, so that np.log of the emission table never returns a negative infinity. A symbol the model considers impossible then costs about \(690\) nats, which is the intended penalty rather than a special case.

    The monotonicity of EM is the one guarantee worth stating. Each iteration maximises a lower bound on the log-likelihood (Jensen's inequality gives the bound), so the likelihood cannot decrease. The demo checks every fitted history for monotonicity, and that is a property of the algorithm, not of the data.

    Viterbi #

    The most probable path is the maximum over paths of the same joint probability that (1) summed. Swapping sum for max gives the max-product recursion

    \[\delta_t(k) = b_k(x_t)\max_j \delta_{t-1}(j)A_{jk}, \qquad (5)\]

    with \(\delta_1(k)=\pi_k b_k(x_1)\) and the path recovered by backtracking through the argmax at each step. It is dynamic programming over the same trellis as the forward pass, with the same \(O(TK^2)\) cost, and the log-domain version is numerically identical to the linear one because there are no sums to underflow.

            for t in range(1, length):
            candidate = delta[t - 1][:, None] + log_a
            back[t] = np.argmax(candidate, axis=0)
            delta[t] = log_b[t] + candidate[back[t], np.arange(self.n_states)]

    The candidate[back[t], np.arange(self.n_states)] indexing is the vectorised "take the maximum along each column"; writing it as a loop would be clearer and twice as slow. The backtracking pass then walks back from the best final state to the start.

    The distinction from posterior decoding matters. Viterbi maximises \(p(z_{1:T}\mid x_{1:T})\) over whole paths. Posterior decoding picks \(\arg\max_k \gamma_t(k)\) at each \(t\) independently, which maximises the expected number of correctly labelled positions but can return a path the model considers impossible. The demo's two decodings differ at three of twelve hundred positions, which is typical when the posteriors are decisive and a warning when they are not.

    Scaling #

    The linear-domain forward pass has the same recursion but no logs, and after a few hundred observations it underflows: \(p(x_{1:T})\) is a product of \(T\) numbers smaller than one, and the smallest double is about \(10^{-308}\). The fix is to renormalise at every step and remember the factors:

    \[\hat\alpha_t = \frac{\alpha_t}{c_t}, \qquad c_t = \sum_k \alpha_t(k), \qquad \log p(x_{1:T}) = \sum_t \log c_t. \qquad (6)\]

    The normalised vector stays a probability distribution, so nothing underflows; the product of the factors is the likelihood, and the sum of their logs is the log-likelihood. It is exactly the same information as the log-space recursion, carried in a different representation.

            alpha[0] = pi * np.exp(log_b[0])
        factors[0] = max(alpha[0].sum(), 1e-300)
        alpha[0] /= factors[0]
        for t in range(1, length):
            alpha[t] = (alpha[t - 1] @ a) * np.exp(log_b[t])
            factors[t] = max(alpha[t].sum(), 1e-300)
            alpha[t] /= factors[t]

    The backward pass must use the same factors, dividing by \(c_{t+1}\) at each step, or the product \(\hat\alpha\hat\beta\) is not \(\gamma\). That consistency condition is the fiddly part of the classical algorithm and the reason log-space implementations became popular.

    Emission models #

    For a diagonal Gaussian, the log density is a sum over features; the reshape is what lets one expression serve both the per-state and the per-component case:

            diff = x.reshape((x.shape[0],) + (1,) * axis + (x.shape[1],)) - means

    With axis=1 the result is \((T,K,d)\) and sums to a \((T,K)\) table; with axis=2 it is \((T,K,M,d)\) and sums to \((T,K,M)\), one column per mixture component. The mixture then needs a log-sum-exp over components and a responsibility per component, which is where the mixture M-step gets its extra factor.

    The Gaussian M-step is weighted moments with \(\gamma\) as the weight, and the variance is floored at a tiny fraction of the global variance. The floor is a guard against a component collapsing onto a single observation, where the likelihood is infinite and EM would happily go there.

    Initialisation #

    EM is local, so the starting point decides which optimum is found. The initialisation here is deliberately structured rather than random: sort the sample along its first principal axis, cut it into equal-frequency chunks, use the chunk means as the state means, add a small jitter, and start the transition matrix sticky. The jitter and the seed are what make a restart a restart; the structure is what keeps most restarts out of the degenerate optima.

    Code walkthrough #

    The likelihood and the state marginals #

    score runs the recursion of (1) and nothing else, which makes it the cheapest thing in the file and the natural place to check a fit. The _require gate is not cosmetic: calling predict on the forward_backward variant, or posterior_marginals on the viterbi variant, raises rather than silently returning something that looks like an answer.

    The scaled pass #

                beta[t] = (a @ (np.exp(log_b[t + 1]) * beta[t + 1])
                       / max(factors[t + 1], 1e-300))

    This is the backward half of (6). The division by factors[t + 1] is the consistency condition: the forward vector was divided by that same number, so the backward vector must be too, and then their product is a posterior instead of a product of unrelated scalings.

    Mixture responsibilities #

            log_resp = weighted - log_b[:, :, None]
        resp = np.exp(log_resp) * gamma[:, :, None]
        totals = np.maximum(resp.sum(axis=0), 1e-300)

    weighted holds \(\log w_{km} + \log N(x_t;\mu_{km},\sigma^2_{km})\) and log_b holds the log-sum-exp over \(m\), so the difference is the log responsibility. Multiplying by the state posterior gives the joint responsibility of state \(k\) and component \(m\) at time \(t\), which is exactly the weight the mixture M-step needs. The 1e-300 floor is there because a component can, in principle, be responsible for nothing.

    Choosing the emission model #

            if self.emission == "unknown":
            emission = "categorical" if (integer_like and values.ndim == 1) else "gaussian"

    The three emission variants fix their family in the constructor; the four algorithm variants infer it. A one-dimensional array of integers is read as symbols, anything else as measurements — which is a heuristic, and the reason discrete exists as an explicit variant for the case where a float array really does hold codes. The guards on length are the other half of _prepare: an empty sequence has no first observation, so \(\alpha_1\) is undefined, and a scalar has no time axis at all.

    Complexity #

    Let \(T\) be the sequence length, \(K\) the number of states, \(d\) the number of features, \(M\) the mixture components and \(I\) the EM iterations.

    operation time memory
    one E-step, symbols or Gaussians \(O(TK^2)\) \(O(TK^2)\)
    one E-step, mixture \(O(TK^2 + TKMd)\) \(O(TK^2)\)
    one M-step, symbols \(O(TK)\) \(O(K^2)\)
    one M-step, mixture \(O(TKMd)\) \(O(KMd)\)
    fit \(O(I\,T K^2)\) \(O(TK^2)\)
    score \(O(TK^2)\) \(O(TK)\)
    predict \(O(TK^2)\) \(O(TK)\)
    posterior_marginals \(O(TK^2)\) \(O(TK^2)\)

    The \(O(TK^2)\) memory for the pairwise posteriors is the only avoidable term. The standard alternative computes the M-step's transition counts in a single forward pass over \(\hat\alpha\), the transition matrix and \(\hat\beta\), never materialising \(\xi\); that is what production implementations do, and it is worth doing when \(T\) is in the millions. Here the explicit array is what makes the code readable.

    Failure modes #

    The likelihood is multimodal and EM finds one mode. With \(K\) states there are \(K!\) labellings of the same model, all with identical likelihood, so the fitted state numbering means nothing. That is why the demos relabel before comparing. Less benign are genuinely different optima: fitting four states to data from three, the demo's restarts disagree by \(0.74\) nats while the likelihood itself differs from the three-state fit by only \(3\). The parameters differ completely; the likelihood barely notices.

    More states is not more model. The same demo fits three and four states to three-state data. Training likelihood improves from \(-1784.2\) to \(-1781.2\), while held-out likelihood degrades from \(-1839.2\) to \(-1845.4\). The extra state is fitting noise. The number of states is a model-selection decision and needs held-out data or a criterion, not the training likelihood.

    Underflow, if you skip the scaling. The unscaled linear recursion in the demo returns exactly \(0.0\) after two thousand observations, while the log-likelihood the model reports is \(-2690.7\). The failure is silent: a zero likelihood looks like a very bad model, not a broken implementation. If a likelihood is exactly zero on a long sequence, this is the first thing to check.

    A dead state takes its row with it. If no observation is ever assigned to a state, its \(\gamma\) column is zero, and the transition row becomes \(0/0\). The code falls back to uniform, which keeps the fit running but leaves a state that means nothing. The symptom is a state with a uniform transition row and a flat emission; the fix is fewer states or a better initialisation, not more iterations.

    A Gaussian component can collapse onto one point. The likelihood of a Gaussian with \(\sigma\to0\) at an observation goes to infinity, so the M-step would happily set the variance to zero. The variance floor prevents the singularity and keeps the model honest; without it, the fit can drive one variance to zero within a few iterations and report a likelihood that is infinite and meaningless. The same issue appears in gmm_hmm and is the reason the floor is relative to the data's own variance.

    The emission model is a modelling assumption, not a detail. On four well-separated clusters, a single Gaussian per state loses \(352\) nats to a two-component mixture per state. In the other direction, a mixture with too many components will split a unimodal state into two identical halves and call the split structure. Compare emission families on held-out data, as the demo does.

    Viterbi and posterior decoding can disagree, and Viterbi can be worse by the metric you care about. Viterbi maximises the probability of the whole path; posterior decoding maximises per-position accuracy. If the downstream task scores positions, posterior decoding is the right decoder even though its path is less probable. The three differing positions in twelve hundred are a reminder that the two answers are answers to different questions.

    Conditional independence is doing a lot of work. The model assumes the observations are independent given the state. Correlated noise, overlapping windows or a slowly varying measurement scale all violate that, and the fitted states will encode the correlation rather than the phenomenon. The usual fixes are richer emissions (full-covariance Gaussians, autoregressive HMMs) or a different model class entirely.

    Applications #

    Speech recognition. The classical system was an HMM per phone, with Gaussian mixture emissions over cepstral features, and the Viterbi and Baum–Welch algorithms in this file are the ones that made it work. Modern recognisers replaced the emission model with neural networks but kept the HMM decoding layer for decades, and CTC — the ctc atom in this chapter — is the alignment machinery that made the frame-level layer optional.

    Bioinformatics. CpG islands, gene finding and copy-number segmentation are all hidden-state problems over nucleotide or probe sequences; the discrete demo is the toy version of a CpG-island model, and the forward–backward posteriors are how the island boundaries are drawn.

    Regime switching in finance and economics. Returns switch between low-volatility and high-volatility regimes; a two-state Gaussian HMM with time-varying variance is the standard starting point, and the smoothed probability of the high-volatility state is a widely published risk indicator.

    Human activity and gesture recognition. Sensor streams from phones and wearables are segmented into activities by an HMM whose emissions are summary features per window; the state sequence is the activity label and the transition matrix encodes which activities follow which.

    Natural language processing. Part-of-speech tagging was an HMM over word emissions before it was a CRF, and the crf atom is its discriminative successor. The Viterbi recursion is the same in the two models; only the features change.

    Fault detection and predictive maintenance. Machine condition is a hidden state, sensor readings are the emissions, and the filtered state probability is the alarm signal. The change_point atom is the alternative when the state is persistent and the question is "when did it change" rather than "which state is it in".
