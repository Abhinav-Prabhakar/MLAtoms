    Conditional random fields — exact inference on a chain

    Intuition #

    A classifier that labels each position independently can produce a sequence that no sensible process would emit: a verb after a determiner, a closing bracket without an opening one, a state transition the model itself assigns probability zero. A conditional random field fixes this by scoring a whole labelling and normalising over all labellings, so the model's answer is always a path, and the training signal is always a path.

    The trick is that the score is local even though the probability is global. Each position contributes a unary term from its features, each adjacent pair contributes a transition term, and the sequence score is their sum. The probability is that sum exponentiated and divided by the sum over every possible labelling — a number that is exponential in the length to write down and linear to compute, because a chain factors.

       x_0     x_1     x_2     x_3
    │       │       │       │
   ┌▼─┐    ┌▼─┐    ┌▼─┐    ┌▼─┐
   │U │    │U │    │U │    │U │     unary scores, one row per position
   └┬─┘    └┬─┘    └┬─┘    └┬─┘
    │  ┌───┐│  ┌───┐│  ┌───┐│
    └─▶│ A │└─▶│ A │└─▶│ A │┘       transition scores between neighbouring labels
       └───┘   └───┘   └───┘

    Everything in the atom follows from that picture. Forward-backward sums over the exponential number of paths with two linear passes and, on the way, hands back the marginal probability of every label at every position. Viterbi replaces the sum with a maximum and returns the single best path. The gradient of the likelihood is an expected count minus an observed count, and both counts come from the same forward-backward pass. Training is then ordinary optimisation: full-batch descent, minibatch descent, or L-BFGS.

    The sixth variant is the interesting extension. A first-order field scores a transition \(A(y_{t-1}, y_t)\), so its state is one label. A second-order field scores \(A(y_{t-2}, y_{t-1}, y_t)\), so its state is a pair of labels. All three algorithms run unchanged once the chain is built over pairs, which is why the implementation has one inference engine and a small adapter that changes the state space.

    The demos are checks first and stories second. Enumeration of all \(243\) paths agrees with the recursions to \(4\times10^{-16}\), the analytic gradient agrees with central differences to \(1.1\times10^{-8}\) for both orders, and posterior decoding loses \(0.32\) of joint score to Viterbi on a random chain because the best label at each position need not be part of the best sequence. Then a process that only a pair of labels can predict: labels run \(0,0,1,1,2,2,0,0,\ldots\) through a noisy channel. The first-order field reaches \(0.769\) held-out accuracy at a log-likelihood of \(-7.105\); the second-order field reaches \(1.000\) at \(-0.001\).

    Variants #

    variant what it adds what it answers
    linear_chain the model and full-batch descent scores, labels, a trained field
    forward_backward the alpha and beta tables the partition function, marginals
    viterbi max-plus decoding the single best labelling
    crf_sgd minibatch descent with momentum the same fit, scaled to many sequences
    crf_lbfgs quasi-Newton with a line search the same fit in far fewer passes
    higher_order states are pairs of labels transitions that depend on two labels 
    graph TD
  A[conditional random field] --> B[the model]
  A --> C[inference]
  A --> D[training]
  A --> E[structure]
  B --> B1[linear_chain]
  C --> C1[forward_backward]
  C --> C2[viterbi]
  D --> D1[crf_sgd]
  D --> D2[crf_lbfgs]
  E --> E1[higher_order]

    linear_chain is the model of (1) plus plain gradient descent on the negative log-likelihood. It exists as its own variant because the simplest optimiser makes the objective visible: every iteration is one exact gradient and nothing else.

    forward_backward exposes the two tables that everything else uses. Asking it for the tables is how you inspect a model, and its alpha and beta are the same arrays the marginal computation consumes.

    viterbi decodes by max-plus dynamic programming and also offers posterior_decode, which takes the per-position argmax of the marginals. The two disagree often, and the disagreement is instructive rather than a bug.

    crf_sgd takes the same gradient on minibatches with momentum. Each pass costs a fixed number of gradient evaluations rather than one, but the updates start moving before the whole training set has been read.

    crf_lbfgs keeps the last few curvature pairs and runs the two-loop recursion. It reaches the same loss in a quarter of the passes of plain descent on the demo data, at the price of a line search that evaluates the objective several times per iteration.

    higher_order replaces the transition with a second-order one by running the first-order machinery over pairs \((y_{t-1}, y_t)\). The state space grows from \(K\) to \(K^2\), so the cost grows by a factor of \(K\); nothing else changes.

    Derivation #

    The model #

    Let \(f_t\in\mathbb{R}^F\) be the features at position \(t\) of one sequence, and \(y_t\in\{0,\ldots,K-1\}\) its label. With weights \(W\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times K}\), a unary bias \(b\), transition scores \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}\), and start and end scores \(s,e\in\mathbb{R}^{K}\), the score of a labelling \(y\) is

    \[E(x,y)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(f_t^{\top}W+b\big)_{y_t} +\sum_{t=2}^{T}A_{y_{t-1},y_t}+s_{y_1}+e_{y_T}, \qquad (1)\]

    and the conditional probability is

    \[p(y\mid x)=\frac{\exp E(x,y)}{Z(x)},\qquad Z(x)=\sum_{y'}\exp E(x,y'). \qquad (2)\]

    The normaliser is a sum over \(K^T\) labellings, which is why the next section matters. Note what is not in the model: no distribution over \(x\), and no independence assumption about the features. The field conditions on the whole input, which is what separates it from a hidden Markov model. That difference is also why a CRF does not suffer the label bias problem of a maximum-entropy Markov model: the transition scores are not renormalised per state before the observation is seen.

    The forward recursion #

    Write \(u_t=W^{\top}f_t+b\) for the unary scores at position \(t\) and define

    \[\alpha_t(j)=u_t(j)+\log\sum_{i}\exp\big(\alpha_{t-1}(i)+A_{ij}\big), \qquad \alpha_1(j)=s_j+u_1(j). \qquad (3)\]

    \(\alpha_t(j)\) is the log of the total unnormalised score of every prefix of length \(t\) that ends in label \(j\). The definition is a sum over prefixes, but the recursion only needs the previous row: every prefix ending in \(i\) can be extended to \(j\) by adding one transition. At the end,

    \[\log Z=\log\sum_{j}\exp\big(\alpha_T(j)+e_j\big). \qquad (4)\]

    The sum inside (3) is in log space, so it uses the same stable logsumexp as the mixture models. The code is the recursion and nothing else:

            alpha[:, 0] = start + unary[:, 0]
        for t in range(1, length):
            alpha[:, t] = _logsumexp(
                alpha[:, t - 1, :, None] + transition[None], axis=1) + unary[:, t]
        return alpha, _logsumexp(alpha[:, -1] + end[None], axis=1)

    The shape gymnastics are worth reading once. alpha[:, t-1, :, None] is (batch, states, 1), transition[None] is (1, states, states), and the sum is over the first state axis, which is the previous label; the result is (batch, states) indexed by the current label. One axis change turns the recursion into a batch of matrix operations with no Python loop over states.

    The backward recursion and the marginals #

    Define the suffix score

    \[\beta_t(i)=\log\sum_{j}\exp\big(A_{ij}+u_{t+1}(j)+\beta_{t+1}(j)\big), \qquad \beta_T(j)=e_j. \qquad (5)\]

    Then the marginal probability of label \(j\) at position \(t\) is the product of the prefix and suffix that meet there, divided by the partition function:

    \[\gamma_t(j)=\frac{\exp\big(\alpha_t(j)+\beta_t(j)\big)}{Z}, \qquad (6)\]

    and the marginal probability of the transition \(i\to j\) between \(t\) and \(t+1\) is

    \[\xi_t(i,j)=\frac{\exp\big(\alpha_t(i)+A_{ij}+u_{t+1}(j)+\beta_{t+1}(j)\big)}{Z}. \qquad (7)\]

    In code both are one exp away from the tables, because the log partition function is subtracted inside the exponent:

            node = np.exp(alpha + beta - log_z[:, None, None])

                edge = np.exp(alpha[:, :-1, :, None] + transition[None]
                          + (unary[:, 1:] + beta[:, 1:])[:, :, None, :]
                          - log_z[:, None, None, None])

    The edge tensor is (batch, steps - 1, states, states) and its last two entries satisfy \(\sum_{ij}\xi_t(i,j)=1\) for every \(t\). The demo checks exactly that, and checks both tensors against enumeration.

    The gradient #

    The log-likelihood of a batch is \(\sum_n \log Z_n-E(x_n,y_n)\), so its gradient with respect to any score is the model's expected count of that score minus the count the observed labels actually produce:

    \[\frac{\partial(-\log p)}{\partial\theta} =\mathbb{E}_{p(y\mid x)}\Big[\frac{\partial E}{\partial\theta}\Big] -\frac{\partial E(x,y)}{\partial\theta}. \qquad (8)\]

    For the unary weights, the expected count of feature \(f\) paired with label \(k\) is \(\sum_t f_{t}\,\gamma_t(k)\), so

    \[\frac{\partial(-\log p)}{\partial W} =\sum_t f_t\big(\gamma_t-\mathbf{1}_{y_t}\big)^{\top}, \qquad (9)\]

    and the transition gradient is the expected edge count minus the observed edge count. The implementation computes the difference once and reuses it:

            difference = (expected - observed) / labels.shape[0]
        grads = {"W_": np.einsum("ntf,ntk->fk", features, difference),
                 "b_": difference.sum(axis=(0, 1))}

                grads["A_"] = (edge.sum(axis=(0, 1)) - observed_edge) / labels.shape[0]

    The observed edge count is a histogram of adjacent label pairs, computed with np.add.at so that repeated pairs accumulate instead of overwriting. The start and end scores get the same treatment at the first and last position. Dividing by the batch size makes the loss a mean, which is what the line search and the learning rates assume.

    The whole gradient is exact. There is no sampling, no truncation and no approximation of \(Z\); the demo verifies it against central differences for every score in both orders.

    Viterbi #

    Replace every log-sum-exp in (3) with a max and every sum in (4) with a max, and keep an argmax at each step:

    \[\delta_t(j)=u_t(j)+\max_i\big(\delta_{t-1}(i)+A_{ij}\big), \qquad \delta_1(j)=s_j+u_1(j), \qquad (10)\]

    then walk the argmax back from \(\arg\max_j(\delta_T(j)+e_j)\). The max-plus semiring is the log-sum-exp semiring with the sum replaced by a maximum, which is why the same code shape appears twice:

            for t in range(1, length):
            scores = delta[:, t - 1, :, None] + transition[None]
            back[:, t] = scores.argmax(axis=1)
            delta[:, t] = scores.max(axis=1) + unary[:, t]

    Viterbi returns the most probable path, not the sequence of most probable labels. The two agree only when one path dominates every marginal, and the demo measures the gap on a random chain: the posterior path is \(0.32\) of joint score worse than the Viterbi path.

    Training #

    Any optimiser can consume (9). The three in the atom are deliberately different in character:

    • linear_chain takes the full-batch gradient and steps downhill.
    • crf_sgd takes a minibatch gradient with momentum \(0.9\).
    • crf_lbfgs builds a low-rank approximation of the inverse Hessian from the last curvature pairs and takes a line-searched step.

    The L-BFGS direction comes from the two-loop recursion: walk backwards through the stored pairs to build the vector \(q\), scale it, then walk forwards to add the curvature corrections back. The forward half is

                q = grad.copy()
            alphas = []
            for direction, difference in zip(directions[::-1],
                                             differences[::-1], strict=True):
                rho = 1.0 / float(difference @ direction)
                alpha = rho * float(direction @ q)
                q = q - alpha * difference
                alphas.append(alpha)

    The stored pairs are \((s_k,u_k)\) with \(s_k=\theta_{k+1}-\theta_k\) and \(u_k=g_{k+1}-g_k\). A pair is kept only when \(s_k^{\top}u_k>10^{-12}\), because a pair with negative curvature would make the approximate Hessian indefinite; the ten most recent pairs are enough in practice.

    The minibatch update is three lines, and the momentum is what makes a small batch usable on a sequence model:

                    for name, grad in grads.items():
                    velocity[name] = 0.9 * velocity[name] - self.lr * grad
                    setattr(self, name, getattr(self, name) + velocity[name])

    Second order #

    A second-order field scores \(A(y_{t-2},y_{t-1},y_t)\), which is not a chain over labels. It becomes one over pairs: define the state \(z_t=(y_{t-1},y_t)\) for \(t\ge 2\), and a sentinel state \((\star,y_1)\) for the first position. The transition from \(z_{t-1}=(y_{t-2},y_{t-1})\) to \(z_t=(y_{t-1},y_t)\) exists only when the shared label matches, and its score is \(A(y_{t-2},y_{t-1},y_t)\).

    \[z_t=(y_{t-1},y_t)\in\{0,\ldots,K-1\}^2,\qquad z_1=(\star,y_1). \qquad (11)\]

    The state space is \(K^2+K\) with the sentinel row. The adapter builds exactly that chain, with impossible transitions set to \(-\infty\):

            pair_unary = np.concatenate([unary] * (states + 1), axis=2)
        start = np.full(size, -np.inf)
        start[states * states:] = self.s2_

            transition = np.full((size, size), -np.inf)
        for previous in range(states + 1):
            for middle in range(states):
                row = previous * states + middle
                for current in range(states):
                    transition[row, middle * states + current] = self.A2_[
                        previous, middle, current]

    The unary term is replicated across the \(K+1\) copies of the pair, so a position contributes its evidence once whichever pair it is in. After decoding, the label sequence is the second coordinate of every pair:

        def _pair_labels(self, path: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        """Recover labels from a path over pairs (previous, current)."""
        return path % self.n_states_

    The demo's second-order experiment is the reason to bother. The labels follow \(0,0,1,1,2,2,0,0,\ldots\), so the next label depends on two previous labels and no first-order transition can express the rule. With \(27\) scores the first-order field is capped by what the emissions say; with \(54\) the second-order field reaches \(1.000\) held-out accuracy.

    Code walkthrough #

    A stable log-sum-exp #

    def _logsumexp(values: np.ndarray, axis: int) -> np.ndarray:
    """Log-sum-exp along one axis, safe when an entry is negative infinity."""
    peak = values.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)
    safe = np.where(np.isfinite(peak), peak, 0.0)
    with np.errstate(divide="ignore"):
        total = safe + np.log(np.exp(values - safe).sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True))
    return np.squeeze(total, axis=axis)

    The subtle case is a row that is entirely \(-\infty\), which happens in the second-order chain: a pair that cannot follow the previous pair. Its maximum is \(-\infty\), the subtraction would be nan, and the whole recursion would be poisoned. Replacing a non-finite peak with zero makes that row's exponentials zero, the log of zero is \(-\infty\), and the row stays impossible, which is correct.

    Reading a labelling's score #

            if self.order == 1:
            transition = self.A_[labels[:, :-1], labels[:, 1:]].sum(axis=1)
            start = self.s_[labels[:, 0]]
            end = self.e_[labels[:, -1]]

    Fancy indexing with two label arrays gathers one score per adjacent pair without a loop, and the sum over positions gives the transition part of (1). The same expression serves training, where the observed score is subtracted from \(\log Z\), and the demo's scoring of the posterior path against the Viterbi path.

    The mask that is not a mask #

            pair_unary = np.concatenate([unary] * (states + 1), axis=2)

    There is no boolean mask anywhere in the second-order adapter. Illegal pairs are handled by a transition score of \(-\infty\), which the same log-sum-exp treats as zero probability. That is the log-space equivalent of a mask, and it composes with the existing recursion instead of requiring a second implementation.

    Checking the tables #

        for t in range(length):
        for state in range(states):
            node[:, t, state] = probabilities[:, paths[:, t] == state].sum(axis=1)

    The brute-force reference in the demo groups the enumerated paths by their label at each position and sums the probabilities. It is the definition of a marginal, written in the least efficient way possible, which is exactly what a reference should be. Enumeration is only possible for a small alphabet and a short chain — \(243\) paths here — and the test is worthless for anything larger, which is why the recursions exist.

    Complexity #

    Let \(T\) be the length, \(K\) the number of labels, \(F\) the number of features and \(B\) the batch size.

    operation time memory
    unary scores \(O(BTFK)\) \(O(BTK)\)
    forward or backward \(O(BTK^2)\) \(O(BTK)\)
    marginals \(O(BTK^2)\) \(O(BTK^2)\) for edges
    Viterbi \(O(BTK^2)\) \(O(BTK)\) plus backpointers
    one gradient \(O(BTFK+BTK^2)\) \(O(BTK^2)\)
    second-order gradient \(O(BTFK+BTK^3)\) \(O(BTK^2)\)

    The transition term dominates unless \(F\gg K\). The second-order adapter has \(K^2+K\) states and a transition matrix of that size, but only \(K\) outgoing edges per state are finite, so a sparse implementation would be \(O(BTK^3)\) time and \(O(BTK^2)\) memory rather than the dense \(O(BTK^4)\) the matrix suggests. A third order would multiply by another \(K\) and is rarely worth it.

    Where the time goes #

    On the demo's \(40\) sequences of length \(8\), plain descent takes \(400\) gradient evaluations to reach a loss of \(0.6953\); minibatch descent takes \(1000\) evaluations across \(200\) passes and reaches \(0.7316\); L-BFGS takes \(321\) evaluations across \(100\) iterations and reaches \(0.6948\). The quasi-Newton method wins on both counts here because the parameter count is tiny and the curvature is informative. The story reverses on a large feature space, where the \(O(md)\) work per iteration and the line search start to matter and stochastic updates win on wall-clock time.

    Failure modes #

    Underflow in linear space. Multiplying \(K^T\) probabilities is hopeless for any realistic length; the recursions must run in log space, and every sum inside them must be a logsumexp. A single exp of raw scores and a product across positions underflows at around position \(30\) for moderate scores.

    A row that is entirely impossible. If a transition is set to \(-\infty\) and a whole label becomes unreachable, the forward row is \(-\infty\) and \(\log Z\) is \(-\infty\). That is a modelling error, not a numerical one: some path must remain possible, or the data is inconsistent with the constraints. The second-order adapter can produce such rows for a malformed A2_, and the log-space arithmetic turns the symptom into nan marginals rather than a crash.

    Posterior decoding mistaken for Viterbi. Taking the argmax of each marginal gives a sequence that can have a lower joint score than the Viterbi path, and can even be a sequence the model considers less likely than many others. The demo shows a \(0.32\) gap on a random chain. Structured prediction requires the joint decoder; per-position accuracy is a different objective.

    Label bias. A locally normalised model — an MEMM — divides by the outgoing mass at each state before seeing the observation, so a state with few outgoing transitions is favoured regardless of the evidence. A CRF avoids this by normalising once over whole sequences, but only if the transitions are global. Renormalising anything per position reintroduces the bias.

    Feature leakage across time. Features that summarise the whole sequence, or that are computed after the labels are known, make the training likelihood optimistic and the test likelihood meaningless. The field assumes \(f_t\) is a function of the input only; nothing in the code enforces that.

    Overfitting with few sequences. There is no regularisation in this atom. With one sequence and a rich feature map the transition and unary scores can grow without bound while the training likelihood rises. An L2 penalty is one extra term in the gradient; the demo's held-out split is the cheap alternative.

    Second-order blow-up. The state space grows as \(K^{\text{order}}\). Three labels at order two is \(12\) states; ten labels at order three is \(1100\), and the transition tensor has more than a million entries. Most of them are zero, which is why real implementations use sparse transitions or feature-based scoring instead of a dense tensor.

    Misaligned start and end scores. The first position gets \(s\) but no transition, the last gets \(e\) but no outgoing transition. A loop that applies the transition at every step double-counts or drops one end, and the symptom is a likelihood that trains but a Viterbi path that is one label short. The second-order adapter's sentinel row exists to keep this bookkeeping explicit.

    Optimiser masking a modelling error. L-BFGS will happily converge to a local optimum of a misspecified model. The second-order experiment in the demo is a reminder: the first-order field does not fail to fit, it fits as well as its state space allows and is simply unable to represent the rule, so more iterations do not help.

    Applications #

    Named entity recognition. The classic use: unary features from word shape, prefixes and a gazetteer, transition scores that learn "I-ORG cannot follow B-PER", and Viterbi decoding. The constraints are hard because the transition scores are global, which is exactly what a per-token classifier cannot promise.

    Part-of-speech tagging and chunking. Both are sequence labelling with strong local dependencies, and both were early CRF benchmarks. Sha and Pereira's shallow parser and Kudo and Matsumoto's chunker are the standard references for the feature templates that made CRFs competitive before neural taggers.

    Gene finding and biological sequence labelling. Exon and intron boundaries are long-range and constrained; a chain with a handful of labels and rich k-mer features is a natural fit, and the forward-backward marginals give per-base posterior probabilities for free.

    Speech and handwriting. Frame-level acoustic scores become unary terms, and the transition matrix encodes phoneme or character order. CTC, the next atom, is the neural version of the same alignment problem with a blank symbol and a sum over alignments instead of a single path.

    Structured output layers. A bidirectional LSTM or transformer produces the unaries and a CRF layer supplies the transitions; the layer is trained by the same negative log-likelihood and decoded by the same Viterbi recursion. The crf_lbfgs optimiser is the piece that a neural framework replaces with backpropagation and Adam, while the inference stays identical.

    Segmentation with semi-Markov structure. A segment-level field scores a whole span rather than a single label, which is the higher_order idea taken to arbitrary length. Semi-Markov CRFs are the standard tool when the duration of a label matters, as in information extraction or activity recognition.

    Constrained generation. Any decoder that must emit a valid sequence — a parser, a finite-state transducer, a schema-constrained generator — can be seen as a field with hard transitions. Viterbi over that chain is the constrained decoding, and the partition function is the normaliser that turns scores into probabilities.
