    10 · Sequences/tier 2/4 variants/source

    Connectionist temporal classification — summing over alignments

    Intuition #

    A frame-level sequence model says something at every frame, but the training data for speech, handwriting or gesture gives one transcription for a whole recording and no idea where each character starts. CTC makes that mismatch into a definition: a label sequence is produced by every frame path that reduces to it, and its probability is the sum of those paths' probabilities. Reduce means merge runs of the same symbol and delete the blank.

     frames   f0     f1     f2     f3     f4     f5
 symbols  a      a     •      b      •      a

 collapse: merge aa -> a, drop blanks      ->   a  b  a

    The blank is the part that is easy to miss. Without it, a a at two frames collapses to a single a, so a model could never emit a doubled letter. With it, the path a • a has a blank separator and collapses to a a. The blank is also where the model puts "nothing is happening here", which is most frames of most recordings.

    The sum over paths looks exponential, and it is: there are \(C^T\) frame paths for \(T\) frames and \(C\) channels. But the paths that collapse to a fixed target form a lattice with \(2S+1\) states, and the sum over lattice paths is two linear recursions. That is the whole algorithm. Forward gives the likelihood, backward gives the posterior over lattice states, and their product gives the gradient with respect to the frame logits, which is exactly the expected count minus the observed count at every frame and symbol.

    Decoding is a separate question. The most likely frame path — take the argmax at every frame and collapse — is not the most likely label sequence, because one label sequence owns many frame paths. A beam over collapsed prefixes, keeping separate probabilities for prefixes that end in a blank and in a label, is the standard answer. The demos measure the difference rather than assert it: on \(24\) random problems greedy decoding matches the exact marginal decoder \(9\) times, a width-eight beam over extended paths matches it \(13\) times, and a width-eight prefix beam \(23\) times.

    Everything is numpy. The loss matches the enumeration of all \(81\) frame paths on a small lattice to \(4\times10^{-16}\), the symbol marginals to \(2.2\times10^{-16}\), and the gradient through a softmax to \(8.4\times10^{-10}\). On that lattice the best single alignment carries only \(7.1\%\) of the target's probability, which is the cleanest statement of what the sum is doing.

    Variants #

    variant what it computes cost
    ctc_loss likelihood, posterior and gradient \(O(T S)\) per sequence
    greedy_decode argmax per frame, collapsed \(O(T C)\)
    beam_decode beam over extended paths \(O(T B C)\)
    prefix_beam beam over collapsed prefixes \(O(T B C)\) 
    graph TD
  A[CTC] --> B[the loss]
  A --> C[decoding]
  B --> B1[ctc_loss]
  C --> C1[greedy_decode]
  C --> C2[beam_decode]
  C --> C3[prefix_beam]

    ctc_loss is the forward-backward pass over the extended lattice. It is the only variant that trains: given frame probabilities and target sequences, it returns the mean negative log-likelihood and the gradient with respect to the frame logits. Its decode is greedy, so it is a complete model on its own.

    greedy_decode takes the most likely channel at each frame and applies the collapse rule. It is exact for the best path, not for the best label sequence, and it is the baseline that everything else is measured against.

    beam_decode keeps the best beam extended paths at each frame, then collapses them at the end and sums the probabilities of paths that agree. It is a beam over alignments, so its slots are spent on different phrasings of the same output.

    prefix_beam searches over collapsed prefixes instead. Each prefix carries two probabilities — the mass of paths ending in a blank and the mass ending in the last label — which lets one beam entry stand for every alignment of that prefix. It is the same search with the alignments merged online.

    Derivation #

    The alignment sum #

    Let \(x\) be \(T\) frames, \(y_t\in\mathbb{R}^{C}\) the model's distribution at frame \(t\), and \(\ell=(\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_S)\) a target with \(S\le T\). A frame path \(\pi\in\{0,\ldots,C-1\}^T\) collapses to \(\ell\) if merging runs and deleting the blank symbol \(b\) gives \(\ell\). Then

    \[p(\ell\mid x)=\sum_{\pi\,:\,\mathrm{collapse}(\pi)=\ell}\ \prod_{t=1}^{T}y_t(\pi_t), \qquad (1)\]

    and training minimises \(-\log p(\ell\mid x)\). The number of terms is the number of alignments, which the atom counts exactly with a small dynamic program; for two channels and five frames the counts for \(1\), \(1\,1\) and \(1\,1\,1\) are \(15\), \(15\) and \(1\). Three runs of one label can only be written as 1 blank 1 blank 1, so there is exactly one alignment left.

    The extended lattice #

    Interleave the target with blanks:

    \[\ell'=(b,\ell_1,b,\ell_2,\ldots,b,\ell_S,b)\in\mathbb{N}^{2S+1}. \qquad (2)\]

    A path through the lattice visits states in order, may stay, may advance one, and may advance two — the last move skips the blank between two different labels. It may not advance two into a blank, and it may not skip over a repeated label, because the two copies would merge. The condition for entering state \(s\) from \(s-2\) is therefore

    \[\ell'_s\ne b \quad\text{and}\quad \ell'_s\ne \ell'_{s-2}. \qquad (3)\]

    The code builds that condition once and reuses it in the forward pass, the backward pass, the Viterbi alignment and the alignment count.

    Forward and backward #

    Let \(\alpha_t(s)\) be the log of the total probability of every lattice path that ends in state \(s\) at frame \(t\) and has emitted frames \(1\) to \(t\). Then

    \[\alpha_1(1)=y_1(b),\quad \alpha_1(2)=y_1(\ell_1), \qquad (4)\]
    \[\alpha_t(s)=y_t(\ell'_s)+\log\!\sum_{s'\in\{s,s-1,s-2\}} \exp\alpha_{t-1}(s'), \qquad (5)\]

    with the terms for \(s-1\) and \(s-2\) dropped at the boundary and \(s-2\) dropped when (3) fails. The likelihood is the sum over the two final states, the last blank and the last label:

    \[\log p(\ell\mid x)=\log\big(\exp\alpha_T(2S+1)+\exp\alpha_T(2S)\big). \qquad (6)\]

    The backward table is the same recursion read from the end, and it deliberately excludes the emission at frame \(t\):

    \[\beta_T(2S+1)=\beta_T(2S)=0,\qquad \beta_t(s)=\log\!\sum_{s'\in\{s,s+1,s+2\}} \exp\big(\beta_{t+1}(s')+y_{t+1}(\ell'_{s'})\big). \qquad (7)\]

    With both tables the posterior over lattice states at frame \(t\) is

    \[\gamma_t(s)=\frac{\exp\big(\alpha_t(s)+\beta_t(s)\big)}{p(\ell\mid x)}, \qquad (8)\]

    and, unlike a hidden Markov model, the two tables multiply rather than one being a conditional of the other. Summing \(\gamma_t(s)\) over the states whose symbol is \(k\) gives the posterior mass on channel \(k\) at frame \(t\).

    The gradient #

    Let \(z_t(k)\) be the logit behind \(y_t(k)\), so \(y_t=\mathrm{softmax}(z_t)\). Differentiating \(-\log p\) through the softmax gives the usual form,

    \[\frac{\partial(-\log p)}{\partial z_t(k)} =y_t(k)-\sum_{s:\,\ell'_s=k}\gamma_t(s), \qquad (9)\]

    which is the model's probability minus the posterior mass that the target puts on that channel at that frame. The first term is local; the second is the entire sequence's opinion about that frame, delivered by the backward pass.

    The code scatters the posterior back onto symbols and subtracts:

                mass = np.zeros((frames, channels))
            for state in range(extended.size):
                mass[:, extended[state]] += posterior[:, state]
            gradient[index] = batch[index] - mass

    Summing (9) over \(k\) gives zero, because both terms sum to one. The demo checks that the frame columns of the gradient sum to \(4.4\times10^{-16}\), and that every entry matches a central difference through the softmax.

    Collapse and greedy decoding #

    The collapse rule is a single pass with one piece of state, the previous symbol:

    \[\mathrm{collapse}(\pi)_i=\pi_t \quad\text{if}\quad \pi_t\ne\pi_{t-1}\ \text{and}\ \pi_t\ne b. \qquad (10)\]

    Greedy decoding applies it to the per-frame argmax. It is exactly the best path through the lattice with the target left free, which is why it is cheap and why it is not the best label sequence.

    Beam search #

    A frame-synchronous beam over extended paths keeps the best \(B\) paths at each frame and extends each by every channel. At the end the paths are collapsed and the ones that agree are summed. Its weakness is visible in the cost: two paths that differ only by a blank in a place that does not change the collapse occupy two beam slots for one output.

    Prefix search fixes that by making the beam entry a collapsed prefix and carrying two masses, \(p_b\) for paths that end in a blank and \(p_{nb}\) for paths that end in the last label. For a prefix \(\ell\) and a channel \(k\ne b\) with \(p=p_b+p_{nb}\),

    \[\begin{aligned} k=b:&\quad p_b'(\ell)\mathrel{+}=p\,y_t(b),\\ k=\ell_{|\ell|}:&\quad p_{nb}'(\ell)\mathrel{+}=p_{nb}\,y_t(k),\quad p_{nb}'(\ell k)\mathrel{+}=p_b\,y_t(k),\\ \text{otherwise}:&\quad p_{nb}'(\ell k)\mathrel{+}=p\,y_t(k). \end{aligned} \qquad (11)\]

    The middle line is the whole trick: emitting the same label twice in a row merges into the same prefix only if the previous frame was a blank, and otherwise starts a new run. The code is those three cases:

                            elif prefix and symbol == last:
                            blank, nonblank = updated.get(prefix, (NEG, NEG))
                            merged = float(np.logaddexp(nonblank, p_nonblank + score))
                            updated[prefix] = (blank, merged)
                            longer = prefix + (symbol,)
                            blank, nonblank = updated.get(longer, (NEG, NEG))
                            reopened = float(np.logaddexp(nonblank, p_blank + score))
                            updated[longer] = (blank, reopened)

    With enough width the prefix beam is exact, and the demo checks that width \(32\) matches the enumerated marginal decoder on all \(24\) problems.

    The best alignment #

    Replacing the log-sum-exp in (5) with a max and keeping an argmax gives the single best path that collapses to a fixed target:

    \[\delta_t(s)=y_t(\ell'_s)+\max_{s'\in\{s,s-1,s-2\}} \big(\delta_{t-1}(s')\big). \qquad (12)\]

    The demo's best alignment carries \(7.1\%\) of the target's probability, and the remaining \(92.9\%\) is spread over other alignments of the same labels. That ratio is the difference between a path and a sequence, and it is why CTC needs a sum.

    Code walkthrough #

    Interleaving the blank #

        def _extended(self, target: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        """Interleave blanks: blank, l1, blank, l2, ..., lS, blank."""
        extended = np.full(2 * target.size + 1, self.blank, dtype=int)
        extended[1::2] = target
        return extended

    The odd positions receive the labels and every even position is a blank, so the lattice has a blank before the first label, between every pair, and after the last. The two sentinel blanks are what let the model emit silence at either end without inventing a label for it.

    The skip condition #

        def _skip(self, extended: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        """May state s be reached from s - 2, skipping the symbol between?"""
        allowed = np.zeros(extended.size, dtype=bool)
        index = np.arange(extended.size)
        later = index >= 2
        allowed[later] = ((extended[later] != self.blank)
                          & (extended[later] != extended[index[later] - 2]))
        return allowed

    A jump into a blank would skip a label, and a jump between two equal labels would skip the blank that keeps them separate. Both are excluded, and because the vector is built once, the four algorithms cannot disagree about the lattice. Getting this condition wrong is the classic CTC bug: the likelihood still runs, the numbers still look plausible, and repeated letters are silently impossible.

    The forward pass #

            for t in range(1, frames):
            previous = alpha[t - 1]
            stay = previous
            step = np.concatenate(([NEG], previous[:-1]))
            jump = np.concatenate(([NEG, NEG], previous[:-2])) if width > 2 else (
                np.full(width, NEG))
            jump = np.where(skip, jump, NEG)
            alpha[t] = log_probs[t, extended] + _lse_rows(np.stack([stay, step, jump]))

    The three candidates are one row of the previous table shifted by zero, one and two places. Stacking them into a (3, width) array and taking a log-sum-exp along axis zero is the vectorised form of the three-term sum in (5). Masked candidates are \(-\infty\), so they vanish from the sum without a branch.

    The backward pass #

            for t in range(frames - 2, -1, -1):
            following = beta[t + 1] + log_probs[t + 1, extended]
            stay = following
            step = np.concatenate((following[1:], [NEG]))
            jump = np.concatenate((following[2:], [NEG, NEG])) if width > 2 else (
                np.full(width, NEG))

    The shifts run the other way and the emission is folded into following, so beta[t] never contains frame \(t\)'s own symbol. That convention is what makes \(\alpha_t+\beta_t\) the probability of every path through state \(s\) at frame \(t\) without double-counting the frame.

    Collapsing a path #

        def _collapse(self, path) -> np.ndarray:
        """Merge repeats, then delete the blank."""
        output = []
        previous = -1
        for symbol in path:
            if symbol != previous and symbol != self.blank:
                output.append(int(symbol))
            previous = int(symbol)
        return np.array(output, dtype=int)

    previous starts at \(-1\), outside the channel range, so the first frame is never mistaken for a repeat. Merging and deleting in one pass is equivalent to the textbook two-pass definition, and it is the function the decoders and the alignment counter share, which is why the loss and the search cannot disagree about what a path means.

    Extending the beam #

                    for path, score in beams.items():
                    for symbol in range(channels):
                        extension = path + (symbol,)
                        value = score + float(frame[symbol])
                        old = candidates.get(extension)
                        candidates[extension] = (
                            value if old is None else float(np.logaddexp(old, value)))

    A tuple is the path, so two extensions that produce the same tuple hash together and their probabilities merge with logaddexp. The beam then keeps the best B tuples. This is a correct beam over alignments and a wasteful one: entries that collapse to the same output stay separate, which is exactly what the prefix version avoids.

    Counting the alignments #

                for state in range(extended.size):
                total = counts[state]
                if state >= 1:
                    total += counts[state - 1]
                if state >= 2 and skip[state]:
                    total += counts[state - 2]
                following[state] = total

    The same lattice, with sums of counts instead of log-probabilities, gives the exact number of frame paths. It is a useful sanity check and a useful warning: the count is binomial-ish and grows fast, so enumeration is only a reference for tiny cases.

    Complexity #

    Let \(T\) be the frames, \(C\) the channels, \(S\) the target length, \(B\) the beam width and \(N\) the batch size.

    operation time memory
    loss and gradient \(O(NTC + NTS)\) \(O(NTS)\)
    greedy decode \(O(NTC)\) \(O(NT)\)
    beam over alignments \(O(NTBC)\) \(O(NTB)\) paths
    prefix beam \(O(NTBC)\) \(O(NTB)\) prefixes
    best alignment \(O(NTS)\) \(O(NTS)\)
    exact marginal decode \(O(C^T)\) exponential

    The loss is linear in the length because the lattice has \(2S+1\) states and each state has three predecessors. The extended sequence is only twice the target length, so the cost is dominated by the frame-by-channel matrix rather than by the target.

    Where the time goes #

    The two searches share an asymptotic bound and differ by a large constant in quality. On one problem the exact answer has log probability \(-2.311\); a beam over alignments never finds it at widths \(1\) through \(32\) and keeps returning a sequence with \(86\%\) of that probability, while the prefix beam finds it at width \(4\). The alignment beam is spending its slots on different placements of blanks inside the same output; the prefix beam spends one slot per output.

    Failure modes #

    The blank at the wrong index. The blank is a channel like any other, and the constructor lets it be any index, but every collapse, target check and lattice builder has to agree on which one. Passing a target that contains the blank is a silent corruption in a careless implementation: here it raises.

    Skipping a repeated label. If the skip transition is allowed between two equal labels, the lattice claims that a • a and a a are the same path, and the model loses the ability to represent doubled letters. The condition in (3) is easy to write as "the destination is not blank" and forget the second half.

    Too few frames for the target. A target of length \(S\) with repeats needs at least \(S\) plus the number of adjacent equal pairs frames. Asking for more than the lattice can supply makes the forward table entirely \(-\infty\); the code raises instead of returning an infinite loss, because a nan in training is much harder to diagnose than an exception.

    Greedy decoding mistaken for the answer. The best path is not the best sequence, and the gap is not small: on the demo's random problems greedy agrees with the exact marginal decoder \(9\) times out of \(24\). Production systems decode with a beam and often with an external language model on top.

    Peaky posteriors and vanishing gradients. CTC's conditional independence assumption means the model can become extremely confident at every frame and still be wrong about the alignment. A frame whose posterior is one-hot contributes almost no gradient, and a model that collapses to the blank at every frame minimises a large part of the loss while learning nothing. Blank probability is worth monitoring directly.

    Beam collapse at the end. Summing the probabilities of beam entries that collapse to the same output is an approximation, because entries that were pruned are gone. A narrow alignment beam can therefore be worse than greedy on some problems, which is not a paradox: it is the same search with a different objective.

    The wrong normalisation for a batch. The gradient here is the mean over sequences, so it carries a factor \(1/N\). Mixing it with a per-sequence loss and a learning rate tuned for the other convention produces a model that trains at a different speed and looks like a bad schedule.

    Length leakage through padding. Padded frames must not contribute to the loss. Here the batch is rectangular by construction and every sequence uses all its frames, so the caller is responsible for masking; the demos use equal lengths, and a real pipeline has to pass lengths or slice the frames.

    Applications #

    Speech recognition. CTC is the loss that made end-to-end speech practical: acoustic frames go in, characters or phonemes come out, and no forced alignment is needed. DeepSpeech and wav2letter are the canonical systems, and the blank channel learns to absorb silence, noise and the frames between words.

    Handwriting and OCR. A line image is a sequence of columns, and the transcription gives no column boundaries. CTC trains the column model directly, and a beam with a character language model decodes it.

    Gesture and sign language. Video frames are labelled with a gloss sequence for the whole clip. The same lattice handles the unknown segmentation, and the blank absorbs the transition frames between signs.

    Keyword spotting. A small vocabulary and a blank-heavy signal make a lightweight CTC model practical on a device; the peak of the blank posterior is also a usable voice-activity detector.

    Music transcription. Audio frames to note sequences, with the blank standing for silence and the collapse merging the sustained part of a note. The repeated note problem is real here, which is why the separator blank matters.

    Neural sequence labelling without alignment. Any task where the input is longer than the output and the alignment is unknown — protein secondary structure, sensor event detection, video captioning — can use the same loss. The crf atom is the alternative when the labels are dense and a transition matrix is affordable.

    Distillation and pseudo-labelling. A CTC model's posterior over lattice states is a soft alignment, useful for bootstrapping a frame-level model or for confidence estimation. posterior exposes it directly.
