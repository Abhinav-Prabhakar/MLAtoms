    10 · Sequences/tier 1/5 variants/source

    Markov chains — order, memory, absorption and a sampler

    Intuition #

    A sequence is a chain when the next symbol depends on the recent past and not on the whole history. That is one assumption, and it is astonishing how much follows: the model becomes one square matrix, the likelihood becomes a product of matrix entries, the long-run behaviour becomes an eigenvector, and the question "how long until this gets stuck" becomes a matrix inverse.

    The five variants are the five questions this object is usually asked.

    How likely is this sequence? Count the transitions, normalise the rows, and multiply. That is first_order, and the estimator is nothing but counting — the maximum-likelihood estimate has a closed form, so the only real decisions are what to do with contexts that were never seen.

    What if one symbol of memory is not enough? Keep \(k\) of them. The state space becomes \(K^k\) contexts instead of \(K\) states, and the same counting works, but the cost grows exponentially in the memory. That is higher_order, and the demo makes the bill explicit: an exactly second-order process is predicted perfectly by an order-2 model and guessed at one third per symbol by an order-1 model.

    How long does it wander, and where does it end? Split the states into transient and absorbing, and invert \(I-Q\). The inverse's row sums are expected absorption times and its product with the absorption rows gives the probabilities of each destination. That is absorbing, and gambler's ruin is the canonical test: on \(0..4\) the expected duration from \(i\) is \(i(4-i)\) and the chance of ending at \(4\) is \(i/4\), both reproduced to machine precision.

    Where does it settle? The stationary distribution solves \(\pi P=\pi\), so it is a left eigenvector. That is stationary, and the demo shows both the convergence of power iteration and the case where it refuses to converge: on the period-two chain \(0\to1\to0\) power iteration alternates forever, while the eigenvector finds \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\) and so does power iteration on the lazy chain \((P+I)/2\).

    Can I build a chain with a distribution I choose? Turn the object around. Propose a move, accept it with probability (6), and the chain you get has your target as its stationary law. That is mcmc_chain, and the demo checks it in the least flattering way available: it runs Metropolis–Hastings, bins the samples, fits a first-order chain to the bins, and power-iterates the fitted matrix back to the histogram it came from. The agreement is \(0.0003\) in total variation.

          0.80       ┌─────────┐
     ┌──────────►│ state 0 │◄──┐
     │           └─────────┘   │ 0.05
     ▼ 0.15                 0.05│
  ┌─────────┐  0.20   ┌─────────┐
  │ state 1 │◄───────►│ state 2 │
  └─────────┘  0.15   └─────────┘
       ▲ 0.70              0.80
       └──────── state 1 ────────┘

    Rows sum to one, so the matrix is a set of conditional distributions; the diagonal is the sticky part and the off-diagonal is the drift. Five thousand draws recover every entry to within \(0.021\) and the stationary law to within \(0.004\).

    Variants #

    variant object fit main methods
    first_order \(K\times K\) matrix row-normalised counts log_probability, sample, stationary
    higher_order \(K^k\times K\) matrix counts over \(k\)-symbol contexts same, with \(k\)-symbol start
    absorbing transient block \(Q\) loaded or counted absorption_analysis
    stationary left eigenvector power iteration or eig stationary(method=...)
    mcmc_chain proposal + target Metropolis–Hastings run(steps, start, burn_in)

    first_order is the default and the base case: one symbol of memory, maximum-likelihood counting, and a likelihood that factorises. Everything else in this file is a variation on it.

    higher_order is the same estimator with the state redefined. A sequence’s context is its last \(k\) symbols, encoded in base \(K\) so that a context is an integer and the count table stays a matrix. It is the honest way to say "the process has memory longer than one step" without inventing a hidden state.

    absorbing is an analysis rather than a model: you supply a chain that has absorbing states and it tells you the expected time to absorption and the destination probabilities. It refuses chains with no absorbing state and chains with no transient state, because both extreme cases make the block decomposition trivial and misleading.

    stationary is also an analysis: the same chain, asked about its long-run behaviour. Two methods, because they fail differently — power iteration is cheap and reveals periodicity by oscillating, while the eigen-decomposition always returns the eigenvector but cannot tell you whether iteration would have found it.

    mcmc_chain inverts the direction of inference. Every other variant estimates \(P\) from data and derives \(\pi\); this one starts from a target \(\pi\propto e^{-U}\) and constructs a \(P\) that leaves it invariant. It is the only variant whose "fit" is a simulation.

    Derivation #

    The chain and its likelihood #

    An order-\(k\) chain satisfies

    \[\Pr(s_t \mid s_{

    for every \(t>k\). Everything downstream is a consequence of that factorisation: the joint probability of a sequence is the product of its conditional probabilities, so the log-likelihood is a sum over transitions and separates into independent rows.

    Writing \(n_{ij}\) for the number of times context \(i\) was followed by symbol \(j\), the maximum-likelihood estimate is the row-normalised count,

    \[\hat P_{ij} = \frac{n_{ij}}{\sum_{j'} n_{ij'}}, \qquad (2)\]

    which is just the empirical conditional distribution. Additive smoothing replaces \(n_{ij}\) by \(n_{ij}+\alpha\) before normalising; \(\alpha=0\) is maximum likelihood and \(\alpha>0\) keeps an unseen transition from carrying probability zero, at the cost of biasing every seen one.

    The implementation is the loop in (2) and nothing else:

            for t in range(order, symbols.size):
            context = self._key(symbols[t - order:t], order, states)
            counts[context, symbols[t]] += 1.0

    _key is a base-\(K\) positional encoding of the context, so a context is an integer and counts is a dense matrix. Two things can go wrong: a context that was never observed has a zero row, and a sequence shorter than the order has no transitions at all. The first is handled by the fallback below; the second is refused.

            matrix = counts + self.smoothing
        totals = matrix.sum(axis=1)
        estimated = np.zeros_like(matrix)
        observed = totals > 0.0
        estimated[observed] = matrix[observed] / totals[observed, None]
        estimated[~observed] = 1.0 / states

    The last line is a modelling decision that maximum likelihood cannot make: an unobserved context has no information, so the fitted chain gives it the uniform distribution rather than an arbitrary one. Without it the row would be all zeros and the log-likelihood of any sequence passing through that context would be \(-\infty\).

    Stationarity #

    A distribution \(\pi\) is stationary when one step leaves it unchanged,

    \[\pi P = \pi, \qquad \sum_i \pi_i = 1, \qquad \pi \ge 0, \qquad (3)\]

    so \(\pi\) is a left eigenvector of \(P\) with eigenvalue one. Existence is guaranteed for a finite chain — the all-ones vector is a right eigenvector, so one is always an eigenvalue — but uniqueness needs irreducibility, and convergence of the iterates needs aperiodicity.

    Power iteration is (3) applied repeatedly from an arbitrary start: \(\pi_{t+1} = \pi_t P\). Its error shrinks like \(|\lambda_2|^t\), where \(\lambda_2\) is the second-largest eigenvalue modulus, so the demo's aperiodic chain with \(|\lambda_2| = 0.473\) converges to twelve digits in about forty steps. A periodic chain has \(|\lambda_2|=1\) and the iteration never settles.

            for _ in range(iterations):
            nxt = vector @ companion
            if np.abs(nxt - vector).sum() < tol:
                vector = nxt
                break
            vector = nxt

    For an order-\(k\) chain the object being iterated is not \(P\) but the companion matrix on \(K^k\) contexts, whose entry from context \(i\) to context \(j\) is nonzero only when \(j\) is \(i\) shifted by one symbol plus a new symbol. Building it is \(O(K^{k+1})\) and sparse in structure, but dense in storage — which is exactly why high-order chains are rarely used beyond order three or four.

    The eigen route is shorter and less informative:

                values, vectors = np.linalg.eig(companion.T)
            index = int(np.argmin(np.abs(values - 1.0)))
            vector = np.real(vectors[:, index])
            vector = np.abs(vector)
            return vector / vector.sum()

    The np.abs is not cosmetic. The eigenvector is real up to numerical error and its overall sign is arbitrary, so a raw eigenvector can come back all-negative; taking the modulus and renormalising makes the answer a distribution regardless. The argmin picks the eigenvalue closest to one, which is the right choice numerically — comparing to exactly \(1.0\) can fail on a matrix whose eigenvalues are computed as \(0.9999999997\).

    Absorption #

    Suppose the states are ordered so that the \(t\) transient ones come first. The transition matrix is block triangular,

    \[P = \begin{pmatrix} Q & R \\ 0 & I \end{pmatrix},\]

    where \(Q\) is transient-to-transient, \(R\) is transient-to-absorbing, and the absorbing block is the identity. The probability of being in transient state \(j\) after \(n\) steps, having started at \(i\), is \((Q^n)_{ij}\), so the expected number of visits to \(j\) is \(\sum_n (Q^n)_{ij}\), and the sum of the geometric series is the fundamental matrix

    \[N = (I - Q)^{-1} = I + Q + Q^2 + \cdots \qquad (4)\]

    Two consequences follow immediately:

    \[\mathbf{t} = N\mathbf{1}, \qquad B = N R, \qquad (5)\]

    where \(t_i\) is the expected number of steps before absorption starting from \(i\), and \(B_{ik}\) is the probability of being absorbed in state \(k\). Both are exact for a finite chain — no asymptotics, no simulation.

            q = matrix[np.ix_(transient, transient)]
        r = matrix[np.ix_(transient, absorbing)]
        fundamental = np.linalg.inv(np.eye(q.shape[0]) - q)
        times = fundamental.sum(axis=1)
        destinations = fundamental @ r

    np.ix_ is doing real work here: the transient and absorbing index sets are interleaved in general, so slicing needs the cross product, not two independent slices. On gambler's ruin the two rows come out as \((3, 4, 3)\) steps and

    start expected steps \(P(\text{end at }0)\) \(P(\text{end at }4)\)
    1 3 0.75 0.25
    2 4 0.50 0.50
    3 3 0.25 0.75

    which are \(i(4-i)\) and \((1-i/4,\,i/4)\) exactly.

    Metropolis–Hastings #

    The sampler is the same equation read backwards. A proposal distribution \(q(x'\mid x)\) is easy to sample from; the target \(p\) is only known up to a constant. Propose \(x'\sim q(\cdot\mid x)\) and accept it with probability

    \[\alpha = \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{p(x')\,q(x\mid x')}{p(x)\,q(x'\mid x)}\right). \qquad (6)\]

    The ratio is what makes the unknown normalising constant cancel. The chain on states has \(p\) as its stationary distribution: detailed balance holds by construction, \(p(x)q(x'\mid x)\alpha = p(x')q(x\mid x')\alpha'\), and detailed balance plus irreducibility implies (3). With a symmetric proposal the \(q\) terms cancel too and the rule is just \(\min(1, p(x')/p(x))\), which is the random-walk Metropolis algorithm.

                proposal = x + self.proposal_scale * rng.standard_normal(x.size)
            ratio = float(self.target(proposal)) - float(self.target(x))
            if math.log(rng.uniform()) < ratio:
                x = proposal
                accepted += 1

    The comparison is done in log space with the same decision rule as log(u) < log α, because probabilities of interest routinely underflow. The proposal scale is the entire tuning problem: too small and the chain crawls with near-certain acceptance, too large and it bounces off the target with near-certain rejection. The demo's correlated two-dimensional Gaussian accepts \(38\%\) of proposals at scale \(1.2\), which is close to the rule of thumb that a random-walk Metropolis in \(d\) dimensions wants an acceptance rate near \(0.234\) for large \(d\) and considerably higher for small \(d\).

    Code walkthrough #

    Two ways to sample a chain #

                for t in range(1, length):
                state = int(rng.choice(states, p=self.transition_[state]))
                output[t] = state

    Sampling is one rng.choice per step, and the state variable is the whole memory of an order-1 chain. The order-\(k\) branch keeps a tuple of the last \(k\) symbols and slides it, which is the same loop with a wider state.

    The companion matrix #

                        target = self._key(np.asarray(digits[1:] + (nxt,)), order,
                                       states)
                    companion[key, target] = probability

    Shifting the window is the only structure an order-\(k\) chain has: from context \((a,b)\) you can only reach \((b,c)\), and the probability of doing so is the matrix entry for the new symbol. This is why the companion matrix has exactly \(K^{k+1}\) nonzeros out of \(K^{2k}\) entries — at order ten over a three-symbol alphabet that is \(0.005\%\) dense, and dense storage is the wrong data structure long before that point.

    Reading a chain out of a sampler #

        bins = (np.digitize(samples[:, 0], edges_x[1:-1]) * 4
            + np.digitize(samples[:, 1], edges_y[1:-1]))
    chain = MarkovChain(variant="first_order").fit(bins)
    stationary = chain.stationary()
    frequencies = np.bincount(bins, minlength=16) / bins.size

    The demo's last check is the one worth remembering. Binning turns a continuous sampler into a finite chain; fitting that chain by counting gives an estimate of its transition matrix; power iteration gives that estimate's stationary distribution. Comparing it to the histogram is a comparison of two things derived from the same samples, so agreement is not evidence about the target — but it is evidence that the two halves of this file, estimation and analysis, are consistent. The total-variation distance is \(0.0003\), which is what consistency looks like.

    Complexity #

    Let \(K\) be the number of symbols, \(k\) the order, \(n\) the sequence length, \(T\) the number of sampler steps and \(d\) the dimension of the target.

    operation time memory
    fit, order \(k\) \(O(n)\) counting \(O(K^{k+1})\)
    log_probability \(O(nk)\) \(O(1)\)
    sample \(O(nK)\) expected \(O(n)\)
    stationary, power \(O(I\,K^{2k})\) \(O(K^{2k})\)
    stationary, eigen \(O(K^{3k})\) \(O(K^{2k})\)
    absorption_analysis \(O(t^3)\) \(O(t^2)\)
    run \(O(Td)\) \(O(Td)\)

    The estimation is linear and trivial. Everything expensive is in the state space: the companion matrix is \(K^k\times K^k\), so both stationary methods are exponential in the memory length, and absorption is cubic in the number of transient states because it inverts a matrix. The sampler is linear in the number of steps, since each step costs one target evaluation.

    Failure modes #

    Contexts you never saw. With \(K^k\) contexts and \(n\) transitions, most contexts are empty unless \(n\gg K^k\). The default fills them with the uniform distribution, which is defensible for a short sequence and dangerous for a long one: a genuine deterministic transition that happened to be missing from the training sample is assigned probability \(1/K\). The smoothing parameter is the knob, and the diagnostic is the fraction of contexts with zero counts.

    Order selection is a likelihood comparison with a price. The demo's second order gets \(0\) on held-out data while the first order gets \(-996\), but it also has three times the parameters. That gap is enormous because the process is exactly second-order; on real data the improvement is usually small enough that the number of parameters matters, and the honest comparison is held-out log-likelihood or BIC, not training likelihood, which always prefers more memory.

    Periodicity stops power iteration, not stationarity. The period-two chain has a perfectly good stationary distribution, \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\), and power iteration started at state \(0\) visits only one state and oscillates forever between the two basis vectors. This is a property of the iteration, not of the chain, and the fix is structural: make the chain lazy, \(P\to(P+I)/2\), which costs nothing but keeps every state reachable at every step. The eigen route sidesteps it entirely and therefore hides it, which is the argument for having both.

    Reducibility makes the stationary distribution non-unique. A chain with two absorbing states and no path between them has infinitely many stationary distributions — every mixture of the two. eig will return one of them, chosen by numerical accident, and the answer is not wrong so much as undefined. That is why absorption_analysis refuses a chain with no transient state and why stationary refuses the absorbing variant outright rather than return a number that would be mistaken for an answer.

    Proposal scale is the whole sampler. Too small and the acceptance rate approaches one while the chain explores a tiny neighbourhood; too large and the acceptance rate approaches zero while the chain stands still. Both failures look identical in a trace plot of one coordinate — a slowly mixing chain — and both are fixed by looking at the acceptance rate and the autocorrelation, not at the histogram. In several dimensions a single scalar scale is also the wrong parameterisation: a correlated target needs a proposal shaped like the covariance.

    Burn-in is a heuristic, and so is one chain. The demo discards two thousand steps from a target that starts at its mean, which is the easy case. A chain started far out needs a burn-in long enough to reach the typical set, and no diagnostic computed from a single chain can detect that it did not. The honest practice is several chains from dispersed starts and a comparison of their answers; the effective sample size, not the number of steps, is the budget.

    MCMC is exact in the limit and approximate at every finite step. The samples are correlated, so the Monte Carlo error shrinks like \(\sqrt{\mathrm{ESS}}\), not \(\sqrt{T}\), and the demo's covariance entries land within \(0.012\) of the truth after twenty thousand steps. That is fine for a two-dimensional Gaussian; a hundred-dimensional target with a badly chosen proposal can need orders of magnitude more, and no amount of computing fixes a proposal that is structurally wrong.

    Applications #

    Language modelling and text generation. The \(n\)-gram model of chapter 10 is exactly the higher_order variant with \(k \in \{1,2,3\}\) and a smoothing scheme; the stationary distribution is a crude model of what the generator drifts toward, and the kneser-ney atom in this chapter is the same counting problem with a better backoff.

    PageRank and web graphs. A random surfer is a Markov chain on pages, and the PageRank vector is its stationary distribution; the damping factor is precisely a lazy-chain correction that guarantees aperiodicity and irreducibility. Everything in the stationary section applies, including the second-eigenvalue convergence rate, which for the web is famously near one.

    Queues, inventory and reliability. States are system conditions and transitions are events; the stationary distribution is the long-run fraction of time in each condition, and the transient absorption analysis is the answer to "probability this system ever reaches total failure". Both are exact and both are standard in operations research.

    Bayesian computation. Metropolis–Hastings, Gibbs sampling and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo are all chains built to have a posterior as their stationary distribution. The acceptance ratio here is the simplest instance; the machinery of diagnostics, effective sample size and convergence checks is the same in all of them.

    Hidden Markov models. The hmm atom in this chapter is a Markov chain on latent states plus an emission model; forward–backward is the chain's filtering recursion and Baum–Welch is the counting estimator of (2) applied to expected counts instead of observed ones.

    Reinforcement learning and control. A policy induces a Markov chain on states, and its value function solves a linear system whose matrix is built from the same transition matrix; the absorption analysis is the undiscounted special case of policy evaluation for an episodic task.
