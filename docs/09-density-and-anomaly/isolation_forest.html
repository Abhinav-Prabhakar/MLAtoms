    09 · Density & anomaly/tier 1/4 variants/source

    Isolation forest

    Intuition #

    Cut the data set in half at random, repeatedly. A point in the middle of a cloud survives many cuts because there is always more cloud around it; a point on the edge, or in a gap, is alone after three or four. Count the cuts, and you have an anomaly score that never estimates a density, never computes a distance and never looks at a neighbour list.

    The algorithm, in four lines:

    \[\text{split a node by projecting on a random } n,\ \text{cutting at } u \sim \mathrm{Uniform}[\min_i x_i \cdot n,\ \max_i x_i \cdot n] \qquad (1)\]
    \[h(x) = \text{depth of the leaf } x \text{ lands in} + c(\text{size of that leaf}) \qquad (2)\]
    \[c(m) = 2H_{m-1} - \frac{2(m-1)}{m}, \qquad H_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{1}{j} \qquad (3)\]
    \[s(x) = 2^{-E[h(x)] / c(\psi)}, \qquad \psi = \text{points per tree} \qquad (4)\]

    Equation (2) is the only subtle one. A tree stops when its node has one point, or when it hits the depth limit \(\lceil \log_2 \psi \rceil\); if it stopped at every single point the trees would be too deep to build. The leaf's remaining points are all isolated at the same depth, so (2) adds the depth that would have been needed to separate them on average, which is \(c(m)\) — the expected isolation depth of a random BST on \(m\) points. A leaf of one point adds nothing, and a leaf of a hundred adds about seven levels the tree did not build.

    Equation (3) is the normaliser that turns a path length into a calibrated number. For a typical point, \(E[h] \approx c(\psi)\), so the exponent is about \(-1\) and \(s \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\); for an isolated point \(E[h] \approx 0\) and \(s \approx 1\). The score is bounded, monotone and — this is the useful part — comparable across data sets of different sizes, because both the numerator and the denominator grow like the logarithm of the sample size. A threshold of 0.6 means something; a mean path length of 9 does not.

       depth 0   ────────────────┬──────────────────────
                             │
   depth 1        ┌──────────┴─────────┐
                  │                    │
   depth 2    ────┴─────         ──────┴──────
                  │                    │
   depth 3        x               ┌────┴────┐
                                  │         │
                               · · · ·   · · · ·    x is alone after 3 cuts

    Two structural choices follow, and they are the two variants worth knowing about. The cuts are axis-parallel by default: every split is orthogonal to a coordinate axis, so a point diagonal to the cloud needs more cuts than a point on an axis at the same distance. That is a bias, and extended removes most of it by cutting with random hyperplanes instead. The trees are also subsampled: each one sees \(\psi = 256\) points, not the data set, because a tree grown on a million points spends its depth resolving density that no anomaly cares about. max_samples exists so that the cost of that choice can be measured instead of assumed.

    Variants #

    variant split direction points per tree output measured
    iforest one axis (1) 256 \(s \in (0,1]\) AUC 1.000, threshold 0.481
    extended hyperplane, \(\ell+1\) axes 256 \(s \in (0,1]\) AUC 1.000, angular bias −64%
    anomaly_score one axis 256 \(s - \text{threshold}\) same AUC, 10.0% flagged
    max_samples one axis the full sample \(s \in (0,1]\) AUC 1.000, planted score 0.738

    The detection table is 300 standard normal points with 10 planted on a ring of radius 5.5 to 6, contamination 0.1. All four variants find the ring — it is not a hard problem — and the differences are in what the number means rather than in whether it works.

    anomaly_score is the same computation with the score shifted by the training threshold: the furthest planted point scores 0.723 under iforest and 0.242 under anomaly_score, and 0.481 is the threshold, so zero is the decision boundary and predict is the sign. Exactly 10.0% of the training set is flagged, because the threshold is that quantile. That is worth stating plainly: the contamination parameter does not detect anything, it sets the false-positive budget, and a forest with contamination=0.1 will flag ten percent of any data set, including one with no anomalies in it.

    max_samples changes the estimator, and the effect is measurable in two places. With \(\psi = 16\) the AUC falls to 0.958 and the seed-to-seed spread rises to 0.020: a tree of sixteen cannot separate a ring point from the edge of a 600-point cloud. At 256 the AUC is 1.000 and the spread is 0.000, and the full sample — 612 points, so 2.4 times the work per tree — gives the same AUC without moving the spread off zero. The second effect is on the scale of the score: the planted points average 0.616 at \(\psi = 16\), 0.680 at 64, 0.728 at 256 and 0.738 at the full sample, because \(E[h]\) changes with the tree size while \(c(\psi)\) is the normaliser. A threshold fitted at one sample size is not a threshold at another — which is why the variant normalises by max_samples_ and not by a constant.

    Derivation #

    1. The tree #

    A node holds a set of points. If it holds one point, or the depth limit is reached, it becomes a leaf. Otherwise a direction \(n\) and an offset \(u\) are drawn, and the points are split by the sign of \(x \cdot n - u\). For iforest, \(n\) is a coordinate axis and \(u\) is uniform between the smallest and largest projection in the node — the standard rule, and the one that makes the tree's structure independent of the data's density inside the node. For extended, \(n\) is a random hyperplane normal.

    The recursion terminates with probability one because a uniform offset strictly between the minimum and maximum projection separates at least one point on each side, and the depth limit caps the work regardless. Two degenerate cases are handled by returning a leaf: a node whose projections are all equal (duplicates, or a direction along which the node has no extent) and a node of size one.

    2. The normaliser #

    \(c(m)\) is the expected depth at which a random cut sequence isolates one of \(m\) points, equivalently the expected depth of an unsuccessful search in a random binary search tree. It satisfies

    \[c(1) = 0, \qquad c(m) = c(m-1) + \frac{2}{m} \qquad (5)\]

    whose solution is (3): the harmonic sum, so \(c(m) \approx 2\log m + 2\gamma - 2\) for large \(m\). The demo checks the recurrence for every \(m\) up to 256 and checks \(c(2) = 1\) exactly; at \(m = 256\) the exact value is 10.2487 against 10.2448 for the \(\log\) approximation, a gap of \(3.9 \times 10^{-3}\) that is the harmonic sum's tail.

    The choice to compute the harmonic sum exactly rather than use the log form is not pedantry. Leaf sizes in a real tree are small — the depth limit guarantees it — and at \(m = 2\) the exact value is 1 while the approximation is 0.154. The leaf correction is added to every query, so an error there is a systematic offset in every score.

    3. The score, and what "calibrated" means #

    \(s\) in (4) is a monotone decreasing map from mean path length to \((0, 1]\), with \(s = 0.5\) at \(E[h] = c(\psi)\). It is not a probability, despite the range: the demo's forest scores a standard normal cloud at 0.447 on average and 0.401 at the centre, both under the 0.5 that the normaliser suggests, because a tree is capped at \(\lceil \log_2 \psi \rceil\) levels and the leaf correction only approximates the depth an unlimited tree would have used. What the scale buys is comparability: doubling the sample size changes \(E[h]\) and \(c(\psi)\) by the same logarithm, so the score of a point stays put while a raw path length would grow.

    The saturation in the demo's ray — 0.551 at radius 2, 0.660 at radius 5 and the same 0.660 at radius 9 — is the same fact seen from the other side. Isolation counts cuts, not distance, so once a point is outside the cloud its score stops growing. An isolation forest ranks "unusual" but does not measure "how far".

    4. Why axis-parallel cuts are biased #

    Every split is perpendicular to an axis, so a region that is diagonal to the coordinate frame is harder to cut than one that is aligned with it. For a point at fixed distance from a cloud, the number of cuts needed depends on the angle. The demo measures it on 720 points of a circle of radius 4 around a normal cloud: the score runs from 0.590 to 0.759 around the circle under iforest, a spread of 0.169, and from 0.652 to 0.712 under extended, a spread of 0.060.

    Extended isolation forest replaces the axis with a random normal vector:

    \[n = \frac{g}{\lVert g \rVert}, \qquad g_j \sim N(0,1) \text{ on } \ell + 1 \text{ coordinates}, \quad g_j = 0 \text{ otherwise} \qquad (6)\]

    with the offset drawn uniformly in the projection range as before. The extension level \(\ell\) controls how many coordinates participate: \(\ell = 0\) has one non-zero coordinate and reproduces the axis-parallel forest exactly, and \(\ell \ge d - 1\) is a fully extended hyperplane. In two dimensions the demo's default of 1 is already full. The remaining 0.060 of variation is six times the seed-to-seed noise at a fixed point, so a little anisotropy survives: some of it is the split geometry and some is the 400-point cloud's own asymmetry, which no orientation of the cuts can average away.

    5. Subsampling, swamping and masking #

    The sample size per tree is a variance–cost trade. With \(\psi\) points the tree has depth \(O(\log \psi)\) and the score's variance over trees falls like \(1/\sqrt{t}\), where \(t\) is the number of trees — so \(\psi = 256\) and \(t = 100\) is the published combination, and the demo's sweep shows why: at \(\psi = 16\) a tree cannot see enough of the cloud to separate its edge from a planted point, and the AUC drops to 0.958.

    The two failure modes the subsampling is meant to avoid are swamping and masking. Swamping is a normal point being scored anomalous because the anomalies changed the path lengths around it; masking is several anomalies hiding one another because a region containing them looks, to a tree, like a region with more points in it. Both are consequences of the score being a property of the sample rather than of the point, and both are visible in the demo's duplicate experiment: adding a copy of a point that is flagged at contamination 0.1 makes both copies unflagged, because the pair now looks like a small cluster. The density-based detectors in the rest of this chapter have the same response, which is a reminder that "isolation" is not the same as "density" but is still a property of the sample.

    Code walkthrough #

    1. The normaliser, exactly #

        if size <= 1:
        return 0.0
    harmonic = sum(1.0 / k for k in range(1, int(size)))
    return 2.0 * harmonic - 2.0 * (size - 1) / size

    Equation (3), with the harmonic sum done rather than approximated, and the whole function memoised with functools.cache because it is called once per leaf per query. The int(size) is defensive: leaf sizes come from len(indices) and are already Python ints, but a caller passing a numpy integer would otherwise make range fine and the cache key a different object. Returning zero for size <= 1 is what makes a single-point leaf contribute exactly its depth.

    2. Choosing the split direction #

            aim = 1 if self.variant != "extended" else min(
            self.extension_level + 1, d)
        normal = np.zeros(d)
        chosen = rng.choice(d, size=aim, replace=False)
        normal[chosen] = rng.normal(size=aim)
        length = float(np.linalg.norm(normal))
        if length == 0.0:  # measure-zero, but the caller must not divide by it
            normal[chosen[0]] = 1.0
            length = 1.0
        return normal / length

    This is the entire difference between the first two variants, and it is four lines: draw a Gaussian vector supported on aim coordinates, normalise it. For iforest, aim = 1 and the vector is a unit basis vector, so the projection is one coordinate and the split is axis-parallel. Making the margin aim = 1 rather than a special case keeps the traversal — which always does a dot product — free of a branch. The zero-norm guard is measure-zero and would be dead code in exact arithmetic; it exists because rng.normal(size=aim) can return all zeros in floating point only if every draw underflows, and a nan normal would poison every path in the tree silently.

    3. Growing a node #

            rows = X[indices]
        normal = self._draw_normal(rng)
        projection = rows @ normal
        low, high = float(projection.min()), float(projection.max())
        if high - low <= 0.0:
            return node  # every point projects to the same place: a leaf
        node.normal = normal
        node.offset = float(rng.uniform(low, high))
        goes_left = projection <= node.offset

    The projection is computed once for the node and reused for both the split decision and the child indices, which is why indices is kept as an array of row numbers rather than a list of points. <= versus < puts points exactly on the offset to the left; with a continuous uniform offset that happens only for duplicated values on the split coordinate, where the choice is arbitrary but has to be made consistently or the recursion would not shrink. The degenerate leaf is the important branch: without it, a node of identical points would recurse until the depth limit and return a tree whose paths are pure noise.

    4. Walking a point down a tree #

            for tree in self.trees_:
            for i, row in enumerate(X):
                node = tree
                while node.normal is not None:
                    side = node.left if float(row @ node.normal) <= node.offset else node.right
                    node = side
                total[i] += node.depth + average_path(node.size)

    Equation (2). The while is a loop rather than a recursion because the depth is bounded by \(\lceil \log_2 \psi \rceil\) and the function is called \(t \times m\) times; a recursive Python call per level would dominate the runtime. node.depth was stored at build time rather than recomputed, and average_path(node.size) is the leaf correction — the only place where a leaf of many points differs from a leaf of one. Note the loop is over points inside the loop over trees, not the other way round: every point walks one tree before the next tree is touched, so that tree's nodes stay hot instead of being reloaded \(m\) times.

    5. The score and the variant #

            depth = self.path_length(X)
        return 2.0 ** (-depth / average_path(self.max_samples_))

            scores = self._calibrated(X)
        if self.variant == "anomaly_score":
            return scores - self.threshold_
        return scores

    Equation (4), and then the one line that makes anomaly_score a variant rather than a documentation note. The threshold is fitted in fit as the 1 - contamination quantile of the training scores, so predict returns -1 exactly when the calibrated score exceeds it. Keeping _calibrated separate from score_samples is what lets the first three variants return a probability-like number and the fourth return a decision function without duplicating the arithmetic.

    6. Fitting #

            if self.max_samples is None:
            size = n if self.variant == "max_samples" else 256
        else:
            size = self.max_samples
        self.max_samples_ = int(min(size, n))
        self.n_features_ = d
        self.depth_limit_ = int(math.ceil(math.log2(self.max_samples_)))

    The variant's default sample size, the cap at \(n\), and the depth limit that makes the leaf correction necessary. max_samples_ is stored rather than recomputed because both average_path(self.max_samples_) and the depth limit depend on the effective size after the cap — a forest fitted on 100 points with max_samples=256 must normalise by \(c(100)\), not \(c(256)\), or every score is systematically too high.

    Complexity #

    For \(n\) points, \(d\) dimensions, \(t\) trees and \(\psi\) points per tree:

    stage time memory
    subsample each tree \(O(t\psi)\) \(O(\psi)\)
    build one tree \(O(\psi \log \psi \cdot d)\) \(O(\psi)\)
    fit the ensemble \(O(t\,\psi \log \psi \cdot d)\) \(O(t\psi)\)
    threshold \(O(nt\log\psi \cdot d)\) \(O(n)\)
    score \(O(mt\log\psi \cdot d)\) \(O(1)\) per point

    The \(d\) in the build and score rows is the dot product: the code always projects onto a full vector, so an axis-parallel forest pays \(d\) multiplications per split where one comparison would do. Production implementations store the feature index for the axis-parallel case, which is why their constant is so much better in high dimension. The dependence on \(n\) is only through the subsampling and the threshold; a forest's per-tree cost does not grow with the data set, which is the point of \(\psi\).

    Failure modes #

    The score saturates, so it cannot rank the far field #

    On the demo's ray the score is 0.551 at radius 2, 0.660 at radius 5 and 0.660 at radius 9. The method counts cuts, and once a point is outside the cloud the number of cuts stops changing. Consequences: an isolation forest is a good detector and a poor ranking of how extreme the extreme points are; two outliers a hundred and a million units away get the same score, and the "most anomalous" point in a report may be the one that happened to sit in the sparsest region rather than the one that is furthest out. If an ordering in the tail matters, use a distance-based score (LOF, Mahalanobis) on the points the forest flags, or use the forest as a filter and rank inside its output.

    Axis-parallel cuts score by direction #

    The circle experiment: 0.590 to 0.759 around a fixed radius, a spread of 0.169, while the seed-to-seed noise at a fixed point is 0.011. That is a real bias — a point at 45° to the axes is scored as less anomalous than a point on an axis at the same distance — and it is why extended exists. Fully extended hyperplanes reduce the spread to 0.060 but do not eliminate it, because the offset is still uniform over a projection range that depends on the cloud's shape. On data whose anomalies are isolated along diagonals (correlated sensors, image patches) the bias systematically understates them.

    Masking, and duplicates that hide #

    Adding a duplicate of a point that is flagged at contamination 0.1 makes both copies unflagged: the score moves by −0.088, and the pair now looks like a two-point cluster rather than an isolated point. This is masking, and it is the reason the method is not a density estimator in the way KDE is — but it is not a defence against duplication either. Near-duplicate rows from a deduplication step, or a burst of identical events, will lower each other's scores.

    The sample size is part of the model #

    max_samples is not a speed knob. At \(\psi = 16\) the AUC on the demo's data falls to 0.958 with a seed spread of 0.020, and the planted points average 0.680 at \(\psi = 64\) against 0.728 at 256. A threshold fitted with one setting is wrong under another; retrain, do not reuse. The published 256 is a compromise between variance (small \(\psi\)) and the swamping that a large \(\psi\) lets in, and it is the number the paper's experiments support, not a theorem.

    Contamination is a budget, not a detector #

    contamination=0.1 flags 10.0% of the training set by construction, on any data, including a sample of pure noise. If the true rate is 0.1% then 99% of the flagged points are false positives caused by the threshold rather than by the score. Fit with a contamination that reflects the operational budget, or drop the parameter and use the score's quantiles downstream where the cost of a false positive is known.

    Applications #

    • Tabular anomaly detection. The default first attempt on structured data, because its cost is bounded by the subsample rather than by the data set, it handles mixed scales without normalisation, and it needs no distance metric. Fraud, intrusion detection, sensor faults and quality control all start here.
    • Feature engineering for supervised models. The score, the mean path length and the per-tree depth are useful features even when the forest is not the detector; a supervised model on top of them often beats either alone.
    • Cheap pre-filtering. Use the forest to discard the obvious 90% and run an expensive detector (a density model, a reconstruction autoencoder) on the rest. The saturation failure mode is harmless in this role: the filter only has to rank, not to measure.
    • Streaming and distributed settings. Each tree is independent and built on a bounded sample, so the ensemble shards trivially and can be refit on a sliding window without touching the history. That is the practical reason it beat density-based detectors in production even before it beat them on accuracy.
    • Where it is a trap. Local anomalies — a point that is normal globally and strange relative to its own neighbourhood — are invisible, because isolation is a global property of the tree. That is exactly what LOF, the next atom, is for. And anything where the magnitude of the anomaly matters, per the saturation failure mode.
