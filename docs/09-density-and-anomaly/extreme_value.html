    09 · Density & anomaly/tier 3/5 variants/source

    Extreme value theory — fit the tail instead of counting it

    Intuition #

    A rank-based detector answers one question and refuses the next. How unusual is a value of 11 when the largest of two thousand observations is 7.8? The empirical CDF says never, because it has never seen 11. That is not an answer; it is the absence of one. Extreme value theory replaces the empty count with a fitted probability, and a fitted probability can be turned into a score, a threshold and a return period.

    The theory comes in two shapes, and they are the same theory seen from two sides:

    • Block maxima. Cut the series into blocks, keep the maximum of each, and model those maxima. The Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem says their distribution converges to the generalised extreme value (GEV) family, which contains Gumbel, Fréchet and Weibull as the three signs of one shape parameter.
    • Peaks over threshold. Keep everything above a high threshold and model the excesses. The Pickands–Balkema–de Haan theorem says those excesses converge to the generalised Pareto distribution (GPD), with the same shape parameter.

    Two routes, five estimators: the GPD by probability-weighted moments (pot) or by maximum likelihood (gpd); the GEV by L-moments (block_maxima) or by maximum likelihood (gev); and a one-parameter straight-line fit on the return-level plot (return_level).

      x_T
   │                        ╱
   │                    ╱
   │                ╱          each point is a value
   │            ╱              exceeded once every T
   │        ╱                  observations, on average
   │    ╱
   └──┬─────┬──────┬───────┬──────► log T
     10   100   1000   10000

    That line is the product. On the demo's exponential sample it is fitted to the top 10% of 5000 observations and used to predict a level exceeded once every thousand observations, and it lands within 6% of the truth.

    The other half of the story is what the tail does when it ends. A distribution with a finite upper endpoint has a negative shape parameter, and the fit places that endpoint. On a beta\((2,2)\) sample the true endpoint is one and the two GPD fits land at \(0.9964\) and \(0.9910\); a query at \(1.2\) then gets probability zero and infinite score. The model can say "impossible", which no rank-based method can.

    The demos also make the choices visible. On two thousand exponential draws with twenty injected points at 11, the fitted tail flags all twenty and one false positive, while the empirical count above 11 is zero. That is the trade: a rank method is assumption-free and cannot extrapolate; this one extrapolates and therefore has to be right about the shape.

    Variants #

    variant model fit shape
    pot GPD on exceedances probability-weighted moments estimated
    gpd GPD on exceedances maximum likelihood estimated
    block_maxima GEV on block maxima L-moments estimated
    gev GEV on block maxima maximum likelihood estimated
    return_level exponential tail least squares on the return-level plot fixed at zero

    pot is the default and the workhorse. Probability-weighted moments are closed-form, need no optimiser and are stable down to a few dozen exceedances. It is the estimator to reach for when the threshold has already been chosen.

    gpd fits the same model by likelihood. It is asymptotically efficient and more sensitive to the largest exceedances, which is either the point or the problem depending on how much you trust them. It is started from the probability-weighted moments, which is what makes it reliable on the samples where it works at all.

    block_maxima is the classical route: L-moments of the block maxima, which are themselves closed-form and far more stable than likelihood in small samples. It throws away everything in a block except the largest value, which is a real cost when the data are expensive.

    gev is the likelihood version of the same model, again started from the L-moments. It uses the maxima more efficiently than the moment fit but inherits the GEV likelihood's small-sample instability.

    return_level drops the shape parameter entirely and fits \(-\log p = (x-u)/\beta\) as a straight line through the origin. One parameter, no optimiser, no finite endpoint, and no ability to bend: it is the model behind a return-level plot that looks straight, and the most stable of the five.

    Derivation #

    Peaks over threshold #

    Let \(X\) have distribution \(F\) and fix a threshold \(u\). The excess \(Y = X-u\) conditional on \(X>u\) has, in the limit of large \(u\), a generalised Pareto distribution with scale \(\beta>0\) and shape \(\xi\):

    \[S(y) = \Pr(Y > y \mid Y > 0) = \Big(1 + \frac{\xi y}{\beta}\Big)^{-1/\xi}, \qquad (1)\]

    with the exponential \(e^{-y/\beta}\) at \(\xi=0\) and the convention that \(S(y)=0\) once \(1+\xi y/\beta \le 0\). The support is \(y\ge 0\) for \(\xi\ge 0\) and \(0 \le y \le -\beta/\xi\) for \(\xi<0\), so a negative shape is a finite endpoint at \(u-\beta/\xi\).

    The unconditional tail follows from the chain rule for conditional probability:

    \[p(x) = \Pr(X>x) = \zeta_u\, S(x-u), \qquad \zeta_u = \frac{n_u + \tfrac{1}{2}}{n+1}, \qquad (2)\]

    for \(x>u\), where \(n_u\) is the number of exceedances. The half-count is the same smoothing the empirical CDF uses in the copod atom, and it is chosen so that (2) at \(x=u\) equals the empirical survival \(\Pr(X>u)\) exactly. Below the threshold the implementation switches to the empirical survival, because the Pareto model says nothing about the body and its extrapolation downwards is nonsense.

    The code is a single guarded evaluation of (1), with the exponential branch kept separate so that no \(0/0\) ever appears:

        y = 1.0 + xi * z / beta
    return np.where(y > 0.0, np.power(np.maximum(y, 1e-300), -1.0 / xi), 0.0)

    The np.maximum is not cosmetic. Without it, np.power of a negative base with a fractional exponent returns NaN, and one NaN poisons the whole tail probability — including the training scores, which is how the failure would first be noticed.

    Return levels #

    The return level \(x_T\) is the value exceeded once every \(T\) observations on average, which is the same as solving \(p(x_T)=1/T\). Inverting (2) and (1):

    \[x_T = u + \frac{\beta}{\xi}\Big((T\zeta_u)^{\xi} - 1\Big), \qquad (3)\]

    and \(x_T = u + \beta\log(T\zeta_u)\) at \(\xi=0\). The factor \(T\zeta_u\) is why the threshold's own return period, \(1/\zeta_u\), is a floor: asking for a period shorter than that is asking for a quantile below the threshold, where the model is not defined, and the implementation refuses.

            if abs(self.xi_) < 1e-8:
            return self.u_ + self.beta_ * math.log(target)
        return self.u_ + self.beta_ / self.xi_ * (target ** self.xi_ - 1.0)

    For \(\xi<0\) the bracket \((T\zeta_u)^\xi\) tends to zero as \(T\) grows, so the return level tends to the endpoint \(u-\beta/\xi\) and never crosses it. That limit is the finite-endpoint behaviour the beta demo checks.

    Block maxima #

    For a block of \(b\) observations with maximum \(M\), independence gives \(\Pr(M\le x)=F(x)^b\). The GEV is the limit law of \(M\):

    \[G(x) = \exp\!\Big(-\big(1+\xi\tfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\big)^{-1/\xi}\Big), \qquad (4)\]

    again with the Gumbel limit \(\exp(-e^{-(x-\mu)/\sigma})\) at \(\xi=0\). Inverting \(G(x)=F(x)^b\) turns a statement about maxima into a statement about a single observation:

    \[p(x) = 1 - G(x)^{1/b}. \qquad (5)\]

    That inversion is exact under the model, not a large-\(x\) approximation, which is why the block variants can be scored on the same surprisal scale as the threshold variants. The implementation computes it with log and expm1 to avoid cancelling \(1-G^{1/b}\) when \(G\) is close to one:

            cdf = self._gev_cdf(x)
        with np.errstate(divide="ignore"):
            probability = -np.expm1(np.log(cdf) / self.block_size_)

    A point beyond a finite endpoint has \(G=1\), so \(\log G=0\) and the probability is zero: the point is impossible, and the score is infinite. A point below the lower support has \(G=0\), so the probability is one: it is not anomalous, it is merely small.

    Block return levels #

    Setting (5) equal to \(1/T\) and solving gives the block version of (3):

    \[x_T = \mu + \frac{\sigma}{\xi}\Big(\big(-b\log(1-1/T)\big)^{-\xi} - 1\Big), \qquad (6)\]

    with the Gumbel limit \(\mu-\sigma\log(-b\log(1-1/T))\) at \(\xi=0\). The quantity \(-b\log(1-1/T)\) is \(-\log G(x_T)\), the GEV's own reduced variable at the return level, and for large \(T\) it is about \(b/T\). It is the bridge between the two routes: (4) turns it back into a cdf, and (5) turns the cdf into a probability.

    Estimating the parameters #

    GPD by probability-weighted moments. With \(Y\) the exceedances and \(a_s=\mathbb{E}[Y(1-F(Y))^s]\), the GPD gives \(a_s=\beta/((s+1)(s+1-\xi))\). For \(s=0,1\) that is two equations in two unknowns, and solving them is closed-form:

    \[\hat\xi = \frac{a_0-4a_1}{a_0-2a_1}, \qquad \hat\beta = \frac{2a_0a_1}{a_0-2a_1}. \qquad (7)\]

    The sample versions replace \(a_s\) by its order-statistic average. It is consistent, needs no iteration, and the denominator is the only thing that can go wrong — which is why the implementation checks it:

        a0 = float(ordered.mean())
    a1 = float(np.sum((n - np.arange(1, n + 1)) / (n - 1.0) * ordered) / n)
    denominator = a0 - 2.0 * a1

    GEV by L-moments. The same idea applied to maxima: form \(b_0,b_1,b_2\) from the sorted maxima with the weights \((i-1)/(n-1)\) and \((i-1)(i-2)/((n-1)(n-2))\), turn them into the L-moments \(\ell_2=2b_1-b_0\) and \(\ell_3=6b_2-6b_1+b_0\), and read the shape off the L-skewness \(\tau_3=\ell_3/\ell_2\) through Hosking's approximation

    \[\xi = -\big(7.8590\,c + 2.9554\,c^2\big), \qquad c = \frac{2}{3+\tau_3} - \frac{\log 2}{\log 3}. \qquad (8)\]

    The scale and location then follow from \(\sigma=\ell_2\xi/((2^\xi-1)\Gamma(1-\xi))\) and \(\mu=b_0-\sigma(\Gamma(1-\xi)-1)/\xi\), with the Gumbel limits \(\ell_2/\log 2\) and \(b_0-\sigma\gamma\) at \(\xi=0\). The signs here are a genuine trap: several references write the shape with the opposite convention, and a wrong sign reproduces every Gumbel sample correctly while turning a Fréchet sample inside out. This implementation's first version did exactly that, and the max-stable demo — where the answer is known to be \(0.2\) — is what caught it:

        c = 2.0 / (3.0 + ell3 / ell2) - math.log(2.0) / math.log(3.0)
    xi = -(7.8590 * c + 2.9554 * c * c)

    Both by likelihood. The GPD and GEV log-likelihoods are two or three parameters, and the only awkward part is the support: the likelihood is undefined, not merely small, outside it. The implementation parameterises the scale by its logarithm, returns a large constant outside the support, and runs a deterministic Nelder–Mead simplex started from the moment fit:

        z = (x - mu) / sigma
    y = 1.0 + xi * z
    if not np.all(y > 0.0):
        return 1e12

    A few dozen lines of reflection, expansion and contraction buy two consistent estimators and no dependency. The returned point is the best vertex of the simplex, so the result is deterministic and reproducible.

    Code walkthrough #

    Scoring a point #

    Everything reduces to a tail probability, and every variant shares the same public surface: tail_probability, score_samples, return_period, return_level, predict. The POT branch shows the hybrid that (2) describes:

            probability = np.empty(x.size)
        below = x <= self.u_
        counts = np.searchsorted(self.sorted_, x[below], side="right")
        probability[below] = (self.n_ - counts + 0.5) / (self.n_ + 1.0)
        probability[~below] = self.scale_ * _gpd_survival(
            x[~below] - self.u_, self.beta_, self.xi_)

    searchsorted on the stored sorted sample is what keeps scoring independent of the batch: a point's probability depends on the training data and on the point, not on the other points scored with it. The split at u_ also makes the model honest about its own domain — the parametric part is only ever evaluated in the region where the theorem applies.

    The GEV cdf, with the support handled #

            y = 1.0 + self.xi_ * z
        cdf = np.zeros_like(x)
        inside = y > 0.0
        cdf[inside] = np.exp(-np.power(y[inside], -1.0 / self.xi_))
        if self.xi_ < 0.0:
            cdf[~inside] = 1.0

    Masking rather than np.where is deliberate: the power is only evaluated on the inside of the support, so no warning is raised and no NaN is created. The xi_ < 0 branch sets the cdf to one above the finite endpoint; for xi_ > 0 the zeros below the lower support are already correct.

    The decision threshold #

            self.scores_ = self.score_samples(x)
        self.threshold_ = float(np.quantile(self.scores_,
                                            1.0 - self.contamination))

    The default decision rule is the same contamination quantile as the rest of the chapter, but it is the weaker interface: it fixes the false-alarm rate to whatever the training set contains and gives up the return period. The model's own rule is return_period(x) > T, which is stable under changes to the sample size and has an interpretation a domain expert can argue with. Both are available; only one of them is a budget.

    Complexity #

    Let \(n\) be the number of observations, \(n_u\) the exceedances, \(n_b\) the blocks, \(m\) the number of points scored and \(I\) the number of simplex iterations.

    operation time memory
    fit, moments (pot, block_maxima, return_level) \(O(n\log n)\) \(O(n)\)
    fit, likelihood (gpd, gev) \(O(I\,n_u)\) after the sort \(O(n_u)\)
    score, threshold variants \(O(m\log n)\) \(O(m)\)
    score, block variants \(O(m)\) \(O(m)\)
    return level \(O(1)\) \(O(1)\)

    The sort dominates every fit, and the exceedances are a small fraction of the sample, so the likelihood fits are cheap in practice: the exponential demos fit five hundred exceedances in a few milliseconds. Scoring is a binary search for the threshold variants because of the empirical body, and a closed form for the block variants, which never look at the sample again. For the threshold variants the state is one sorted copy of the data; the block variants keep three numbers.

    Failure modes #

    A high threshold buys honesty at the price of variance. The sweep in the demo keeps 600, 300, 150 and 30 exceedances from the same exponential sample, and the standard deviation of the shape estimate grows from \(0.043\) to \(0.206\):

    threshold quantile exceedances mean \(\hat\xi\) std \(\hat\xi\) mean \(\hat p(6)\) std \(\log_{10}\hat p(6)\)
    0.80 600 \(-0.0072\) \(0.0429\) \(0.002314\) \(0.124\)
    0.90 300 \(0.0042\) \(0.0714\) \(0.002467\) \(0.155\)
    0.95 150 \(-0.0072\) \(0.1050\) \(0.002347\) \(0.162\)
    0.99 30 \(-0.0400\) \(0.2060\) \(0.002264\) \(0.177\)

    The probability estimate stays near the truth because the Pareto model is exact here, but the spread does not: the highest threshold's shape estimate moves almost five times as much as the lowest one's. A threshold is a bias-variance choice, not a formality.

    A low threshold buys data at the price of bias. The GPD is a limit as \(u\to\infty\). Below the point where the limit has bitten, the fitted shape is describing the body of the distribution, and the return levels it produces are confidently wrong. The diagnostics are a mean-residual-life plot (should be linear in \(u\)), a shape-stability plot (should be flat in \(u\)), and the sweep table above.

    Block size is a bias, too. Gaussian maxima are only asymptotically Gumbel, and at a block of 25 the fitted shape is \(-0.12\); at a block of 250 it is \(-0.08\). Both are negative because the Gaussian's tail converges slowly, not because the data have a finite endpoint. A Fréchet sample, which is max-stable, recovers its \(0.2\) even at the small block:

    data block block_maxima \(\hat\xi\) gev \(\hat\xi\)
    Gaussian 25 \(-0.120\) \(-0.118\)
    Gaussian 250 \(-0.083\) \(-0.090\)
    Fréchet\((5)\) 25 \(0.162\) \(0.171\)

    Extrapolation error grows with distance. The fitted return levels are within \(1.5\%\) at \(T=100\) but \(10\%\) low at \(T=10000\). That is the honest error bar on the plot above: the further the extrapolation, the more the answer depends on the shape, and the shape is the least well estimated parameter. Report an interval, not a point.

    The endpoint can be missed, and the shape can be degenerate. A bounded distribution whose density does not vanish at the endpoint has \(\xi=-1\), where the GPD is a point mass and the likelihood has no interior maximum. Uniform data are the canonical case: both fits in this file return a shape below \(-1\) and are refused with the shape estimate is outside (-1, 1). Beta\((2,2)\), whose density vanishes linearly, has \(\xi=-0.5\) and fits cleanly. "The tail has an endpoint" is not enough information; the behaviour of the density at the endpoint decides whether the model can represent it.

    Non-stationarity breaks everything. Every equation above assumes the tail is the same at the start and the end of the sample. A trend, a season or a regime change makes the fitted shape an average of incompatible tails, and the return level a statement about a distribution that never existed. Detrend or condition first; the change_point atom is the diagnostic.

    It is univariate, by construction. The theory describes the tail of one random variable. Correlated features must be reduced first — a norm, a projection, a residual — and the reduction is a modelling choice that the tail fit cannot make for you. For a multivariate score, use copod or mahalanobis and keep this atom for the univariate statistic they produce.

    The contamination interface hides the extrapolation. With contamination=0.01 the threshold is the 99th percentile of the training scores, so one percent of the training set is flagged by construction, exactly as in every other atom in the chapter. The whole point of the tail model is the part beyond the data; the quantile rule throws it away. Use return_period when the question is "how rare is this", and the quantile when the question is "flag the worst one percent".

    Applications #

    Hydrology and civil engineering. The hundred-year flood is a return level with \(T\) in years, fitted to annual maxima or to peaks over a threshold. The confidence interval matters more than the point estimate, and the block-size and threshold diagnostics above are standard practice.

    Finance and insurance. Value-at-risk and expected shortfall are tail quantiles and tail means; the GPD is the standard model for operational-loss severity above a high threshold, and the shape parameter is the number that decides how much capital the far tail needs.

    Reliability and materials. Component failure times, fatigue lives and extreme loads are block maxima or exceedances; the Weibull case (\(\xi<0\)) gives the finite life that engineers design against.

    Network and system monitoring. The SPOT and DSPOT detectors apply exactly this file's pipeline — fit a GPD to the largest observations of a stream and flag the points whose fitted probability crosses a target — to network traffic and sensor streams, with a drifting threshold for non-stationary data.

    Environmental compliance. Air-quality and temperature extremes are regulated as return levels ("not to be exceeded more than once in ten years"), and the shape parameter is what distinguishes a bounded pollutant from an unbounded one.

    A second opinion for any anomaly detector. Fit the tail to the scores of another model and check whether the flagged points are rare under the fitted tail. If a point is flagged by a rank rule but has a return period of two observations under the tail model, the flag is a tie, not an extreme.
