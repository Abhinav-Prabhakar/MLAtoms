    09 · Density & anomaly/tier 1/4 variants/source

    Local outlier factor

    Intuition #

    A point is anomalous if it is sparser than its neighbours. Not sparser than the data set — sparser than the handful of points around it. That is the whole idea, and it is what separates LOF from everything above it in this chapter:

       dense cluster              sparse region            a hole
   · · · · · ·                ·        ·              · · · ·
   · · x · · ·                ·   ·        ·          ·  x   ·
   · · · · · ·                ·        ·              · · · ·
   LOF(x) ≈ 1                 LOF ≈ 1                  LOF > 1
   (dense, like its           (sparse, but its         (sparse, and its
    neighbours)                neighbours agree)        neighbours do not)

    The middle case is the one that costs a global detector its accuracy. A point in a sparse region is far from everything, so a k-nearest-neighbour distance ranks it as anomalous; but every point around it is equally far from everything, so relative to its own neighbourhood it is perfectly ordinary. LOF divides, and the division cancels the region's scale. The demo measures this: on a tight blob plus a deliberately wide one, the eight genuinely odd points on the edge of the tight blob are separated by LOF at AUC 0.971, while among the same 68 points the global distance score gets AUC 0.000 — it ranks every wide-blob point above every odd one, because wide-blob points are further from everything.

    Formally, with \(k\) a neighbourhood size,

    \[N_k(p) = \text{the } k \text{ nearest points to } p, \qquad k\text{-dist}(p) = \max_{o \in N_k(p)} d(p, o) \qquad (1)\]

    the quantity that gets compared is a local reachability density,

    \[\mathrm{reach}_k(p, o) = \max\bigl(k\text{-dist}(o),\, d(p, o)\bigr) \qquad (2)\]
    \[\mathrm{lrd}_k(p) = \left(\frac{1}{k} \sum_{o \in N_k(p)} \mathrm{reach}_k(p, o)\right)^{-1} \qquad (3)\]

    and the factor is the ratio of the neighbours' density to the point's own:

    \[\mathrm{LOF}_k(p) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{o \in N_k(p)} \frac{\mathrm{lrd}_k(o)}{\mathrm{lrd}_k(p)} \qquad (4)\]

    One is the neutral value. Above one, the point is sparser than its neighbourhood; below one, denser, which happens at the centre of a cluster and is not usually interesting. The score is dimensionless and scale-free: the demo translates, scales by three and rotates a sample, and every score is unchanged to \(8.9 \times 10^{-16}\).

    Equation (2) is the part that is easy to get wrong, and the part that makes LOF work. The reachability distance is not \(d(p,o)\): it is \(d(p,o)\) floored by the neighbour's own k-distance. A point at the edge of a dense cluster may have one neighbour that is very close and \(k-1\) that are not; without the floor, the one close neighbour would make its density look high. The floor says: a neighbour cannot give you credit for being closer than its own scale. The demo's chain of five points makes the effect visible in the other direction: the two end points of a uniform chain score exactly 1, where simplified_lof — the same estimator with the floor removed — gives them 1.5 and the middle points 0.83. The floor is what stops an end from looking sparse merely for being an end.

    k sets what "local" means and is the only real parameter. The published value is 20; a k of 1 compares a point with a single neighbour and is mostly noise, and a k close to \(n\) turns LOF into a global density ratio. The variants are four answers to "what is a local density":

    • lof is (3), the paper's definition.
    • simplified_lof drops the reachability floor and uses the plain mean distance.
    • lof_knn drops the mean too, keeping only the k-th distance.
    • cluster_lof computes the ratio between micro-clusters, weighting each by its point count, so the score is piecewise constant and a scattered cluster is judged as a unit.

    Variants #

    variant density of a point scores constant within measured
    lof \(1/\text{mean reach}\) (3) no ring 6.78, sparse blob 1.21
    simplified_lof \(1/\text{mean } d\) no ring 8.39, sparse blob 1.33
    lof_knn \(1/k\text{-dist}\) no ring 6.61, noisier point to point
    cluster_lof mass / volume (5) yes, per micro-cluster ring 11.29, flags 7.6%

    The table is 300 tight points, 60 wide ones, and 10 on a ring of radius 4.5, scored with \(k = 20\). All four find the ring (AUC between 0.907 and 0.981) and all four are near 1 through the interior; they disagree exactly where "density" stops being obvious.

    simplified_lof is the cleanest comparison, because it changes one thing: it removes the \(\max\) from (2). The wide blob's score rises from 1.207 to 1.331, because a point there is allowed to be credited for a neighbour that is close by its own standard but far by the region's. The reachability floor is a regulariser, and removing it makes the score more sensitive and less stable.

    lof_knn removes the averaging instead. Its density is \(1/k\text{-dist}(p)\), so one unusually placed neighbour moves the score; the ring's mean falls from 8.39 to 6.61 and the ranking within the ring gets noisier even though the AUC is the highest of the four here. It is the cheapest to compute — one stored distance per point — and the least smooth.

    cluster_lof is a different estimator, not a tweak. It runs k-means, treats each cluster as one weighted point, and applies (4) at that resolution:

    \[\rho_c = \frac{w_c}{\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{c' \in N_k(c)} \mathrm{reach}(c, c')\right)^{d}} \qquad (5)\]

    with \(w_c\) the number of points in cluster \(c\). Mass over volume is what makes it a density rather than a distance ratio: a cluster with twice the points at the same radius is twice as dense, which the bare centroid would not know. The price is resolution. Scores are constant within a cluster — the demo asserts the spread is exactly zero — so it cannot rank points inside one, and with only ten distinct scores it cannot hit a 10% threshold exactly: it flags 7.6% instead. In exchange it is robust to a cluster of anomalies that is too small to fill a point-level neighbourhood but large enough to be its own cluster, which is precisely the case that defeats ordinary LOF below.

    Derivation #

    1. Why a ratio, and why it is near one #

    For a homogeneous sample, the \(k\) nearest neighbours of every point are at comparable distances, so every \(\mathrm{lrd}\) in (4) is about the same number and the ratio is about one. That is the calibration: LOF is not a probability and its scale is not absolute, but 1 is the value a point gets when its neighbourhood is typical for its own region. The demo's uniform chain gives exactly 1 at all four non-outlier points, and on the variants demo's 300-point tight Gaussian blob the mean score is 1.111, against 1.207 for the deliberately wide blob beside it.

    The ratio is also what makes the estimator invariant to a similarity transform. Scaling every distance by \(c\) multiplies every \(\mathrm{lrd}\) by \(1/c\), so the ratio in (4) is unchanged; rotating or translating the data does not change any distance at all. The demo asserts this to \(8.9 \times 10^{-16}\), and it is worth stating because it is not true of the threshold: the threshold is a quantile of the scores, so it moves with the shape of the distribution even though the scores do not.

    2. The reachability floor #

    Without the floor, \(\mathrm{lrd}\) is \(1/\text{mean } d\), which is a density estimate with a bandwidth equal to the neighbourhood radius. That is fine inside a homogeneous region and bad at a boundary, where the mean includes one short and \(k-1\) long distances. The floor replaces each short distance with the neighbour's own \(k\)-distance, which is the radius at which that neighbour's density is defined. The result is a density that is continuous across a density boundary rather than jumping at it, which is why LOF gives the same answer on both sides of a sharp edge.

    The cost is that the floor discards information: two points at distances 0.1 and 1.0 from a neighbour whose k-distance is 2.0 are treated identically. That is the intended behaviour — they are both inside the neighbour's own scale — and it is also why simplified_lof can be sharper on data with genuine fine structure inside a neighbourhood.

    3. Why the mean is over the neighbours' densities #

    Equation (4) compares \(p\)'s density with the mean density of the points \(p\) is compared with, not with the density of the region. The two differ when the neighbourhood is asymmetric: a point on the edge of a cluster has most neighbours inside the cluster, so its reference is the cluster's density, and it is flagged if it is sparser than the cluster even though the empty side is not represented. This is the definition of "local": the reference set is the neighbourhood, not a fixed radius. It also means the score depends on the density gradient — a point on a slope is compared with points uphill of it.

    4. Choosing k #

    \(k\) controls both the variance and the scale of the comparison. Small \(k\) makes the score noisy, because the mean in (3) is over few distances and a single neighbour can dominate; large \(k\) smooths the score but compares a point with a region so wide that "local" stops meaning anything. There is no closed-form optimum, and the paper's 20 is a default rather than a result. The failure-mode section shows the one case where the choice is structural rather than a trade-off: if an anomaly group has at least \(k\) members, the group supplies its own reference and disappears.

    5. The threshold #

    fit stores the \(1 - \text{contamination}\) quantile of the training scores, and predict returns \(-1\) when a score exceeds it. The parameter is a budget, not a detector, exactly as in isolation forest: 10% contamination flags 10% of any sample, including pure noise. The difference is that LOF's scores are ratios near 1 with a right tail, so the quantile is usually close to 1 and the flag set is "the points whose neighbourhood is sparsest relative to its own surroundings". That is a more meaningful flag than a raw distance threshold, and it is still not a statement about the true anomaly rate.

    6. The cluster variant #

    cluster_lof applies (4) to micro-clusters. Each cluster is a point with a mass, and (5) converts the mass and the mean reachability into a density. Two details matter. The exponent \(d\) is the dimension: volume grows like radius to the power \(d\), so without it a cluster of twice the mass at twice the radius would look equally dense in any dimension, which is wrong above one dimension. And the neighbour search is over clusters, so \(k\) in the cluster variant is a number of clusters, not points; the default n_clusters = 8 makes k = min(n_neighbors, 7) in practice, which is why the demo passes n_clusters = 10 for a \(k\) of 9.

    Code walkthrough #

    1. Neighbourhoods #

        distances = D.copy()
    if exclude_self:
        np.fill_diagonal(distances, np.inf)
    order = np.argsort(distances, axis=1, kind="stable")[:, :k]
    return order, np.take_along_axis(distances, order, axis=1)

    The exclude_self flag is the whole reason this is a function and not two lines. For a square matrix of the training points against themselves, the diagonal is zero and must be masked before the sort, or every point's nearest neighbour is itself and the k-distance is zero. For a query against the training set, the matrix is not square and row \(i\)'s zero entry is the distance to training point \(i\) — masking it would silently drop a genuine neighbour. Passing the flag explicitly, rather than inferring it from the shape, is what keeps score_samples correct on both paths. stable sorting makes ties deterministic, which the demo's exact assertions depend on.

    2. The three point-level densities #

            if self.variant == "lof":
            reach = np.maximum(dists, self.kdist_[self.neighbours_])
            spread = reach.mean(axis=1)
        elif self.variant == "simplified_lof":
            spread = dists.mean(axis=1)
        else:  # lof_knn keeps only the k-th distance
            spread = self.kdist_
        return 1.0 / np.maximum(spread, self._eps)

    Equations (3) and its two variants, in the order of the table. The indexing is the subtle part: self.kdist_[self.neighbours_] is an \((n, k)\) array whose entry \(i, j\) is the k-distance of neighbour \(j\) of point \(i\). That is (2)'s asymmetry — the floor comes from the neighbour — and transposing it, or using self.kdist_[:, None], would floor each reach by the point's own k-distance, which is a different estimator and is not LOF. The np.maximum against _eps is the only numerical guard: duplicate points give zero distance, and a density of infinity would make every ratio involving them nan.

    3. The factor #

            neighbour_density = density[self.neighbours_]
        return neighbour_density.mean(axis=1) / density

    Equation (4) in two lines, and the shape is the point: density[self.neighbours_] is \((n, k)\), the mean over the \(k\) neighbours is the numerator, and dividing by the \((n,)\) vector broadcasts the denominator across it. The same expression serves both levels of the estimator — points and clusters — because both are "density array plus neighbour index array". That is the design decision that keeps cluster_lof from being a separate class.

    4. Fitting #

                D = _distances(X)
            scale = float(np.median(D[~np.eye(n, dtype=bool)]))
            self._eps = 1e-12 * max(scale, 1e-12)
            self.neighbours_, dists = _neighbourhoods(D, self.n_neighbors,
                                                      exclude_self=True)
            self.kdist_ = dists[:, -1]
            self.lrd_ = self._density(dists)
            self.scores_ = self._factor(self.lrd_)

    The order matters: distances, then the numerical floor, then neighbourhoods. The floor is \(10^{-12}\) times the median off-diagonal distance, not a fixed constant, so that the guard is scale-free — the same code works on data measured in nanometres and in light years. dists[:, -1] is the k-distance because the neighbours are sorted, and self.kdist_ is stored rather than recomputed because score_samples needs the training k-distances to floor a query's reachability against.

    5. k-means for the cluster variant #

        centres[0] = X[rng.integers(n)]
    closest = _distances(X, centres[:1])[:, 0] ** 2
    for j in range(1, k):
        total = float(closest.sum())
        if total <= 0.0:
            centres[j] = X[rng.integers(n)]
        else:
            centres[j] = X[rng.choice(n, p=closest / total)]
        closest = np.minimum(closest, _distances(X, centres[j:j + 1])[:, 0] ** 2)

    k-means++ seeding: the first centre is uniform, each next one is drawn with probability proportional to its squared distance to the nearest centre chosen so far. That is what makes the clustering reproducible enough to assert properties about and good enough that ten clusters on the demo's data are stable across runs. The total <= 0 branch is the degenerate case where every point coincides with a centre; without it the probabilities are nan and rng.choice raises a confusing error much later. The Lloyd loop that follows is the ordinary one, with an empty cluster re-seeded at the point furthest from its centre.

    6. Cluster density #

            reach = np.maximum(dists, self.kdist_[self.neighbours_])
        # Mass per unit volume: a cluster holding twice the points at the same
        # radius is twice as dense, which a bare centroid would not know.
        density = weights / np.maximum(reach.mean(axis=1), self._eps) ** d
        self.lrd_ = density
        self.cluster_scores_ = self._factor(density)
        self.labels_ = labels
        self.scores_ = self.cluster_scores_[labels]

    Equation (5). weights is np.bincount(labels), so the mass is exact and not a weighted average of points. The ** d is the volume term, and it is the one line that makes the variant dimension-aware. self.scores_ broadcasts the cluster factor back to points through the labels, so the training scores and the per-cluster scores stay consistent; score_samples then assigns a query to its nearest centre, which is why a query's score is exactly the cluster's and why the spread inside a cluster is zero.

    Complexity #

    For \(n\) points in \(d\) dimensions, \(k\) neighbours, \(m\) queries, \(c\) clusters and \(T\) Lloyd iterations:

    stage time memory
    pairwise distances \(O(n^2 d)\) \(O(n^2)\)
    k nearest neighbours \(O(n^2 \log n)\) implemented, \(O(n^2 \log k)\) with selection \(O(nk)\)
    lrd and LOF \(O(nk)\) \(O(n)\)
    query score \(O(mn(d + \log k))\) \(O(n)\)
    k-means, cluster_lof \(O(T n c d)\) \(O(nc)\)
    cluster distances \(O(c^2 d)\) \(O(c^2)\)

    The quadratic distance matrix is the honest cost of this implementation and the reason production LOF uses a k-d tree or a ball tree; with an index, the neighbour search is \(O(n \log n)\) in low dimension and the matrix is never materialised. The cluster variant is the exception: after k-means, everything happens on \(c\) centroids, so its cost is dominated by the assignment step and is linear in \(n\) for fixed \(c\). That is also why it scales to data where point-level LOF cannot be built — at the price of the resolution documented above.

    Failure modes #

    Masking: a group that fills its own neighbourhood #

    This is the one to know, and it is structural rather than numerical. Take thirty anomalies packed within 0.15 of one point, at radius 6 from a normal blob, and score with \(k = 15\): they average 1.08, AUC 0.439, and only 2 of 30 are flagged. The same thirty points spread at radii 7 to 9 average 7.66, AUC 1.000, and all 30 are in the top 30. The mechanism is in (4): if the group has at least \(k\) members, every member's neighbour set is made of group members, so its density is compared with the group's own density and the ratio is near one. The group is its own reference and looks normal.

    The demo also measures the boundary of the condition: eight points packed just as tightly, scored with \(k = 20\), average 8.78 — they cannot hide, because 13 of their 20 neighbours are blob points. So masking is a relation between the group size and \(k\), not a property of tightness. Lowering \(k\) makes it worse, and the defence is a \(k\) larger than the plausible group size, or a variant that does not let a group be its own reference — cluster_lof at a finer resolution, or the isolation forest above, which counts the group as a cluster of points but still has to separate it from the blob.

    Duplicates are invisible #

    With \(k = 1\), a duplicated row and its original are each other's only neighbour, both k-distances are zero, the reachability floor in (2) is zero, and the ratio is exactly 1.0. With \(k = 5\) the pair scores 1.110 against a threshold of 1.205. Either way the copy is not flagged: two coincident points make each other look normal, which is masking at the smallest possible scale. Deduplicate first; a near-duplicate burst in a log is exactly this case, and it is the reason a production pipeline should not treat LOF's silence as evidence.

    The distance metric is a modelling decision #

    LOF uses Euclidean distance, so it mixes units. A column measured in thousands dominates one measured in fractions, and two columns with different variances are effectively weighted by their scales. Unlike a density estimator on a standardised space, there is no bandwidth to absorb it. Standardise or whiten before fitting, and remember that (4) is invariant to a global scale but not to per-column scales — the invariance that makes the score dimensionless does not make it unit-free.

    High dimensions #

    Above roughly ten dimensions the distances to every point concentrate, every neighbourhood has the same radius, and (4) converges to 1 for every point. LOF does not fail loudly: it returns a plausible set of numbers near one and a threshold that flags an arbitrary 10%. This is the same curse that defeats the KDE at the start of the chapter, and the local methods do not escape it; they only postpone it. If a 20-dimensional LOF run separates nothing, that is usually the reason, not a bug.

    The threshold is still a budget #

    contamination sets the flag rate, not the detection rate. On the masking data, LOF's AUC is 0.439 — worse than chance, because the tight group's scores are below the blob's — while predict still flags 10% of the points, chosen from the least-dense blob points. A high flag rate and a bad ranking coexist happily, and only the AUC (or a labelled sample) tells them apart.

    Applications #

    • Intrusion detection. The original application: network connections have wildly different baselines per host and service, and a login rate that is anomalous for one server is normal for another. LOF's local reference is the host's own history, not the fleet's.
    • Fraud and account takeover. A transaction is judged against the account's recent behaviour rather than against all accounts; the ratio cancels the account's scale, which is what a global threshold cannot do.
    • Manufacturing and sensor faults. A machine's vibration signature is dense in its healthy region; LOF flags excursions from its own operating point, and the wide-normal-region case is handled without flagging every point in a naturally noisy machine.
    • Feature construction. The LOF score, the k-distance and the neighbour count are useful features for a supervised model even when LOF is not the detector, in the same way as the isolation-forest path lengths.
    • As a cross-check. LOF and isolation forest disagree in a informative way: the forest is global and saturates, LOF is local and masks groups. Running both and inspecting the disagreements is a cheap way to find the cases where the answer depends on the definition of anomalous.
    • Where it is a trap. Clustered anomalies, duplicates, and anything with more than about ten effective dimensions, per the failure modes above. For those, angle-based or isolation-based scores are the better default.
