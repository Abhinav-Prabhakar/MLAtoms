    Elliptic envelope — the robust ellipsoid as a decision region

    Intuition #

    An anomaly score says how far a point is. A decision region says whether it is inside. The second is the one you can draw on a chart, ship to a plant, or compare across dimensions, and for a Gaussian model the region is an ellipsoid:

                ·    ·    ·
        ·                 ·          ✕
      ·      ·     ·        ·
     ·    ·     ·     ·       ·
      ·      ·     ·        ·
        ·                 ·
            ·    ·    ·

    The points are the sample, the ring is the boundary, and the cross is outside. Everything in this atom is a choice about that ring: where its centre is, what shape it has, and how big it is. The Mahalanobis atom computes the score that the ring is a level set of; this atom is about fitting the ring itself.

    The obvious fit is the sample mean and covariance. It is also the one that fails, because the outliers take part in it. Move a tenth of the data into a tight cluster at distance \(2.5\) and the variance along that direction grows by \(1 + 0.1 \cdot 2.5^2 = 1.63\); the ellipse becomes 28 percent wider in every direction, and the cluster that caused the inflation is now comfortably inside the ellipse built to exclude it. This is masking, and it is the reason the minimum covariance determinant exists.

    On clean two-dimensional Gaussian data the four variants agree, which is the right way to check that a robust estimator is not simply a broken one:

    variant centre error fresh coverage volume scale C-steps
    mcd_ellipse 0.047 0.897 11.656 1.344 85
    min_cov_det 0.046 0.488 3.568 1.000 85
    shrinkage 0.091 0.899 11.714 1.000 0
    chi2_threshold 0.046 0.876 11.555 3.238 85

    The data are 400 points from a correlation-\(0.6\) Gaussian and the nominal coverage is 90 percent. The analytic volume of the 90 percent ellipse of the true covariance is \(11.574\), and the three corrected estimators land on it to within one percent. The raw determinant minimiser does not: its volume is \(3.568\) and it covers 49 percent of fresh points. That gap is a factor of \(3.26\) — the small-sample correction (6) — and it is not a detail you can skip.

    Variants #

    variant centre and shape boundary works when
    min_cov_det raw MCD \(\chi^2_p\) never on its own
    mcd_ellipse corrected MCD, reweighted empirical quantile default
    chi2_threshold corrected MCD \(\chi^2_p\) the data are plausibly Gaussian
    shrinkage Ledoit-Wolf \(\chi^2_p\) \(p\) approaches \(n\)

    min_cov_det is the estimator of (5) with the Gaussian boundary and no correction. It exists because it is the shortest path from the paper to running code, and because seeing it fail is the fastest way to understand why (6) is not optional. It recovers the centre and finds every outlier in the contaminated demo, and it still covers only half the clean points.

    mcd_ellipse is the complete detector: corrected covariance, a reweighting step that refits on the points inside the envelope, and a size taken from the empirical quantile of the training distances. It is distribution-free in the sense that its in-sample coverage is exactly \(1-\alpha\) whatever the data look like. The price is that the empirical quantile is only as good as the sample: with a 10 percent budget and 10 percent contamination it lands on the largest clean distance and over-covers.

    chi2_threshold keeps the corrected estimator and uses the theoretical boundary, which is what scikit-learn's EllipticEnvelope does. It is the right choice when the Gaussian model is believable, and it is the one that stays calibrated in high dimensions, where the in-sample quantile is optimistic.

    shrinkage replaces the robust estimator with Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage toward a scaled identity. It is not robust — the demo shows it inheriting the classical mean and the classical failure — but it is the only variant that produces an invertible, well-conditioned covariance when the dimension is a large fraction of the sample size. At \(p=40\) and \(n=50\) the sample covariance has condition number \(356\) and covers none of a fresh sample; shrinkage has condition number \(1.4\) and covers 81 percent.

    Derivation #

    The score, the region, and the name #

    For a location \(\mu\) and a positive-definite covariance \(\Sigma\), the squared Mahalanobis distance is

    \[d^2(x) = (x-\mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x-\mu) \qquad (1)\]

    and the envelope at level \(t\) is the set of points whose distance does not exceed it:

    \[R_t = \{\, x : d^2(x) \le t \,\} \qquad (2)\]

    Write \(\Sigma = U\Lambda U^\top\) with \(\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_p)\). Then \(d^2(x) = \sum_i u_i^\top(x-\mu)^2/\lambda_i\), so \(R_t\) is the ellipsoid whose \(i\)-th semi-axis is \(\sqrt{t\lambda_i}\) along \(u_i\). The name is literal: the region is an ellipsoid, and its orientation is the eigenbasis of the covariance.

    The volume of the region #

    The linear map \(x \mapsto \Lambda^{-1/2}U^\top(x-\mu)/\sqrt{t}\) sends \(R_t\) to the unit ball. Its determinant is \(t^{-p/2}\det(\Sigma)^{-1/2}\), so

    \[\mathrm{vol}(R_t) = V_p\, t^{p/2} \sqrt{\det \Sigma}, \qquad V_p = \frac{\pi^{p/2}}{\Gamma(p/2+1)} \qquad (3)\]

    with \(V_p\) the volume of the unit ball. For \(p=2\), \(V_2 = \pi\) and the volume is \(\pi t\sqrt{\det\Sigma}\); the demo checks that identity against (3) to machine precision. This is why the determinant is the right thing for (5) to minimise: it is the volume of the ellipse up to the factor \(V_p t^{p/2}\), which does not depend on the support.

    Where the boundary comes from #

    If \(x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)\) then \(\Sigma^{-1/2}(x-\mu) \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\), so \(d^2(x)\) is a sum of \(p\) squared standard normals:

    \[d^2(x) \sim \chi^2_p \qquad (4)\]

    and the level set at the \(1-\alpha\) quantile contains \(1-\alpha\) of the mass. Two cases have closed forms and the demo asserts both: for \(p=2\), \(P(\chi^2_2 > t) = e^{-t/2}\), so the 95 percent quantile is \(-2\log 0.05 = 5.991\); for \(p=1\) it is the square of the normal quantile, \(1.96^2 = 3.841\).

    The catch is that (4) is exact only when \(\mu\) and \(\Sigma\) are the true parameters. With the plug-in estimates the in-sample distances are smaller than the law says — the demo measures 87.2 percent coverage at a nominal 90 — because the ellipse was fitted to those points. That in-sample optimism is the whole difference between chi2_threshold and mcd_ellipse.

    The minimum covariance determinant #

    The classical estimate is not robust because every point contributes to it. The minimum covariance determinant estimator replaces the average with a search:

    \[(\hat\mu, \hat\Sigma) = \arg\min_{H \subset \{1..n\},\ |H| = h} \det \mathrm{cov}(H) \qquad (5)\]

    Since the determinant is a volume, this is "the tightest ellipsoid that contains \(h\) points". A far-away point cannot make a set tighter by being far away, which is exactly the property the sample covariance lacks.

    The naive version of this idea — trim the points with the largest distance — is circular: you need a centre to measure distance and a covariance to scale it, and the outliers are the ones that corrupt both. MCD breaks the circle by searching over subsets rather than over residuals. The price is a combinatorial problem, and the algorithm that makes it practical is the C-step:

    1. Start from a random \(h\)-subset.
    2. Compute its mean and covariance.
    3. Replace the subset with the \(h\) points of smallest distance under them.
    4. Repeat until the subset stops changing.

    Each step cannot increase the determinant, so the loop terminates. It terminates at a local minimum, so the implementation runs several random starts and keeps the smallest determinant; in the demo ten starts take 85 C-steps in total and agree on the same support.

    The choice of \(h\) is the robustness/efficiency dial. With \(h = (n+p+1)/2\) the estimator tolerates just under 50 percent contamination, which is the maximum any affine-equivariant estimator can achieve; larger \(h\) uses more data and is more efficient, and tolerates less contamination. The default here is that maximal-breakdown value, capped at \(n\).

    The correction #

    The covariance of the \(h\) most concentrated points of a Gaussian is not the covariance of the Gaussian. It is the covariance of a truncated Gaussian, and truncation removes variance. The exact factor comes from one identity. If \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I_p)\) and \(q = \chi^2_p(\alpha)\), then

    \[t\,f_{\chi^2_p}(t) = p\,f_{\chi^2_{p+2}}(t)\]

    so the first moment of the truncated squared radius is

    \[\mathbb{E}\big[\|Z\|^2 \mathbf{1}\{\|Z\|^2 \le q\}\big] = \int_0^q t f_{\chi^2_p}(t)\,dt = p\,F_{\chi^2_{p+2}}(q),\]

    and dividing by \(\alpha = P(\|Z\|^2 \le q)\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[\|Z\|^2 \mid \text{inside}] = p F_{\chi^2_{p+2}}(q)/\alpha\). By symmetry the truncated second moment matrix is a multiple of the identity, so the truncated covariance is \([F_{\chi^2_{p+2}}(q)/\alpha]\,\Sigma\), and multiplying by

    \[c(\alpha,p) = \frac{\alpha}{F_{\chi^2_{p+2}}\big(\chi^2_p(\alpha)\big)} \qquad (6)\]

    undoes it. The factor is large exactly where the estimator is most robust: \(c = 3.259\) at \(\alpha = 0.5\) in two dimensions, \(1.344\) at \(\alpha = 0.9\), and \(1.131\) at \(\alpha = 0.9\) in eight dimensions. Skipping it turns a 90 percent envelope into a 49 percent one, and even after the correction the coverage falls further short of nominal as the dimension grows — 0.878 at \(p=2\) against 0.823 at \(p=8\) at \(h/n = 0.5\) — because the volume carries the factor to the power \(p/2\) and the sampling error in the support covariance grows with it.

    Reweighting #

    The corrected MCD covariance is unbiased but noisy: it is computed from \(h\) points, which at the default \(h \approx n/2\) throws away half the sample. The fix is to use the robust fit only to decide which points are clean, and then estimate the covariance classically from all of them:

    1. Correct the MCD covariance by (6).
    2. Keep the points inside the \(1-\alpha\) ellipse.
    3. Refit the mean and covariance on the kept points.
    4. Correct again — the kept set is itself a truncation, now at a much larger \(\alpha\), where \(c\) is close to one.

    The demo's mcd_ellipse reports scale_ = 1.344, which is exactly \(c(0.9,2)\): the reweighted set is the central 90 percent of the data, and its covariance is corrected by the corresponding factor. The step buys back the efficiency the \(h\)-subset gave up: chi2_threshold, which skips it, covers 87.6 percent where mcd_ellipse covers 89.7.

    The cut has to be the same \(1-\alpha\) as the envelope. Some implementations hard-code 97.5 percent, which lets points between the two thresholds back into the covariance; when those points are a contaminating cluster, the ellipse inflates to include the very points it was meant to exclude.

    Shrinkage #

    When \(p\) is a large fraction of \(n\) the sample covariance is invertible but useless: its smallest eigenvalues are noise, so the ellipse is a sliver in some directions and unbounded in others. Ledoit-Wolf replaces it with a convex combination of the sample covariance and a scaled identity,

    \[\hat\Sigma = (1-\lambda) S + \lambda\,\mu I, \qquad \mu = \frac{\mathrm{tr}(S)}{p},\]

    choosing \(\lambda\) to minimise the expected squared Frobenius error. With \(d^2 = \|S - \mu I\|_F^2/p\) the squared distance to the target and \(b^2 = \frac{1}{n^2p}\sum_k \|x_kx_k^\top - S\|_F^2\) the variance of the sample covariance's entries, the optimal weight is \(\lambda = \min(1, b^2/d^2)\).

    The estimator is not robust — a cluster still moves the mean — but it is the only one here that survives \(p/n \to 1\). In the demo at \(p=40\), \(n=50\) it chooses \(\lambda = 0.893\), collapses the condition number from \(356\) to \(1.4\), and covers 81 percent of a fresh sample where both the sample covariance and the MCD cover none. The shortfall from 90 is the bias of pulling every direction toward a common variance, and at \(p/n = 0.8\) it is large.

    Code walkthrough #

    The quadratic form #

        centred = X - location
    solved = np.linalg.solve(covariance, centred.T)
    return np.einsum("ij,ij->j", centred.T, solved)

    One solve for the whole matrix rather than one inverse and a matrix product: the solve is \(O(p^3)\) once and \(O(np^2)\) for the right-hand side, and it is more accurate than forming \(\Sigma^{-1}\). The einsum is the row-wise dot product \(\sum_i c_{ij} s_{ij}\), which is (1) for every point at once.

    Chi-square without scipy #

    The whole atom needs \(F_{\chi^2}\) and its inverse, and neither is in numpy. The CDF is the regularised lower incomplete gamma function, evaluated by its power series below \(a+1\) and by a continued fraction above it; the quantile is then twenty lines of bisection:

        lo, hi = 0.0, 1.0
    while _chi2_cdf(hi, k) < q:
        hi *= 2.0
        if hi > 1e12:
            raise RuntimeError("chi-square quantile did not converge")
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if _chi2_cdf(mid, k) < q:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
        if hi - lo < 1e-14 * max(1.0, hi):
            break
    return 0.5 * (lo + hi)

    Doubling to bracket, then halving to converge. The bracket starts at \([0,1]\) and doubles, which costs at most 40 extra CDF evaluations and removes any need for a good initial guess. The tolerance is relative, so it holds for the \(p=40\) quantile as well as the \(p=2\) one, and the demo checks the result against the two closed forms.

    The correction #

        return alpha / _chi2_cdf(_chi2_ppf(alpha, p), p + 2)

    Equation (6) in one line. The inner quantile is the truncation point; the outer CDF is evaluated with \(p+2\) degrees of freedom, which is the identity \(t f_{\chi^2_p}(t) = p f_{\chi^2_{p+2}}(t)\) doing its work.

    The concentration step #

                sub = X[index]
            location = sub.mean(axis=0)
            covariance = np.atleast_2d(np.cov(sub, rowvar=False, bias=True))
            try:
                distance = _quadratic(X, location, covariance)
            except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
                singular = True
                break
            total += 1
            # the h points closest to the current ellipse are the next support
            updated = np.sort(np.argpartition(distance, h - 1)[:h])
            if np.array_equal(updated, index):
                break
            index = updated

    argpartition selects the \(h\) smallest distances in \(O(n)\) without sorting the rest, which matters when \(n\) is large and \(h\) is not. The try is not decoration: a random \(h\)-subset can be degenerate (duplicate rows, or \(h\) points on a hyperplane), and a singular covariance means the start is abandoned rather than crashing the whole fit. np.sort keeps the index set canonical so that the equality test detects a genuine fixed point rather than a permutation of one.

    Reweighting #

        distance = _quadratic(X, location, covariance)
    keep = distance <= _chi2_ppf(1.0 - contamination, p)
    if keep.sum() < p + 2:
        return location, covariance, np.ones(len(X), dtype=bool), 1.0
    sub = X[keep]
    new_location = sub.mean(axis=0)
    new_covariance = np.atleast_2d(np.cov(sub, rowvar=False, bias=True))
    scale = _correction(1.0 - contamination, p)
    return new_location, new_covariance * scale, keep, scale

    The guard is the interesting line. If the envelope is so small that fewer than \(p+2\) points survive, the refit would be singular, so the function returns the robust fit unchanged rather than a broken one. The returned keep mask becomes support_, so the caller can see which points the final estimate came from — useful when the next question is "why is this point inside".

    Shrinkage without the tensor #

        sample = (centred.T @ centred) / n
    target = (float(np.trace(sample)) / p) * np.eye(p)
    d2 = float(np.sum((sample - target) ** 2)) / p
    # sum_k || x_k x_k^T - S ||_F^2 without materialising the n x p x p tensor
    squared = np.sum(centred * centred, axis=1)
    quadratic = np.einsum("ij,ij->i", centred @ sample, centred)
    frobenius = float(np.sum(sample * sample))
    b2 = float(np.sum(squared * squared - 2.0 * quadratic + frobenius))
    b2 /= (n * n) * p

    The textbook definition of \(b^2\) is a sum of \(n\) Frobenius norms of \(p\times p\) matrices. Expanding the square turns it into three scalar sums per point: \(\|x\|^4\), \(-2x^\top S x\), and \(\|S\|_F^2\). That is the difference between \(O(np^2)\) memory and \(O(n)\) memory, and it is why this atom can run a \(p=40\) demo at all.

    Choosing the size #

            if self.variant == "mcd_ellipse":
            return float(np.quantile(self.distances_, 1.0 - self.contamination))
        return float(_chi2_ppf(1.0 - self.contamination, self.n_features_))

    Two lines, two philosophies. The empirical quantile is exact in-sample and needs no distributional assumption; the chi-square quantile is exact out-of-sample under the Gaussian model and is the only one that survives \(p/n \to 1\), because in-sample distances are systematically too small there. The demo measures that directly: at \(p=40\), \(n=50\) the empirical quantile would cover 56.5 percent of fresh points against a nominal 90.

    Complexity #

    Let \(n\) be the sample size, \(p\) the dimension, \(S\) the number of C-steps per start and \(K\) the number of starts.

    operation time memory
    score \(n\) points \(O(p^3 + np^2)\) \(O(np)\)
    one C-step \(O(np^2)\) \(O(np)\)
    MCD \(O(KSp^2n)\) \(O(np)\)
    reweight \(O(np^2)\) \(O(np)\)
    Ledoit-Wolf \(O(np^2 + p^3)\) \(O(np)\)
    volume \(O(p^3)\) \(O(p^2)\)

    The MCD dominates, and the constant that matters is \(S\), not \(K\): each start converges in a handful of steps, so ten starts cost about ten times one start, not a hundred. The demo's ten starts take 85 C-steps in total on 400 points in two dimensions, which is a few milliseconds. In high dimensions the \(p^2\) inside each step and the \(p^3\) in the solve are what hurt, and \(h\) must exceed \(p\) for the covariance to be non-singular at all.

    Failure modes #

    Masking. The classical covariance is dragged toward the contamination and inflates until the outliers are inside. In the demo the volume goes from 11.6 to 14.6 and the classical envelope flags only 49 percent of the cluster while covering 93 percent of the clean points. Shrinkage has the same mean and the same failure: it fixes conditioning, not contamination.

    Skipping the correction. min_cov_det covers 0.488 at a nominal 0.9, and its volume is a third of the truth. The factor is 3.26 in two dimensions at \(h/n=0.5\); it is not a rounding error, and it grows with the dimension because the volume carries it to the power \(p/2\).

    The empirical quantile needs headroom. With a 10 percent budget and 10 percent contamination, the 90th percentile of the training distances lands on the largest clean distance, so mcd_ellipse over-covers at 0.976 and misses a quarter of the cluster. At a 20 percent budget the same fit covers 0.866 and catches 0.989. The budget must exceed the contamination you actually have, and you rarely know it.

    The chi-square boundary assumes a Gaussian. On \(t_3\) data the theoretical envelope covers 0.736 at a nominal 0.9, because the squared radius is not chi-square when the tails are heavy. The empirical boundary covers 0.910 on the same data: it does not care what the law is, only that the training sample resembles the test sample.

    In high dimensions the in-sample quantile is optimistic. At \(p=40\), \(n=50\) the same shrinkage fit covers 0.807 with the chi-square boundary and 0.565 with the empirical one. Distances computed on the training points are smaller than distances on new points, and the gap grows with \(p/n\). Use the chi-square boundary there, or a held-out calibration set.

    Contamination above 50 percent breaks the estimator. The default support is the maximal-breakdown one, and it assumes the majority of the data is clean. If more than half the sample is bad, the "tightest half" is the bad cluster. There is no affine-equivariant estimator that survives that, so the answer is a different model, not a different \(h\).

    The result depends on the seed. C-steps converge to local minima. Ten starts are enough for the demo and are not a guarantee; if two runs disagree on the support, the determinant surface has more than one deep basin and you should raise n_init. The estimator is deterministic given seed, which is what makes the demo reproducible, not what makes it correct.

    \(p \ge n\) has no answer here. The fit refuses with "need at least \(p+2\) rows", and even when \(n > p+2\) the MCD support can be too small to estimate a \(p \times p\) covariance. The refusal is deliberate: the alternative is a singular matrix and a silent inf score.

    Applications #

    Analytical chemistry. MCD comes from chemometrics, where a calibration set contains a few samples that are not what the label says. The ellipse is fitted on the clean majority and the suspect samples are re-run; the method is Rousseeuw's, and it is still the standard robust covariance in that field.

    Manufacturing and process control. A two- or three-dimensional envelope over a few measured quality variables is a control chart that respects their correlation. Points outside are inspected; the volume of the envelope is a summary of how much variation the process is allowed.

    Fraud and transaction monitoring. An elliptic envelope over a handful of scaled features is a cheap first-pass detector, and its main virtue is that the decision is explainable: a flagged point is outside a described ellipsoid, not "the 4,217th leaf of a tree".

    Astronomy and survey pipelines. Outlier rejection before fitting a model is the same problem, and MCD is used to define the clean subset without letting the outliers set the scale. The robust covariance then feeds a Mahalanobis cut on the full catalogue.

    As a baseline for lof and isolation_forest. The envelope is the multivariate Gaussian baseline: if a non-parametric detector does not beat it on your data, the extra machinery is not paying for itself.

    As a preprocessing step for supervised models. Fit the envelope on the training set, drop or down-weight the points outside, and fit the model on the rest. The risk is the same as everywhere here: the envelope defines "normal", and a rare but valid subpopulation will be thrown away with the errors.
