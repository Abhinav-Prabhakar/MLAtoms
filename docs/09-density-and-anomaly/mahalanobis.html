    09 · Density & anomaly/tier 2/4 variants/source

    Mahalanobis distance and robust covariance

    Intuition #

    Euclidean distance treats every direction as equally surprising. Mahalanobis distance asks how far out a point is along the direction it lies in, measured in the standard deviations the data actually have:

           x  ← far in x, but the data are wide here,
      /        so this is ordinary
     /
    ·  ·  · ·  ·        the ellipse is one standard
   ·  · · · ·  · ·      deviation of the sample
   · · ·  · · ·  ·
    ·  ·  · ·  ·
        \  y  ← close in y, but the data are thin
               here, so this is anomalous

    Concretely: subtract the mean, rotate onto the covariance's eigenvectors, divide by the standard deviation on each new axis, then measure length. Those four steps are one quadratic form,

    \[d^2(x) = (x - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu) \qquad (1)\]

    and for a Gaussian it is not an approximation — (1) is the squared exponent of the density, so a level set of \(d^2\) is an ellipsoid of constant probability. That is also why the threshold is a real number rather than a tuning knob: under the model \(d^2 \sim \chi^2_p\), and the \(95\%\) quantile is a fact about the dimensions, not about the data.

    The whole difficulty is \(\Sigma\). The obvious estimator, the sample covariance, has breakdown point zero. Move a point far enough and it stops looking far: the mean travels toward it and the covariance widens along its direction until its own distance is finite again. This is masking, and it is not a bug in the arithmetic — it is the maximum-likelihood Gaussian doing exactly what it is asked, since a distribution that has widened to cover the outlier now explains it. The demo shows the extreme form. Four hundred points from a 6-dimensional Gaussian plus a hundred packed into a ball of radius \(0.3\) at distance 8 along one axis:

    variant AUC mean \(d^2\), outliers normals flagged
    hotelling_t2 0.340 4.43 6.36 0% of outliers, 12% of normals
    mcd 0.174 4.00 8.34 0% of outliers, 12% of normals
    ogk 1.000 82.23 6.11 50% of outliers, 0% of normals
    shrinkage 0.350 4.45 6.27 0% of outliers, 12% of normals

    The classical fit does not merely fail to find the outliers; it ranks them below the data and then flags the data. The variants are four answers to estimating \(\Sigma\) when part of the sample is not from the model:

    • hotelling_t2 is the classical estimate with the exact small-sample correction, and the \(F\) threshold that goes with it.
    • mcd is the minimum covariance determinant: the smallest-volume ellipsoid covering a fixed fraction of the data, refitted on what it covers.
    • ogk assembles \(\Sigma\) from pairwise robust scales, with no subset search.
    • shrinkage does not aim at robustness at all; it fixes singularity by pulling \(\Sigma\) toward a scaled identity.

    Variants #

    variant \(\hat\Sigma\) breakdown fixes measured
    hotelling_t2 sample covariance 0% nothing; it is the reference error 0.078 clean → 1.010 at 30%
    mcd min-det subset, reweighted \(1 - h/n\) scattered contamination error 0.141 → 0.134
    ogk pairwise \(Q_n\), reweighted 50% compact clusters too error 0.073 → 0.104
    shrinkage Ledoit–Wolf toward \(\mu I\) 0% \(n \le p\) singularity \(\lambda = 0.59\) at \(p/n = 2\)

    The measured column is the relative Frobenius error of the fitted covariance against a known truth, on 600 points in 6 dimensions with gross errors drawn from a wide Gaussian: hotelling_t2 0.078 at no contamination and 1.010 at 30%, mcd 0.141 and 0.134, ogk 0.073 and 0.104, shrinkage 0.070 and 1.014. Read it as two separate claims. mcd and ogk are insensitive to the contamination; the other two are not, and shrinkage tracks the classical estimate so closely that it is clearly not a robust estimator wearing a different hat.

    hotelling_t2 is the one to be precise about, because it is the variant with a distributional result rather than a heuristic. If \(x\) is a new observation and \(\bar x, S\) come from \(n\) others, then \(x - \bar x\) has covariance \(\Sigma(1 + 1/n)\), so the plain distance is inflated by that factor; dividing it out gives Hotelling's statistic

    \[T^2(x) = \frac{n}{n+1}\, (x - \bar x)^\top S^{-1} (x - \bar x) \qquad (2)\]

    which follows an \(F\) distribution exactly,

    \[T^2 \sim \frac{p(n-1)}{n-p}\, F_{p,\,n-p} \qquad (3)\]

    and the demo checks it. At a nominal 5%, averaged over 200 independent samples of \(n = 15\), \(p = 8\), the exact threshold (3) flags 4.4% of held-out points, the asymptotic \(\chi^2_8\) threshold on the same statistic flags 51.6%, and the \(\chi^2_8\) threshold on the uncorrected \(d^2\) flags 55.1%. At \(n = 200\) the three are 5.0%, 6.6% and 6.8%. The correction is not cosmetic: at \(n = 15\), \(p = 8\) the asymptotic threshold is wrong by a factor of ten, because \(\chi^2\) is the limit in which \(\Sigma\) is known, and here it is estimated from 15 points.

    mcd replaces the mean and covariance with the ones from the half of the data that is most tightly packed. Formally, over subsets \(H\) of size \(h\),

    \[(\hat\mu, \hat\Sigma) = \arg\min_{|H| = h} \det \hat\Sigma_H \qquad (4)\]

    with \(h = \lceil \alpha n \rceil\) and \(\alpha\) the support_fraction. The determinant is a volume, so (4) is asking for the smallest ellipsoid that still covers an \(\alpha\) fraction of the sample; \(1 - \alpha\) is the fraction of contamination it can survive, and the default \(\alpha = 0.75\) gives a nominal breakdown of 25%. The demo's 20% compact cluster is inside that budget and MCD still fails — see the failure modes — because the determinant is minimised by volume, and a compact cluster of outliers has less volume than the good data.

    ogk avoids the search. The identity \(\mathrm{Var}(u+v) - \mathrm{Var}(u-v) = 4\,\mathrm{Cov}(u,v)\) lets it estimate every off-diagonal entry from two robust scales of one-dimensional projections,

    \[s_{jk} = \tfrac{1}{4}\left(\hat\sigma^2(x_j + x_k) - \hat\sigma^2(x_j - x_k)\right) \qquad (5)\]

    which costs \(p(p-1)/2\) one-dimensional problems instead of a combinatorial search, and has the 50% breakdown of the scale it uses. The pairwise matrix is not guaranteed positive semidefinite (the \(s_{jk}\) need not be a covariance), so it is repaired by clipping negative eigenvalues, and the shape is then corrected by the transformation in the walkthrough.

    shrinkage is the regulariser. Ledoit–Wolf pulls the sample covariance toward a scaled identity,

    \[\hat\Sigma = (1 - \lambda) S + \lambda \frac{\mathrm{tr}(S)}{p} I \qquad (6)\]

    with \(\lambda\) estimated from the data rather than chosen. It never claims to be robust — the demo's \(\lambda\) on the masking data is 0.007, which is the sample covariance plus a rounding error — and it is the only variant that returns an answer when \(n \le p\).

    Derivation #

    1. Why (1) and not some other distance #

    Let \(S = U\Lambda U^\top\) and \(W = \Lambda^{-1/2}U^\top\), so that rows of \(XW^\top\) are the data whitened: their covariance is \(I\). Then

    \[(x - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x-\mu) = \lVert W(x - \mu) \rVert^2\]

    so Mahalanobis distance is Euclidean distance after whitening. Every property follows from that. It is invariant to invertible linear maps — whitening either version of the data gives the same \(I\) — and the demo confirms it: a random invertible matrix and a translation leave every score unchanged to \(3 \times 10^{-14}\). It is not invariant to a change of units that is not a linear map of the whole vector, which is why a column's scaling still matters through \(\Sigma\). And it degenerates to Euclidean when \(\Sigma = I\), so nothing here contradicts the simple case; it generalises it.

    The identity that the demo checks to \(10^{-10}\) is a useful sanity test for any implementation. With the unbiased covariance and the sample mean,

    \[\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^\top S^{-1}(x_i - \bar x) = \mathrm{tr}\left(S^{-1} \sum_i (x_i-\bar x)(x_i-\bar x)^\top\right) = \mathrm{tr}\left(S^{-1}(n-1)S\right) = (n-1)p\]

    so the mean training distance is exactly \(p(n-1)/n\) — 2.9925 for the demo's \(n = 400\), \(p = 3\) — whatever the data are. If a fit does not reproduce that number, the covariance convention (divisor \(n\) or \(n-1\)) is wrong, not the data.

    2. The exact threshold #

    For a new point, \(x - \bar x \sim N_p(0, \Sigma(1+1/n))\) independently of \((n-1)S \sim W_p(\Sigma, n-1)\). The standard Hotelling result for a normal vector independent of a Wishart matrix is

    \[z^\top S^{-1} z \cdot \frac{n-p}{p(n-1)} \sim F_{p,\,n-p}\]

    and applying it to \(z = x - \bar x\), whose covariance is \(\Sigma(1+1/n)\), gives (2) and (3). The \(\chi^2\) threshold

    \[d^2 > \chi^2_p(1 - \alpha) \qquad (7)\]

    is the limit of (3) as \(n \to \infty\): \(F_{p,\,q}\) converges in distribution to \(\chi^2_p / p\), so the product in (3) tends to \(\chi^2_p\), and \(n/(n+1) \to 1\). The gap between them is the price of estimating \(\Sigma\), and it is large exactly when \(p\) is a substantial fraction of \(n\) — the demo's \(n = 15, p = 8\) is more than half, and 5% becomes 55%.

    The calibration is also a warning about how to measure it. The \(F\) law holds when averaged over the estimation of \(\Sigma\): for one fixed fit every test point shares the same \(\hat\Sigma\), so the flag rate of that fit depends on how it happened to land, and a single fit measured on 200 000 points is one draw from the distribution of flag rates rather than the nominal 5%. That is why the demo averages over 200 independent samples instead.

    3. Why masking inverts the ranking #

    Take good data \(N(0, I_p)\), and put a fraction \(\epsilon\) in a tight cluster at distance \(r\) along \(e_1\). The mean moves to \(\epsilon r e_1\); the variance along \(e_1\) becomes \((1-\epsilon) + \epsilon r^2\), and along the other \(p-1\) axes it is \(1-\epsilon\). An outlier at \(r e_1\) is \((1-\epsilon)r\) from the mean, so

    \[d^2_{\text{out}} \approx \frac{(1-\epsilon)^2 r^2}{(1-\epsilon) + \epsilon r^2} \;\xrightarrow[\epsilon r^2 \gg 1]{}\; \frac{(1-\epsilon)^2}{\epsilon} \qquad (8)\]

    while a normal point near the origin pays almost nothing along \(e_1\) and a full standard deviation along the rest:

    \[d^2_{\text{in}} \approx \frac{p-1}{1-\epsilon} \qquad (9)\]

    At \(\epsilon = 0.2\), \(p = 6\) these are 3.2 and 6.25, against the demo's measured 4.43 and 6.36 — the approximations ignore the cluster's own radius and the sampling noise, but the ordering is the point. Two consequences follow. First, (8) has a limit in \(r\): moving the cluster further away makes it look more normal, because the variance grows like \(r^2\) while the distance grows like \(r\). Second, the gap widens with \(p\), because the outlier gains nothing in the other \(p-1\) directions while every normal point pays for them. In one dimension there is no masking by this mechanism; in ten it is catastrophic. The demo runs at \(p = 6\) because that is where the inversion is unambiguous.

    4. MCD, and why volume is the wrong thing to trust #

    Equation (4) has two moving parts: the subset search and the refit. The search is combinatorial — \(\binom{n}{h}\) subsets — so Fast-MCD takes random starts and applies concentration steps: fit on the current \(h\) points, sort all points by distance, keep the \(h\) closest, repeat. Each step cannot increase the determinant of the retained set, so the procedure converges, and a handful of starts find the global optimum with high probability for \(n\) in the thousands. The refit is the step that makes it usable: after the raw subset, points inside the 97.5% ellipsoid are kept and the ordinary covariance is computed on them. That restores consistency — the raw MCD covariance is biased low because it is computed on a truncated sample — and it is what makes the errors in the table comparable to the classical ones at zero contamination.

    The failure is structural. The determinant of a subset is a product of variances, and a compact cluster contributes a small variance in every direction but one. At \(p = 6\), shrinking five variances by a factor of ten more than pays for inflating one, so the determinant prefers a subset that contains the cluster — the demo's MCD ends up with a covariance that describes neither population, collapsed in most directions and stretched along the cluster's axis. Volume is the right criterion when the outliers are scattered, which is the assumption the method was designed under, and the wrong one when they are clustered, which is what masks the classical estimator in the first place. ogk has no such preference because a pairwise scale over all pairs is dominated by whichever group holds the majority of each projection.

    5. OGK's correction step #

    Equation (5) gives a matrix that is symmetric but not necessarily a covariance. Two repairs follow. The matrix is projected to the nearest positive semidefinite one by clipping eigenvalues, then decomposed as \(S = U\Lambda U^\top\) and used to decorrelate the data: \(Y = X S^{-1/2}\) has identity covariance under the pairwise estimate. If the shape were right, every column of \(Y\) would have robust scale one; whatever scale it actually has is the correction. With \(V = \mathrm{diag}(\tau_j^2)\),

    \[\hat\Sigma = S^{1/2} \, V \, S^{1/2} \qquad (10)\]

    which leaves the pairwise directions alone and fixes the lengths. This is what makes OGK work in the demo's masking case: the pairwise estimates recover the orientation of the good data, and the column scales then measure the good data's extent rather than a mixture. The final reweighting step is the same one MCD uses, and for the same reason.

    6. Why shrinkage tracks p/n #

    Ledoit–Wolf chooses \(\lambda\) to minimise expected squared error against the true \(\Sigma\), with the target \(F = (\mathrm{tr}S/p)I\). Writing \(d^2 = \lVert S - F \rVert_F^2/p\) for how far the sample covariance is from the target, and \(b^2\) for the total variance of the entries of \(S\) around their expectation, the optimum is

    \[\lambda = \frac{b^2}{d^2}, \qquad b^2 = \frac{1}{p}\sum_{ij} \mathrm{Var}(s_{ij}) \qquad (11)\]

    and the estimate is (6). The important scaling is in \(b^2\): \(s_{ij}\) is an average of \(n\) terms, so its variance falls like \(1/n\), and the sum over \(p^2\) entries then gives \(b^2 = O(p/n)\) while \(d^2 = O(1)\). Hence \(\lambda \approx p/n\) — the demo measures 0.59 at \(p/n = 2\), 0.11 at \(0.1\) and 0.02 at \(0.02\). Shrinkage is needed exactly when there are not enough observations per dimension to pin the covariance down, which is the same condition as singularity, so the same knob fixes both. At \(n = 30\), \(p = 60\) the demo's fitted covariance has condition number 7.7 and is positive definite, where the sample covariance has rank 29 and hotelling_t2 refuses to fit.

    The direction of the bias matters. Shrinking toward \(\mu I\) makes every eigenvalue at least \(\lambda\mu\), which is why the condition number is bounded, and it also understates the large eigenvalues and overstates the small ones. The demo's error at \(n = 30\) is 0.600 against the pseudo-inverse's 1.025, but that comparison overstates the case: the pseudo-inverse is not an estimate of \(\Sigma\) at all, it inverts a rank-29 matrix as if the 31 directions the data never spanned had zero variance. The honest comparison is with what an unbiased estimator would achieve given enough data, and that estimator does not exist here. That is the trade: a biased estimator whose variance is small enough to be useful, against an unbiased one that cannot be computed.

    Code walkthrough #

    1. The distance #

        diff = X - mean
    solved = np.linalg.solve(cov, diff.T).T
    return np.einsum("ij,ij->i", diff, solved)

    One solve rather than an explicit inverse, and then a row-wise dot product. np.linalg.solve(cov, diff.T) is \((p \times p)^{-1}(p \times m)\), which is \(O(p^3 + mp^2)\) and numerically better behaved than forming \(\Sigma^{-1}\); the transpose is because numpy's solve wants the matrix on the left. The einsum sums \(ij\) over \(j\) — the quadratic form, without materialising the \(m \times p \times p\) outer product. If cov were singular this raises LinAlgError, and fit checks the rank first so that the failure arrives as a clear refusal rather than as a division by a zero eigenvalue.

    2. The MCD search #

            idx = rng.choice(n, h, replace=False)
        for _ in range(n_csteps + 1):
            mean, cov = _classical(X[idx])
            d2 = _sq_mahal(X, mean, cov)
            idx = np.argpartition(d2, h - 1)[:h]

    A random start, then concentration steps. n_csteps + 1 is deliberate: the loop body fits first and trims second, so \(c\) concentration steps need \(c+1\) fits to end on a fitted subset. np.argpartition is \(O(n)\) and returns the \(h\) smallest distances without sorting the rest — the selection is all that is needed, and the order inside the subset does not matter. The determinant is compared in log space, via slogdet, because determinants of covariance matrices overflow quickly; the sign is checked because a subset of \(h\) points in \(p\) dimensions with \(h < p\) gives a singular matrix, and that start must be discarded rather than ranked.

    3. OGK's pairwise step #

            for k in range(j + 1, p):
            plus = _qn(X[:, j] + X[:, k]) ** 2
            minus = _qn(X[:, j] - X[:, k]) ** 2
            S[j, k] = S[k, j] = 0.25 * (plus - minus)

    Equation (5) literally, with \(Q_n\) as the scale. The diagonal is filled separately with \(Q_n^2\) of the column, since (5) is an identity about covariances and the diagonal is a variance. The loop is \(O(p^2)\) projections, each \(O(n^2)\) because \(Q_n\) needs the pairwise differences — which makes OGK quadratic in the sample, unlike MCD, and the reason it is the choice when \(p\) is large and \(n\) is not.

    4. The shrinkage intensity #

        outer = centred[:, :, None] * centred[:, None, :]
    # b^2 is the total variance of the sample covariance entries: sum_ij
    # Var(s_ij) / p. Var(s_ij) = Var(x_i x_j)/n, and the outer product gives
    # the per-observation term, so the double division by n is not a typo.
    b2 = float(np.sum((outer - S) ** 2) / (n * n * p))
    lam = min(max(b2 / d2, 0.0), 1.0)
    return mean, (1.0 - lam) * S + lam * target * np.eye(p), lam

    The outer product is \(n \times p \times p\), one \(x_t x_t^\top\) per observation, and S broadcasts across the first axis so the subtraction is \(x_{ti}x_{tj} - s_{ij}\) for every \(t\). The double division is where implementations go wrong: np.mean over \(t\) would give the per-entry variance of \(x_ix_j\), and the variance of the average \(s_{ij}\) is that divided by another \(n\). Getting this wrong makes \(b^2\) independent of \(n\), and then \(\lambda \to 1\) for every sample however large — the estimator collapses to the identity target and the error stops improving. That is why the demo prints \(\lambda\) next to \(p/n\) rather than only an error. The clip to \([0, 1]\) is not cosmetic either: \(b^2\) is estimated, and an estimate above \(d^2\) would ask for negative weight on \(S\).

    5. The threshold #

            if self.contamination is not None:
            return float(np.quantile(self.scores_, 1.0 - self.contamination))
        if self.variant == "hotelling_t2":
            return p * (n - 1) / (n - p) * _f_ppf(1.0 - self.alpha, p, n - p)
        return _chi2_ppf(1.0 - self.alpha, p)

    Three regimes in five lines. A contamination budget is a quantile of the training scores and makes no distributional claim — it flags that fraction of any sample, including a clean one. contamination=None asks for the calibrated threshold instead: (3) for the Hotelling statistic, (7) for the others. The \(F\) quantile is computed by bisection on the incomplete beta function, which is why the file carries _betainc and _betacf; the chi-square quantile is bisection on the incomplete gamma. Both are twenty lines of standard numerical code and remove the only reason this atom would otherwise need scipy.

    Complexity #

    For \(n\) points, \(p\) features, \(m\) queries, \(t\) MCD starts and \(c\) concentration steps:

    stage time memory
    classical fit \(O(np^2 + p^3)\) \(O(p^2)\)
    score \(m\) points \(O(mp^2)\) \(O(p^2)\)
    MCD \(O(t\,c\,n p^2 + t\,p^3)\) \(O(np)\)
    OGK \(O(p^2 n^2 + p^3)\) \(O(np)\)
    shrinkage \(O(np^2)\) \(O(np^2)\)
    all four, demo size under a second tens of MB

    MCD is linear in \(n\) per start and quadratic in \(p\) per fit, which is why it is the practical robust estimator at moderate dimension; with \(p\) in the hundreds the subset search is replaced by a projection-pursuit or a cell-wise method. OGK's \(Q_n\) is the awkward term: \(O(n^2)\) per projection and \(p(p-1)/2\) projections. For large \(n\) the scale is replaced by a faster high-breakdown estimator, or the pairwise step is run on a subsample; the structure of (5) is unchanged. The shrinkage estimate's memory is the only one that grows with \(np^2\), because of the outer-product array — a loop over observations would remove it at the cost of speed.

    Scoring is where these methods pay for their fitting cost: once \(\hat\mu\) and \(\hat\Sigma\) exist, a new point costs one solve, and the same covariance can be reused for a stream of points. That is why the classical and shrinkage variants are the default in monitoring systems, and why the robust ones are fitted on a window and then applied cheaply.

    Failure modes #

    Masking, in the strong sense #

    The demo's numbers are the definition: classical AUC 0.340 with outliers scoring 4.43 and normals 6.36, and 12% of the normals flagged at a 10% budget. In high dimensions the inversion is not a rare coincidence but the expected behaviour of a tight far cluster, per (8)–(9). If a Mahalanobis detector reports that the anomalies are unusually close to the mean, that is this, not a sign flip.

    A compact cluster defeats MCD #

    The demo's MCD scores AUC 0.174 on the same data, worse than the classical fit it was meant to repair, because the determinant criterion prefers a subset whose volume is smaller — and a tight cluster has less volume than the bulk. The breakdown point is about the fraction of contamination, not its geometry, and these are different failure modes. Remedies: increase support_fraction so the cluster cannot dominate the subset, run MCD per cluster or on a projection, or use OGK, which is insensitive here.

    n <= p is not a small-sample problem #

    With fewer observations than features, or with collinear columns, the sample covariance is singular and (1) is undefined. hotelling_t2 raises rather than returning a pseudo-inverse distance, because a distance in the null space is arbitrary — any value can be produced by moving within the subspace the data never spanned. shrinkage and mcd return finite answers by construction. In between, \(n\) slightly above \(p\), the distances are finite and wildly unstable, because the smallest eigenvalue is essentially noise; the condition number is the diagnostic, and the demo's table shows it climbing from 7.7 to 37.1 as \(n/p\) falls.

    The chi-square threshold assumes the model #

    (7) is a statement about Gaussian data. Heavy tails inflate \(d^2\) for everyone, so a nominal 5% threshold on a \(t_3\) sample flags far more; skew makes the ellipsoid the wrong shape, so the flag set is concentrated in one tail. The symptom is a detector that fires constantly on clean data. Fix the threshold with an empirical quantile (contamination), or transform the marginals first — but note that a marginal transform destroys the correlations, so it must be a copula-style or rank-based transform, not a per-column log.

    Qn assumes the bulk is inliers #

    ogk's 50% breakdown is real, but the guarantee is about the number of contaminated points, and \(Q_n\) is a single order statistic — a bimodal contamination that shifts the 25th percentile of differences will move it. It is also \(O(n^2)\), which is the reason production code uses faster scales at large \(n\). The demo's OGK is the most accurate estimator on clean data (0.073 against MCD's 0.141), so the method's cost is not efficiency but computation.

    Shrinkage is not robustness #

    The demo makes this explicit: on the masking data shrinkage's \(\lambda\) is 0.007, so it is the sample covariance, and its AUC is 0.350 against the classical 0.340. On the contamination sweep its error tracks the classical one almost exactly (0.278 against 0.273 at 5%). A scaled-identity target absorbs singular directions, not outlying ones — the two problems look similar and are not.

    Applications #

    • Statistical process control. Hotelling's \(T^2\) chart is the multivariate version of a Shewhart chart: fit \(\mu, \Sigma\) on a calibration window, monitor (2) on incoming batches, and use (3) for the control limit. The small-sample correction matters because calibration windows are short.
    • Novelty detection on features. A Gaussian fit on embeddings, with (1) as the score, is one of the strongest baselines for out-of-distribution detection in vision and language models — precisely because it uses the covariance that a distance-on-features detector throws away.
    • Quality control with contamination. A production line's calibration sample usually has some bad parts in it; MCD is the standard way to fit the process covariance without them, and the reweighted distances are what get plotted.
    • Anomaly detection under masking. ogk is the robust default when anomalies come in clusters — a bad sensor batch, a botnet's traffic — where the classical estimator provably inverts the ranking.
    • Regularising a downstream model. Shrinkage covariances are the standard input to Gaussian mixture models, linear discriminant analysis and portfolio optimisation, where the sample covariance is singular or nearly so.
    • Metric learning and matching. (1) is the distance inside a learned metric; re-ID, face verification and tracking all use a Mahalanobis form, and the covariance's conditioning is the practical issue at embedding dimension.
    • Where it is a trap. Clustered anomalies, heavy tails, \(n \lesssim p\), and any setting where "the mean and covariance of normal data" is not a meaningful object because normal behaviour is multimodal. The local methods earlier in this chapter exist for that last case.
