    09 · Density & anomaly/tier 3/3 variants/source

    COPOD — the empirical copula, one marginal at a time

    Intuition #

    Sklar's theorem says that a joint distribution is its marginals plus a copula, and that the copula is the part which carries the dependence. Estimating a copula is hard. COPOD makes the bravest possible assumption about it — the copula is the independence copula — and then everything becomes counting:

    \[p(x_1,\dots,x_p) \approx \prod_{j=1}^{p} p_j(x_j), \qquad -\log p(x) \approx \sum_{j=1}^{p} -\log \hat F_j(x_j).\]

    There is no bandwidth, no tree depth, no distance metric, no learning rate. The model is the empirical CDF of each column and the score is how improbable a point is under those CDFs. It is the simplest multivariate density estimator in this chapter, and on tabular data it is embarrassingly hard to beat.

    The picture is one histogram per feature, with the score read off the tail:

      density
    │        ▁▁▁
    │     ▁▁▁   ▁▁▁
    │  ▁▁▁         ▁▁▁
    └──┬───────┬───────┬──────► x
       q01     med     q99
       ↑               ↑
    score 4.6       score 4.6

    A point in either tail is surprising, and the score says how surprising. The symmetry in that picture is the whole design: the 3rd smallest value and the 3rd largest get the same score, because both are three counts from an end.

    That symmetry is wrong when the distribution is skewed, because the two tails do not carry the same kind of surprise: a lognormal is bounded below and unbounded above, so a point just under the minimum is impossible while a point far above the maximum is merely rare. The skewness correction tries to encode that. The demo shows both what it buys and what it costs, on a beta\((2, 5)\) sample with a probe beyond each end:

    variant score at \(-0.5\) score at \(1.5\) clean max
    copod 3.546 3.546 1.621
    copod_skew 3.546 7.092 3.200
    ecod 7.092 7.092 3.200

    The correction does not suppress the short tail; it doubles the long one. That is a prior about where anomalies live, and here the prior is arbitrary: both probes lie outside the support of the beta sample, so \(-0.5\) is exactly as impossible as \(1.5\) and gets half the weight. On a lognormal the same prior would be right, because the left probe is impossible and the right one merely rare. ECOD keeps the maximum of both tails at full weight and is the safer default.

    The structural limitation is independence, and it is not a small one. On a Gaussian with correlation \(0.95\), the points \((2.5, 2.5)\) and \((2.5, -2.5)\) have identical marginal coordinates, so every variant gives them the same score to machine precision:

    variant score \((2.5,2.5)\) score \((2.5,-2.5)\) clean 99th pct
    copod 5.087710 5.087710 5.642
    copod_skew 10.175420 10.175420 11.283
    ecod 10.175420 10.175420 11.283

    The true squared Mahalanobis distance is \(6.410\) for the first and \(250\) for the second. Neither is flagged, because along the ridge a 2.5-sigma marginal is ordinary: the clean 99th percentile is higher than both. One probe is a normal draw from the joint and the other is impossible, and the independence copula cannot tell which is which.

    Variants #

    variant per-feature score idea
    copod \(\tfrac{1}{2}(u_L + u_R)\) charge both tails equally
    copod_skew \(\max(\text{skew term},\ \tfrac{1}{2}(u_L+u_R))\) amplify the long tail
    ecod \(\max(u_L, u_R, \text{skew term})\) keep both tails at full weight

    copod is the symmetric estimator: the average of the two negative log-tail probabilities. It is the one to reach for when the marginals are roughly symmetric, and it is the baseline that any correlation-aware method has to beat.

    copod_skew adds the skewness term. The sign of the sample skewness picks the tail — right-skewed features score on the right, left-skewed on the left — and the score is the maximum of that term and the symmetric one. It therefore never lowers a score; it only amplifies points in the long tail. That is a prior, and priors are sometimes wrong.

    ecod is the same three terms combined differently: the maximum of the two tail scores and the skew term, per feature. It keeps the short tail at full weight, which is what saves it in the skewed demo. On perfectly symmetric data ecod and copod_skew coincide, because the zero-skew branch sums both tails and dominates both. ECOD is the recommended default of the two published methods; COPOD is kept for the historical record and because its symmetric score is easier to reason about.

    Derivation #

    The empirical marginal #

    For feature \(j\) with training values \(x_{1j},\dots,x_{nj}\), the empirical CDF is the fraction of the sample at or below a value. COPOD needs the two tails separately, and it smooths both by half a count so that neither is ever zero:

    \[L_j(x) = \frac{\#\{i : x_{ij} < x\} + \tfrac{1}{2}}{n+1}, \qquad (1)\]
    \[R_j(x) = \frac{\#\{i : x_{ij} > x\} + \tfrac{1}{2}}{n+1}. \qquad (2)\]

    The denominators are \(n+1\), not \(n\), and that is what makes the smoothing coherent: the three pieces — strictly below, exactly equal, strictly above — partition the sample with weights \((\text{below}+\tfrac12)\), the atom at the point, and \((\text{above}+\tfrac12)\), which sum to \(n+1\). For a value that occurs once, the atom has weight \(1/(n+1)\), exactly the spacing between consecutive plotting positions. In code the two counts are two binary searches:

        n = sorted_values.size
    below = np.searchsorted(sorted_values, x, side="left")
    above = n - np.searchsorted(sorted_values, x, side="right")
    return (below + 0.5) / (n + 1.0), (above + 0.5) / (n + 1.0)

    side="left" counts strictly less; side="right" counts at most, so \(n\) minus that is strictly greater. The half-count is the difference between a score of infinity at the sample maximum and a score of \(\log(2(n+1))\), and it costs one constant per feature.

    The symmetric score #

    The independence assumption turns the joint tail probability into a product, so the negative log is a sum. COPOD's symmetric version averages the two tails:

    \[s(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{p} -\frac{1}{2}\Big[\log L_j(x_j) + \log R_j(x_j)\Big]. \qquad (3)\]

    The average, rather than the sum, is what makes the score comparable between a point that is extreme on the left and one that is extreme on the right: a point at the sample minimum has \(L = \tfrac{1}{2}(n+1)^{-1}\) and \(R \approx 1\), and the average is half the surprisal of the tail it is in.

    The expectation has a closed form. Under any continuous marginal the probability integral transform makes \(L_j(X_j)\) uniform on \((0,1)\), and \(\mathbb{E}[-\log U] = \int_0^1 -\log u\,du = 1\). So the mean score is exactly the number of features, whatever the marginals are. The demo checks it:

    \(p\) mean copod mean copod_skew mean ecod copod 99th pct
    1 1.000 1.347 1.693 2.628
    2 2.001 2.694 3.387 4.023
    4 4.001 5.387 6.773 6.492
    8 8.003 10.775 13.546 11.379
    16 16.005 21.549 27.093 20.849

    The copod column is the theorem. The other two are proportional to it: the maximum of two or three terms exceeds their average by a factor that depends only on the variant, not on the dimension, so over this table copod_skew is \(1.347\,p\) and ecod is \(1.693\,p\) to three decimals. The constants are the expected maximum of those terms after the probability integral transform, and they are what a threshold fitted on one variant has to account for.

    Skewness and the two corrections #

    The sample skewness of feature \(j\) is the third standardised moment,

    \[b_j = \frac{m_{3j}}{m_{2j}^{3/2}}, \qquad m_{kj} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (x_{ij} - \bar x_j)^k, \qquad (4)\]

    with \(b_j = 0\) for a constant feature. A positive \(b_j\) means a long right tail, so the tail where anomalies are expected is the right one, and the skew term is \(u_R\); a negative \(b_j\) gives \(u_L\). When \(b_j\) is exactly zero the term sums both tails, which is the published convention and the reason copod_skew and ecod agree on symmetric data.

    The two methods then differ only in how they combine the three numbers:

    \[s_{\text{copod-skew}}(x) = \sum_j \max\Big(u_{T_j j},\; \tfrac{1}{2}(u_{Lj}+u_{Rj})\Big), \qquad T_j = R \text{ if } b_j > 0, \quad L \text{ if } b_j < 0. \qquad (5)\]
    \[s_{\text{ecod}}(x) = \sum_j \max\big(u_{Lj},\; u_{Rj},\; \text{skew term}_j\big). \qquad (6)\]

    When \(b_j = 0\) the chosen term is the sum \(u_{Lj}+u_{Rj}\), which is why the two variants agree exactly on symmetric data. Both are sums of per-feature maxima, so both are at least the symmetric score: the short tail is never ignored, only charged at the symmetric rate. What the skew sign changes is the long tail, and the demo's sample makes the difference visible — the right-tail probe scores \(7.092\) and the left-tail probe \(3.546\) under copod_skew, while ecod gives both \(7.092\).

    The sign test is fragile in one specific way. A skewness of \(10^{-17}\) is rounding noise, and its sign is meaningless, but it is still a sign. The implementation treats \(|b_j| < 10^{-12}\) as zero:

        safe = np.where(m2 > 0.0, m2, 1.0)
    b = np.where(m2 > 0.0, m3 / safe ** 1.5, 0.0)
    # the sign of a skewness of 1e-17 is rounding noise, not a shape
    return np.where(np.abs(b) < 1e-12, 0.0, b)

    Without that guard, the antithetic training sample in the correlation demo — which is exactly symmetric by construction — gets one column labelled "right" and the other "left" from floating-point dust, and the two probes stop having equal scores.

    What the score is, and what it is not #

    The score is a sum of surprisals, not a log-density. Under independence the two would coincide, but the marginals are empirical, so the score is a rank: it says how many sample points are less extreme, not how much probability mass is out there. Three consequences follow, and all three are failure modes below.

    1. The score has no units. A score of 6 means something different at \(p=2\) and at \(p=16\), and the threshold is a quantile of the training scores, not a probability.
    2. The score saturates outside the training range, because the empirical CDF has no data there.
    3. The score cannot see dependence, because it never looks at two features at once.

    Code walkthrough #

    Per-feature contributions #

    The whole estimator is one loop over columns, and the loop is written so that the per-feature matrix is the return value. Summing it is scoring; reading a row is explaining a point.

                left, right = _tail_probabilities(self.sorted_[j], X[:, j])
            u_left = -np.log(left)
            u_right = -np.log(right)
            symmetric = 0.5 * (u_left + u_right)
            if self.variant == "copod":
                per_dimension[:, j] = symmetric
                continue

    self.sorted_ is a list of sorted columns built once at fit time. Scoring is therefore \(p\) binary searches of \(m\) queries each, with no re-sorting and no state that depends on the batch — the same point gets the same score whether it arrives alone or in a thousand-row batch. That sounds obvious, and it is not: the reference implementation concatenates the training set with the test batch before computing the ECDF, which makes a point's score depend on which other points were scored with it.

    Choosing the tail #

                skew = self.skew_[j]
            if skew < 0.0:
                skew_term = u_left
            elif skew > 0.0:
                skew_term = u_right
            else:
                skew_term = u_left + u_right

    Three branches, one per sign. The zero branch is the surprising one: it sums rather than averages, so a symmetric feature contributes twice its symmetric score to copod_skew and to ecod. That is faithful to the published algorithm and it is why the two variants coincide on symmetric data, but it also means the score jumps discontinuously as the skewness crosses zero. In practice the crossing happens only for data that are symmetric by construction; for real data \(b_j\) is never exactly zero.

    Combining #

                if self.variant == "copod_skew":
                per_dimension[:, j] = np.maximum(skew_term, symmetric)
            else:
                per_dimension[:, j] = np.maximum(
                    np.maximum(u_left, u_right), skew_term)

    Two lines separate the published methods. copod_skew takes the better of the skew term and the symmetric average; ecod takes the best of all three. Since the skew term is one of the two tails (their sum when \(b_j = 0\)), ecod's maximum is the larger tail score, and the skew sign becomes irrelevant to the result — which is exactly the robustness ECOD was designed for.

    Fitting and the threshold #

    Fitting is a sort followed by a self-score, and the threshold is a quantile of the scores the model gives its own training data:

            self.scores_ = self.score_samples(X)
        self.threshold_ = float(np.quantile(self.scores_,
                                            1.0 - self.contamination))

    Scoring the training set through the same code path as a test batch is what makes the score a pure function of the point and the sorted columns. It also fixes the meaning of contamination: the threshold is the \(1-c\) empirical quantile, so exactly a fraction \(c\) of the training set is labelled -1, by construction, whether or not any of it is anomalous. That is why contamination=0.1 on a clean training set gives ten percent false positives, and why the parameter is a budget rather than a test.

    predict compares with > rather than >=, so points exactly at the quantile stay normal. With continuous scores that is a measure-zero tie; with discrete data it decides how a whole tie group is labelled, and the strict comparison keeps the flagged fraction at or below the budget.

    Complexity #

    Let \(n\) be the training size, \(m\) the number of points scored and \(p\) the number of features.

    operation time memory
    fit (sort each column) \(O(np\log n)\) \(O(np)\)
    score \(O(mp\log n)\) \(O(mp)\)
    per-feature explanation \(O(mp\log n)\) \(O(mp)\)

    Sorting dominates the fit and the binary searches dominate scoring. There is no iterative step, no random seed and no hyperparameter except the threshold, so the estimator is deterministic by construction — a property that is rare in this chapter and worth stating explicitly. The \(O(mp)\) memory for the per-feature matrix can be avoided by accumulating the row sums, at the cost of losing the explanation; the reference keeps it because \(p\) is usually small.

    Failure modes #

    Dependence is invisible. The demo's two probes have the same marginals and therefore the same score, while their true squared Mahalanobis distances are \(6.4\) and \(250\). Worse, neither is flagged, because the clean 99th percentile along the ridge is higher than a 2.5-sigma marginal point. If the features are strongly correlated — sensor arrays, prices, pixels — COPOD is the wrong estimator, and the fix is a method that models the joint: mahalanobis, isolation_forest, or a copula that is not the independence one.

    The score saturates outside the range. Every point beyond the training maximum scores \(3.200960\) in the demo, and the maximum itself scores \(3.202627\). "Largest value ever seen" and "a thousand times larger" are the same event. If the anomalies you care about are the ones just past the edge, this is fatal; tail extrapolation is the extreme_value atom, and a held-out calibration set is the cheaper fix.

    The score scales with the feature count. The mean is exactly \(p\) on uniform data, and the 99th percentile grows from \(2.628\) at \(p=1\) to \(20.849\) at \(p=16\). A threshold fitted on a ten-feature model is meaningless on a twenty-feature one, and adding a noisy feature raises every score, including the clean ones.

    The skew sign is a guess. copod_skew doubles the long tail and leaves the short tail at the symmetric value. On a bounded-below distribution the short tail is where the impossible points are, so the correction gives them half the weight of points that are merely far out. ECOD's max-of-both is the safer default; neither is right for every distribution.

    Constant features contribute a constant. A column with no variance has \(b_j = 0\) and \(L = R = \tfrac{1}{2}(n+1)^{-1}\) for every training point, so it adds \(\log(2(n+1))\) to every score and changes nothing. A test point that differs from the constant gets \(u_L = 0\) and \(u_R = \log(2(n+1))\), so the feature suddenly becomes a perfect detector. Drop constant columns before fitting.

    The threshold is a quantile, not a probability. contamination=0.1 puts the cut at the 90th percentile of the training scores, so by construction ten percent of the training set is labelled anomalous. On a clean training set that is ten percent false positives. The number is a budget, and it has to be chosen from the cost of a false alarm, not from the data.

    Ties inside the range collapse ranks. The half-count smoothing gives every point with the same value the same tail probability, which is correct but means a large tied group — a discretised feature, a sensor with few levels — cannot be ranked within itself. The score is still finite and monotone in the value; it just stops discriminating inside the tie.

    Applications #

    Tabular anomaly detection. This is the default first model for a table of mixed features, precisely because it has nothing to tune. Fit it, look at the per-feature scores for the top rows, and you have an explanation as well as a ranking.

    Interpretable alerting. feature_scores returns the contribution of each column, so a flagged row can be reported as "flagged because feature 7 is at the 0.1st percentile and feature 12 at the 0.3rd" rather than as an opaque score. That is the property that gets a model deployed in an operations setting.

    Baselines for density estimators. Any multivariate density model — kde, gaussian_mixture, a normalising flow — should be compared against the independence copula. If it does not beat COPOD on your data, the dependence it models is not worth the parameters.

    Feature screening. The per-feature score matrix is a cheap measure of which columns carry the anomaly signal. A column whose contribution is flat across the flagged rows is not doing any work, and can be dropped before a heavier model is fitted.

    Streaming and edge deployment. Fitting is a sort and scoring is a binary search, so the model is a few arrays of sorted values. There is no iterative solver to time out and no floating-point accumulation to drift, which makes it a reasonable choice where the model has to run on the device that produces the data.
