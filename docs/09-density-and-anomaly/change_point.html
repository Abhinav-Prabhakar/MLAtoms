    09 · Density & anomaly/tier 2/5 variants/source

    Change-point detection — where the process changed, not just that it is odd

    Intuition #

    An anomaly detector asks whether a point is unusual. A change-point detector asks whether the process that produced the points is still the same one. The two questions have different answers. A single spike is anomalous and is not a change point. A shift from mean \(0\) to mean \(0.01\) standard deviations is not anomalous at any single point, and it is a change point if it lasts long enough.

      x_t
  2 ┤              ┌───────────┐
    │              │           │
  0 ┤──────────────┘           └──────────
    └──────────────┬───────────┬──────────
                   t₁          t₂

    A spike is a point that does not belong to the segment it is in; a change point is a place where the segment ends. Fitting the first does not find the second: a robust anomaly detector will happily absorb a spike and see no change, and a change-point detector will happily report a change for a run of mildly unusual points that never rises to an anomaly.

    Every variant in this atom answers three questions:

    1. What does one segment cost? A negative log-likelihood of the points in it, given a mean and a variance of its own.
    2. What does a change cost? A penalty \(\beta\) per change, or a threshold on a test statistic, or a prior hazard rate.
    3. When do we decide? After seeing the whole series, or at each step with only the past.

    The answers to (1) and (2) are the offline methods, pelt and binary_segmentation. The answer to (3) splits the online pair, cusum and bocpd, from the rest. window_based is the cheap local test: compare two adjacent windows and ask whether they look like the same process.

    The demo's first series is four blocks of ninety points with means \(0\), \(2\), \(-1\) and \(0.5\), unit noise, and true change points at \(90\), \(180\), \(270\):

    method change points segments costed objective
    pelt, Gaussian cost 89, 180, 270 20 800 545.63
    pelt, mean-only cost 89, 180, 270 16 273 190.14
    binary_segmentation 89, 180, 270 1 059 545.63
    window_based, \(w=40\) 89, 179, 270 360 —

    All four recover the truth to within two samples. The exact optimiser and the greedy one report the same objective, \(545.63\), which is the only direct evidence that the greedy recursion happened to be exact here. The window method is sixty times cheaper in work than the exact optimiser and the least precise: its resolution is its window, and at \(w=20\) the same test reports only [89, 180], losing the third change.

    The two objectives in the table are not comparable. The mean-only cost fixes one variance for the whole series and therefore reports a smaller number for the same segmentation; only differences within a column mean anything.

    Variants #

    variant decides price of a change online
    pelt exact minimisation of (2) penalty \(\beta\) no
    binary_segmentation greedy minimisation of (2) penalty \(\beta\) no
    cusum sequential test on the running sum threshold \(h\) yes
    bocpd posterior over the run length hazard \(H\) yes
    window_based likelihood ratio between two windows \(\chi^2_2\) quantile no

    pelt is the reference implementation. It computes the exact optimum of the penalised objective by dynamic programming, and prunes candidate change points that provably cannot be optimal later. The pruning is what brings the cost down to linear in \(n\) when the number of changes grows with \(n\), instead of quadratic always. Use it when you want the answer and can afford to look at the whole series.

    binary_segmentation optimises the same objective greedily: find the single best split, recurse on both halves while the gain pays for the penalty. It costs \(O(n \log n)\) in the good case and it is not exact. The demo's five-level series shows the failure exactly: it reports [149, 200] and pays \(379.55\) where the optimum [56, 90, 149, 200] pays \(369.81\). Use it when \(n\) is large enough that PELT's bookkeeping matters, and check the answer against PELT on a subsample.

    cusum is Page's cumulative sum. It accumulates the evidence for a shift of a given size and alarms when the accumulator crosses a threshold. It is the oldest detector here and still the one on the factory control chart: constant memory, one pass, and a threshold with a known average run length between false alarms. The cost is a detection delay and a blind spot for shifts much smaller than the one it was tuned for.

    bocpd keeps a probability distribution over the run length — how many steps since the last change — and updates it with Bayes' rule at every step. It needs no threshold, returns a distribution rather than a point, and is the right choice when you want to say "the change probably happened between 88 and 92" rather than "there is a change at 89". The price is \(O(n)\) work per step, which makes it \(O(n^2)\) over a series.

    window_based slides two adjacent windows along the series and tests whether they come from the same distribution. It is the only variant here that reacts to a change in variance as readily as one in mean, and it needs no model of the segment cost. Its resolution is the window: two changes closer together than \(w\) are one change.

    Derivation #

    The cost of one segment #

    For a segment covering indices \(a+1\) to \(b\), length \(m = b-a\), with its own mean and variance, the negative log-likelihood at the maximum-likelihood parameters is

    \[C(a,b) = \frac{m}{2}\left(\log 2\pi + 1 + \log\frac{\mathrm{SS}(a,b)}{m}\right) \qquad (1)\]

    where \(\mathrm{SS}(a,b) = \sum_{t=a+1}^{b}(x_t - \bar x)^2\). The derivation is three lines: the Gaussian negative log-likelihood is \(\frac{m}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) + \mathrm{SS}/(2\sigma^2)\), the maximiser over \(\sigma^2\) is \(\mathrm{SS}/m\), and substituting it gives (1). The \(+1\) is the \(\log e\) from \(\log(\mathrm{SS}/m)\) replacing \(\log \sigma^2\) at the optimum.

    Because the variance is profiled out, a segment that changes only its spread is charged for it: a variance that doubles over a segment of \(m\) points costs about \(0.35m\) nats, which is linear in the length and eventually pays any fixed penalty. That is why model="gaussian" finds variance changes and model="mean" cannot.

    Both sums in \(\mathrm{SS}\) come from prefix sums, so a segment costs \(O(1)\) rather than \(O(m)\):

    \[\mathrm{SS}(a,b) = S_2(b) - S_2(a) - \frac{(S_1(b)-S_1(a))^2}{b-a}, \qquad S_1(t) = \sum_{s\le t} x_s,\quad S_2(t) = \sum_{s\le t} x_s^2\]

    This is the single most important implementation decision in the file: it turns every inner loop over a segment into two array lookups, and it is what lets PELT cost tens of thousands of candidate segments in a few milliseconds.

    The mean-only model is the same expression with the variance fixed at \(\sigma^2\), the robust estimate \(\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[(x_t - x_{t-1})^2]\). Successive differences are used because a change in the mean does not change them: the estimator is blind to the very thing being detected, so it is not inflated by the changes it is supposed to find.

    The objective and the price of a change #

    A segmentation is a set of change points \(0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_m = n\), and the objective is

    \[\min_{t_1,\dots,t_{m-1}} \; \sum_{k=1}^{m} C(t_{k-1}, t_k) + \beta m \qquad (2)\]

    with the convention that the last segment ends at \(n\). The penalty is the whole model-selection problem in one number. Too small and every noise fluctuation is a change; too large and real changes are absorbed into their neighbours. The default is BIC, \(\beta = 2\log n\), because a change adds two parameters (a mean and a variance) and the likelihood-ratio gain of a spurious split is asymptotically \(\frac{1}{2}\chi^2_2\), whose maximum over \(n\) positions is \(O(\log n)\). A real change of size \(\delta\) over \(m\) points gains \(m\delta^2/(2\sigma^2)\), which grows linearly in \(m\) and therefore beats any \(\log n\) penalty for long enough segments. That is the consistency argument, and it is why the penalty must be in nats and must grow with \(n\).

    The demo's penalty sweep makes the trade-off concrete on a \(300\)-point series with three changes:

    \(\beta\) changes penalised objective fit only
    0.0 137 89.43 89.43
    4.0 27 429.12 321.12
    12.0 3 487.20 451.20
    24.0 2 521.30 473.30
    48.0 1 554.83 506.83

    At \(\beta = 0\) the optimum is a segment every min_size points — 137 of them — because splitting can only reduce the fit. The fit-only column is monotone increasing in \(\beta\) for the same reason, and the count falls monotonically — the only thing a penalty is supposed to do. The objective values themselves are not comparable across rows, because \(\beta\) is part of the objective.

    PELT: the same optimum for less work #

    Let \(F(t)\) be the optimal objective for the prefix ending at \(t\). Then

    \[F(t) = \min_{s \in \mathcal{R}_t} \big[ F(s) + C(s,t) + \beta \big], \qquad F(0) = -\beta \qquad (3)\]

    and the optimal segmentation is recovered by following the argmin back from \(n\). The naive evaluation of (3) is \(O(n^2)\). PELT's observation is that most candidates \(s\) can never be optimal again. Suppose at time \(t\)

    \[F(s) + C(s,t) > F(t).\]

    For any later \(T > t\), splitting a segment can only lower its cost, so \(C(s,T) \ge C(s,t) + C(t,T)\). Therefore

    \[F(s) + C(s,T) + \beta \;\ge\; F(s) + C(s,t) + C(t,T) + \beta \;>\; F(t) + C(t,T) \;\ge\; F(T) - \beta + \beta,\]

    so \(s\) cannot be the minimiser at \(T\) either, and it can be dropped for good. The inequality \(C(s,T) \ge C(s,t) + C(t,T)\) is exactly why the cost must be a profile likelihood: two free segments fit at least as well as one, so the pruning is a theorem about this cost and not a heuristic that happens to work.

    The demo checks the theorem directly. On a \(300\)-point series PELT costs \(18\,779\) candidate segments and the unpruned recursion costs \(44\,850\), a factor of \(2.4\), and both return [60, 139, 208] with objectives equal to nine decimal places. PELT's advantage is asymptotic in the number of change points, not in \(n\): with three changes in three hundred points, most of the quadratic work is still being done.

    Binary segmentation and its failure #

    Greedy splitting takes the segment \([a,b)\), finds the split \(t\) maximising

    \[C(a,b) - C(a,t) - C(t,b) - \beta,\]

    and recurses while that gain is positive. The demo's five-level series has changes at \(60\), \(90\), \(150\) and \(200\) with levels \(0, 1.2, -0.6, 2.0, 0.3\). The first split is at \(149\), which is right, and the recursion then looks at \([0,149)\), which contains two changes. No single split of that half pays for itself — the two-level model fits neither side well — so the recursion stops and loses both. The exact optimiser pays \(369.81\); the greedy one pays \(379.55\) and reports half the changes.

    That is masking, and it is the reason to prefer PELT. The failure is not about the penalty: the two missed changes are jointly worth more than \(2\beta\) but neither is worth \(\beta\) on its own in the presence of the other.

    CUSUM: the sequential test #

    For a shift from \(\mu_0\) to \(\mu_0 + \delta\) with known unit variance, the log-likelihood ratio of the last \(m\) points is

    \[\sum_{s=t-m+1}^{t}\Big[\delta\,(x_s - \mu_0) - \frac{\delta^2}{2}\Big],\]

    so the running maximum over all starting points is the recursion

    \[g_t = \max\Big(0,\; g_{t-1} + x_t - \mu_0 - k\Big), \qquad k = \delta/2 \qquad (4)\]

    with an alarm when \(g_t > h\). The allowance \(k\) is half the shift the test is tuned for, and it is the only tuning in the recursion: too small and noise accumulates, too large and the test is blind to the shift it was built for.

    The threshold controls the average run length between false alarms, which grows exponentially in \(h\). With a known mean and \(k = 0.5\), simulation gives a one-sided ARL of \(951\) at \(h=5\), \(2\,556\) at \(h=6\), \(16\,690\) at \(h=8\) and \(34\,451\) at \(h=10\); the default here is \(h = 8\), so a two-sided test costs about one false alarm in \(8\,350\) samples. The detection delay is roughly \(h/(\delta-k)\), which is \(16\) samples for a one-sigma shift and \(5.3\) for a two-sigma one; the demo measures \(13\) and \(4.5\).

    Two details make the implementation usable. The reference mean is re-estimated from a warm-up window after each alarm, because a detector that keeps comparing against the original level will fire forever after one change. And the reported change point is the last index at which the winning statistic was zero — the start of the excursion that eventually crossed the threshold — not the alarm time. The alarm time is the change point plus the delay, and reporting it would bias every estimate late by exactly the quantity the test is worst at.

    BOCPD: a posterior over the run length #

    Let \(r_t\) be the number of steps since the last change, so \(r_t = 0\) means a change happened at \(t\). With a constant hazard \(H\),

    \[p(r_t \mid x_{1:t}) \;\propto\; \sum_{r_{t-1}} p(r_t \mid r_{t-1})\, p(x_t \mid r_{t-1}, x_{1:t-1})\, p(r_{t-1} \mid x_{1:t-1}) \qquad (5)\]

    where \(p(r_t = r_{t-1}+1 \mid r_{t-1}) = 1-H\) and \(p(r_t = 0) = H\). The predictive \(p(x_t \mid r_{t-1}, \cdot)\) is where the model lives. With a Normal-Inverse-Gamma prior on \((\mu, \sigma^2)\) the posterior after \(r\) observations is conjugate, and the predictive is a Student-t:

    \[x_t \mid r \;\sim\; t_{2\alpha_r}\Big(\mu_r,\; \frac{\beta_r(\kappa_r+1)}{\alpha_r\kappa_r}\Big)\]

    with the one-step updates

    \[\kappa_r = \kappa_{r-1}+1,\quad \mu_r = \frac{\kappa_{r-1}\mu_{r-1} + x_t}{\kappa_r},\quad \alpha_r = \alpha_{r-1}+\tfrac{1}{2},\quad \beta_r = \beta_{r-1} + \frac{\kappa_{r-1}(x_t-\mu_{r-1})^2}{2\kappa_r}.\]

    The Student-t is not decoration. Marginalising the unknown variance gives heavy tails, so one wild point does not by itself make a long run impossible — which is what stops the detector from restarting on every outlier. It damps the effect; it does not remove it (see failure modes).

    The branch \(r_t = 0\) gets the prior predictive, not a flat weight. Without it the model would treat a restart as equally likely at every value, which is wrong and, worse, asymmetric: a value near the prior mean would look like a cheaper restart than one far from it. The prior is centred on the series mean rather than on \(x_0\) for the same reason — a prior centred on the first point manufactures a change every time the series revisits its origin.

    The detector reports a change when the maximum a posteriori run length collapses, not when \(p(r_t = 0)\) rises. A single surprising point lifts \(p(r_t=0)\) for one step and then forgets it; the mode moves only when the evidence persists. In the demo the 3.8-sigma outlier at \(t=303\) lifts the short mass from \(0.006\) to \(0.1495\) for one step while the mode stays at \(33\), and the mode only collapses six steps later, at \(309\), to a run length of \(6\) — which points back at \(303\). The reported change is the outlier, not the true change at \(270\).

    The window test #

    Two adjacent windows of length \(w\), with means \(m_L, m_R\) and variances \(v_L, v_R\). Let \(v\) be the variance of the \(2w\) points pooled:

    \[v = \frac{v_L+v_R}{2} + \frac{(m_L-m_R)^2}{4}, \qquad G = w\big[2\log v - \log v_L - \log v_R\big] \qquad (6)\]

    The second term in \(v\) is the between-window mean difference converted to a variance, which is what makes the same statistic sensitive to a mean shift and to a spread change at once. Expanding (6) under no change, with \(a = \log v_L\) and \(b = \log v_R\),

    \[G \;\approx\; \frac{w(m_L-m_R)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{w}{4}(a-b)^2,\]

    two squares of standard normals, so \(G \sim \chi^2_2\) asymptotically. That gives a threshold with no tuning: since \(P(\chi^2_2 > x) = e^{-x/2}\), the level-\(\alpha\) quantile is \(-2\log\alpha\), Bonferroni-corrected over the number of window positions. For \(400\) points and \(w=40\) that is \(-2\log(0.05/321) = 16.8\), and the demo's twenty stationary series produce exactly one alarm over \(6\,420\) positions against the one that \(\alpha = 0.05\) predicts.

    The statistic is smeared over about one window either side of a change, because every window that straddles the change sees a mixture. Peaks are merged within \(w\), and the resolution is \(w\): at \(w=20\) the statistic at the third change is \(14.7\) against a threshold of \(17.5\) and the change is lost.

    Code walkthrough #

    Segment costs from prefix sums #

    Everything in the file is built on these six lines:

        m = end - starts
    total = s1[end] - s1[starts]
    total2 = s2[end] - s2[starts]
    ss = np.maximum(total2 - total * total / m, 1e-300)
    if model == "mean":
        return ss / (2.0 * sigma2)
    return 0.5 * m * (_LOG2PI + 1.0 + np.log(ss / m))

    starts is an array, so this costs every candidate start for a fixed end in one vectorised pass. The np.maximum(..., 1e-300) is not cosmetic: a segment whose values are all equal has \(\mathrm{SS} = 0\) and a cost of \(-\infty\), which would make the optimiser split every constant stretch into single points. The floor gives such a segment the cost of a very tight one instead.

    The pruning loop #

            reach = best[starts] + _costs(s1, s2, starts, t, model, sigma2)
        evaluations += starts.size
        k = int(np.argmin(reach))
        best[t] = reach[k] + penalty
        prev[t] = int(starts[k])
        # keep s only while it can still beat the best segmentation ending here
        keep = {int(s) for s, ok in zip(starts, reach <= best[t], strict=True) if ok}
        candidates = [s for s in candidates if s in keep or t - s < min_size]

    reach is \(F(s) + C(s,t)\) for every candidate at once, and best[t] is \(F(t)\) once the penalty is added. The set comprehension is the pruning test of the derivation: keep \(s\) only if \(F(s)+C(s,t) \le F(t)\). The or t - s < min_size keeps candidates that are not yet eligible, which matters because a candidate younger than min_size has not been tested and must not be discarded.

    The greedy split #

            base = _costs(s1, s2, np.array([a]), b, model, sigma2)[0]
        left = _costs(s1, s2, np.full(starts.size, a), starts, model, sigma2)
        right = _costs(s1, s2, starts, np.full(starts.size, b), model, sigma2)
        evaluations += starts.size
        gain = base - left - right - penalty
        k = int(np.argmax(gain))
        if gain[k] <= 0.0:
            continue

    np.full(starts.size, a) is the vectorised way of saying "every candidate has the same left edge". The recursion is an explicit stack rather than a Python recursion, which keeps deep segmentations from hitting the recursion limit and makes the order of the splits irrelevant to the result.

    CUSUM #

                up = max(0.0, up + (x[t] - mu) - k)
            down = max(0.0, down - (x[t] - mu) - k)
            if up == 0.0:
                last_up = t + 1
            if down == 0.0:
                last_down = t + 1
            if up > h or down > h:
                hit = (t, last_up if up > h else last_down)
                break

    The two accumulators are the two-sided test: one for an upward shift, one for a downward one, each reset at zero. last_up records where the current excursion began, which is the change-point estimate, and it is updated whenever the accumulator returns to zero. Note that the reset is t + 1, not t: after processing \(x_t\) the accumulator is zero, so an excursion starting at \(t+1\) has not yet seen a point.

    The run-length posterior #

            updated = np.empty(m + 1)
        updated[0] = hazard * prior_density
        updated[1:] = (1.0 - hazard) * grow
        total = float(updated.sum())
        if total <= 0.0 or not np.isfinite(total):
            mass[:m + 1] = 0.0
            mass[0] = 1.0
            continue
        mass[:m + 1] = updated / total

    The whole recursion is those five lines. grow is the elementwise product of the old masses with the Student-t predictive, computed for every run length at once; the new mass at run length \(r+1\) is \((1-H)\) times that, and the mass at \(0\) is \(H\) times the prior predictive. Normalising by the total is Bayes' rule. The total <= 0 guard matters on long series: with a thousand run lengths the predictive densities underflow to zero and the normalisation becomes \(0/0\).

    The sufficient statistics are updated in the same loop, shifted by one so that row \(r+1\) is the posterior after \(r+1\) observations:

            kappa_new = kappa[:m] + 1.0
        mu[1:m + 1] = (kappa[:m] * mu[:m] + x[t]) / kappa_new
        kappa[1:m + 1] = kappa_new
        alpha[1:m + 1] = alpha[:m] + 0.5
        beta[1:m + 1] = beta[:m] + 0.5 * (kappa[:m] * (x[t] - mu[:m]) ** 2
                                          / kappa_new)

    This is the conjugate update written incrementally. The \(\beta\) update is the textbook \(\beta + \frac{\kappa(x-\mu)^2}{2(\kappa+1)}\) with the new \(\kappa\) in the denominator, and the shift [1:m + 1] is what lets the run length grow by one without recomputing anything from scratch.

    Deciding where the change is #

        found = []
    for t in range(1, n):
        previous = map_run[t - 1]
        if map_run[t] < previous and map_run[t] <= max(min_size, 0.2 * previous):
            found.append(max(0, t - int(map_run[t])))

    A collapse is a drop to at most a fifth of the previous run length, with a floor at min_size so that the first few steps after a change do not count as a collapse from a run length of three. The reported index is t - map_run[t], because a run length of \(r\) at time \(t\) means the last change was at \(t-r\). The max(0, ...) is needed at the start of the series, where the estimated change can point before the first sample.

    The penalty #

            parameters = 2.0 if self.model == "gaussian" else 1.0
        return parameters * math.log(max(self.n_, 2))

    Two parameters per change for the Gaussian cost, one for the mean-only cost, and max(self.n_, 2) so that a two-point series does not get a negative penalty. The penalty is in nats, like the cost, and that is the only thing that makes them addable.

    Complexity #

    Let \(n\) be the series length, \(m\) the number of changes found and \(w\) the window.

    variant time memory
    pelt \(O(n)\) expected, \(O(n^2)\) worst \(O(n)\)
    binary_segmentation \(O(n\log n)\) typical, \(O(n^2)\) worst \(O(n)\)
    cusum \(O(n)\) per pass \(O(1)\) streaming
    bocpd \(O(n^2)\) \(O(n)\)
    window_based \(O(nw)\) \(O(n)\) or \(O(w)\) streaming

    PELT's expected linearity is in the number of change points, not in \(n\): the pruning removes candidates at a rate that depends on how much better the segmentation gets with more changes. On data with no changes at all, nothing prunes and the recursion is fully quadratic — which is the honest worst case and the reason the demo reports evaluation counts rather than claiming linearity.

    bocpd is the expensive one: every step touches every run length, so a thousand-point series costs a million predictive evaluations, vectorised into a thousand numpy calls. The file's largest series is \(360\) points and the whole demo runs in a quarter of a second. For a long stream you would truncate the run length distribution or use the online variant of PELT instead.

    Failure modes #

    The penalty is in nats, and everything depends on it. \(\beta = 0\) produces \(137\) changes in \(300\) points; \(\beta = 48\) produces one. The default \(2\log n\) is right for the Gaussian cost and is not right for the mean-only cost, which uses \(\log n\) because it adds one parameter. Comparing objectives across models — \(190.14\) against \(545.63\) in the first table — is meaningless: they are different likelihoods, not different segmentations.

    Greedy segmentation masks. The five-level series loses two of its four changes and \(9.74\) nats. The mechanism is that a single split of a segment containing two changes fits neither, so the gain never clears the penalty and the recursion stops. The diagnostic is cheap: run PELT on the same series. If the objectives differ, the greedy answer is not the optimum.

    CUSUM's calibration assumes a known reference. With a known mean and \(h=8\), the two-sided false-alarm rate is about one in \(8\,350\) samples. The demo measures eight alarms in eight thousand stationary points, an order of magnitude more. The reference mean is estimated from a twenty-point warm-up, whose standard error is \(1/\sqrt{20} = 0.22\) standard deviations — a fifth of the \(0.5\)-sigma allowance. A reference that is off by \(0.22\sigma\) turns the allowance into \(0.28\sigma\) in one direction, and the ARL at \(0.28\) is a fraction of the ARL at \(0.5\). Use a longer warm-up, or accept the rate and say so.

    CUSUM's delay is not a property of the data. At \(h=8\) and \(k=0.5\) a half-sigma shift is caught \(13\) times in twenty and takes \(21\) samples when it is; a one-sigma shift is caught every time in \(13\); a three-sigma shift in \(3\). The threshold and the allowance set the delay, and the threshold sets the false alarms. There is no setting that is fast and quiet at once; that trade-off is the method.

    BOCPD cannot tell an outlier from a change. The demo's series has one 3.8-sigma point at \(t=303\) and BOCPD reports a change there, six steps after the point, because the contaminated long run eventually loses to a fresh segment that also contains the outlier. The Student-t predictive damps this and does not prevent it. If your data has spikes, run an anomaly detector first and mask them, or use pelt, whose segment cost absorbs a single point into a long segment at a cost of a few nats.

    The window method's resolution is its window. At \(w=20\) the third change scores \(14.7\) against a threshold of \(17.5\) and is lost; at \(w=40\) it scores \(26.3\) and is found. Two changes closer together than \(w\) merge into one, and a change in the first or last \(w\) points cannot be seen at all.

    A mean-only model on a variance change finds the wrong thing. The demo's series doubles its variance at \(150\) and keeps its mean. pelt/mean reports [231, 234] — spurious changes inside the noisy half — and misses \(150\) entirely, while the Gaussian cost finds \(150\) exactly. If the spread can change, the cost must profile the variance.

    Stationarity within a segment is assumed. Every variant here fits a constant mean and variance per segment. A slowly drifting series will be chopped into many segments by PELT, or produce a stream of alarms from CUSUM, because the model has no way to say "drifting". Detrend or difference first.

    Applications #

    Statistical process control. CUSUM was invented for manufacturing (Page, 1954) and is still the standard chart: one accumulator per quality variable, a threshold set from the ARL the plant can tolerate, and an alarm that means "stop the line". The demo's delay table is the production trade-off in miniature.

    Telemetry and operations. Offline, pelt segments a metric series for a postmortem — "the latency changed at 14:32 and again at 15:10". Online, bocpd puts a posterior over the run length on a dashboard, which is more useful than a binary alarm because it degrades gracefully: an ambiguous point moves the distribution rather than firing.

    Genomics. Copy-number segmentation is change-point detection on a log-ratio series along a chromosome, and circular binary segmentation (Olshen et al., 2004) is the standard tool. The segments are the answer; the individual probes are not.

    Climate and hydrology. Regime shifts in temperature, rainfall and river flow are change points in a noisy series, and the penalty is the scientific claim: a segmentation with many changes says the climate is variable, one with few says it shifted.

    Finance. Volatility regimes are variance changes, which is why the Gaussian cost and the window test matter more here than a mean-only detector. The distinction between "a bad day" and "a new regime" is exactly the anomaly versus change-point distinction.

    As a preprocessing step. Segment first, then run an anomaly detector per segment. hbos or kde fitted on a series that spans a regime change will score the old regime as anomalous; fitted per segment, it will not. The two atoms compose, and the order matters.
