    Histogram density

    Intuition #

    Cut the line into bins, count the points in each, divide by the bin's width. That is a density estimate, and it is the one every plotting library draws when you ask for a histogram:

       density
      │        ┌──┐
      │   ┌──┐ │  │ ┌──┐
      │   │  │ │  │ │  │
      │───┘  └─┘  └─┘  └───
      └───┬────┬────┬────┬──→ x
        bin  bin  bin  bin

    Each bar's height is a count over a width, so the area is a probability and the whole picture integrates to one. The estimate is piecewise constant, and that is the entire difference from the KDE of the previous atom: a KDE puts a smooth bump on every point and adds, a histogram puts every point in a box and divides.

    \[\hat{p}(x) = \frac{\text{count of points in } \mathrm{bin}(x)} {n \cdot \text{width of } \mathrm{bin}(x)} \qquad (1)\]

    The only decision is the binning, and there are three classical answers plus one modern one:

    \[\text{Freedman--Diaconis:}\quad h = 2\,\mathrm{IQR}\,n^{-1/3} \qquad (2)\]
    \[\text{Scott:}\quad h = 3.49\,\hat{\sigma}\,n^{-1/3} \qquad (3)\]
    \[\text{Sturges:}\quad k = \lceil \log_2 n \rceil + 1 \qquad (4)\]
    \[\text{Bayesian blocks:}\quad \max_{\text{partitions}} \sum_{b} N_b \log\frac{N_b}{W_b} - \text{prior}\cdot B \qquad (5)\]

    The first three choose a constant width from a summary statistic; the last chooses a partition and lets the width vary from block to block. All three rules have the same \(n^{-1/3}\), which is the bias–variance optimum for a piecewise-constant estimate — a slower rate than the KDE's \(n^{-1/5}\) in width and \(n^{-4/5}\) in error, and the reason a histogram is a fast baseline rather than a competitor. Equations (2) and (3) differ only in how they estimate the scale: Scott uses the standard deviation and is exact under a Gaussian assumption, Freedman–Diaconis uses the interquartile range and survives a heavy tail. Sturges does not look at the data's spread at all, which is why it is the default in old software and the worst of the three on anything skewed.

    The whole estimator is np.histogram(x, bins="auto") in numpy, with the rule in place of "auto" and the counts divided by \(n\) times the widths. What the rest of this atom adds is the choice of rule, the product across features, and the arithmetic for scoring new points.

    The demo measures the whole story: on a lognormal sample, (2) asks for 29 bins of width 0.214, (3) asks for 19 of width 0.326, and (4) asks for 10. All three integrate to one to \(10^{-12}\). Quadrupling the sample cuts the integrated squared error by 0.53, against the \(n^{-2/3}\) prediction of 0.40 and the KDE's 0.33 — the histogram learns, just more slowly. And translating the sample by half a bin moves 12.8% of the probability mass to a different bin, because the bin edges move with the data: a histogram is not translation invariant, and that is a property of the estimator, not a bug in this one.

    Variants #

    variant rule width measured on 500 lognormal points
    fd_rule (2) constant, robust scale 29 bins, \(h = 0.214\)
    sturges (4) constant, no data 10 bins
    scott (3) constant, Gaussian scale 19 bins, \(h = 0.326\)
    bayesian_blocks (5) varies per block 2 blocks on a two-level sample, prior 4

    The first three are one line apart in the code — they all end in np.linspace(min, max, k + 1) — and the demo makes them comparable on the same data by reporting both the target width and the width actually used: the rule gives a target \(h\), and the implementation then uses the smallest number of equal bins that achieves it, so the realised width is at most the target (0.2137 against 0.2152 for Freedman–Diaconis).

    sturges is the odd one: ten bins for any sample between 257 and 512 points, regardless of whether the data are a spike or a uniform. It was derived for the binomial coefficients of a normal and for samples of a few hundred; at \(n = 10^6\) it still asks for about 21 bins, where (2) asks for hundreds. The demo's skewed sample makes the difference visible: ten bins hide the tail completely, twenty-nine resolve it.

    bayesian_blocks is a different kind of answer. On 800 points uniform on \([0,1]\) and 400 uniform on \([1,3]\) — a density that drops by a factor of four at \(x = 1\) — the dynamic program with prior 1 finds 84 blocks, prior 2 gives 21, and prior 4 gives 2. The two-block answer is the truth, and its boundary is at 0.997, one grid step from 1. The prior is not estimated from the data; it is a statement about how many change points you expect. That is the honest trade: the partition is exact for a given prior and the prior is a judgement.

    Derivation #

    1. Why the rate is n^{-1/3} #

    Write the point's position inside its bin as \(x = a + uh\) with \(u \in [0, 1)\). The expected estimate is the average of \(p\) over the bin,

    \[\mathbb{E}\,\hat{p}(x) = \frac{1}{h}\int_a^{a+h} p(t)\,dt = p(x) + h\left(\tfrac{1}{2} - u\right)p'(x) + O(h^2)\]

    so the bias is first order in \(h\), not second: it is the sawtooth in \(u\) visible when you compare a point near a bin edge with one at its centre. Averaging \((\tfrac{1}{2}-u)^2\) over the bin gives \(1/12\), and the variance of a Poisson count is \(p(x)/(nh)\), so

    \[\mathrm{AMISE}(h) = \frac{h^2}{12}\int p'(x)^2\,dx + \frac{1}{nh} \qquad (6)\]

    Differentiating and solving gives

    \[h^\star = \left(\frac{6}{n\int p'^2}\right)^{1/3} \propto n^{-1/3}, \qquad \mathrm{MISE}(h^\star) \propto n^{-2/3} \qquad (7)\]

    The constant is \(\int p'^2\), which is exactly as unknown as the \(\int p''^2\) in the KDE's optimum. For a normal density \(\int p'^2 = 1/(4\sqrt{\pi}\sigma^3)\), so

    \[h^\star = (24\sqrt{\pi})^{1/3}\,\sigma\, n^{-1/3} = 3.49\,\sigma\, n^{-1/3} \qquad (8)\]

    which is Scott's rule (3). It is not a heuristic: it is the normal-reference optimum for a histogram, and the constant \(3.49\) is \((24\sqrt{\pi})^{1/3}\) rather than a fitted number. Freedman–Diaconis keeps the same exponent and replaces \(\sigma\) with \(\mathrm{IQR}/1.349\), which is the normal-consistent scale estimated from the middle half of the sample; on a Gaussian the two differ by a factor of \(3.49/2.70 = 1.29\) in width. On the demo's skewed sample the robust scale is smaller still, so the gap widens: 29 bins from Freedman–Diaconis against 19 from Scott.

    The rate is the part worth remembering. The histogram's width shrinks like \(n^{-1/3}\) and its error like \(n^{-2/3}\); the KDE's width shrinks like \(n^{-1/5}\) and its error like \(n^{-4/5}\). The histogram is paying for the discontinuity at every bin edge — the bias in (6) is first order because the estimate jumps, where a kernel's smoothness makes its bias second order. The demo checks the rate on a standard normal: 2000 points give ISE \(2.56\times10^{-3}\), 8000 points give \(1.35\times10^{-3}\), and the ratio 0.53 brackets the predicted 0.40 from above, as a finite-sample measurement should.

    2. The product form, and what integrates to one #

    For \(d\) features, this implementation fits one histogram per column and takes the product of the per-axis densities. Each axis integrates to one because each density is counts over \(n\) times width, so the product integrates to one over \(\mathbb{R}^d\). This is the same decision as product_kernel in the KDE atom and it has the same limitation: it cannot represent a correlation between columns. A joint histogram would capture the correlation and would need \(k^d\) cells, which is the emptiness of §3 measured directly.

    3. Why high dimensions kill it #

    In \(d\) dimensions a grid with \(k\) bins per axis has \(k^d\) cells. The number of points is \(n\) and the number of occupied cells is at most \(n\), so as \(d\) grows the cells become singletons and the estimate at a cell is \(1/(n \prod_j h_j)\) — a number determined by the grid, not by the density. The demo's table is the measurement: 1000 standard normal points, Freedman–Diaconis per axis, 500 fresh points scored —

    \(d\) mean log density points at the floor occupied cells
    1 −2.81 0.2% 24
    2 −5.57 0.4% 265
    5 −19.46 1.8% 1000
    10 −41.78 4.0% 1000

    39 nats between one and ten dimensions at the same \(n\). The occupied-cell column is the mechanism: by \(d = 5\) every point is alone in its cell, so the density is one over a volume and the volume shrinks with \(d\). A KDE degrades more gracefully because its bandwidth is a length, not a grid; a histogram has no such knob.

    4. Bayesian blocks as an exact partition #

    For a partition into blocks \(b\) with counts \(N_b\) and widths \(W_b\), the multinomial log-likelihood of the binned sample is

    \[\log L = \sum_b N_b \log\frac{N_b}{n\,W_b} = \sum_b N_b \log\frac{N_b}{W_b} - n\log n \qquad (9)\]

    and the second term is constant across partitions, so maximising the first is maximising the likelihood of a piecewise-constant density. Adding a penalty per block gives (5); subtracting a constant penalty for each cut turns the problem into a shortest path, and the dynamic program

    \[\mathrm{best}(j) = \max_{i

    solves it exactly in \(O(K^2)\) for \(K\) candidate cut points. This implementation puts the candidates on a regular grid of n_grid = 512 cells, which is what makes a block of one point impossible: without a minimum width the likelihood is unbounded and the optimum is a partition into singletons. The grid is a resolution limit, not a smoothing parameter — the demo's boundary is at 0.997 rather than 1.000 because 0.997 is the nearest grid point.

    The penalty is the model selection. It is not estimated; it is the expected number of blocks, expressed in log-likelihood units. The demo sweeps it and gets 84, 21 and 2 blocks on the same data, which is the whole range from "follow every fluctuation" to "two pieces, which happen to be the truth".

    Code walkthrough #

    1. The three rules #

        bins = _sturges_bins(n)
    if variant == "fd_rule":
        q75, q25 = np.percentile(x, [75.0, 25.0])
        width = 2.0 * (q75 - q25) * n ** (-1.0 / 3.0)
        if width > 0.0:
            bins = max(1, int(math.ceil(span / width)))
    elif variant == "scott":
        width = 3.49 * float(x.std(ddof=1)) * n ** (-1.0 / 3.0)
        if width > 0.0:
            bins = max(1, int(math.ceil(span / width)))
    return np.linspace(low, high, bins + 1)

    The shared structure is the point: each rule produces a target width, and the code converts it to a bin count over the data's range. ceil means the realised width is never larger than the target, which matters for (2) and (3) — a rule that asked for 0.215 and got 0.25 would be a different estimator — and it means the last bin absorbs the rounding, so the bins stay equal. bins = _sturges_bins(n) is the fallback for a zero IQR or zero standard deviation: a constant column has no scale, and the alternative to a fallback is a division by zero. ddof=1 is the sample standard deviation, matching every other scale estimate in the chapter.

    2. The dynamic program #

        for j in range(1, n_grid + 1):
        i = np.arange(j)
        mass = cumulative[j] - cumulative[i]
        length = (j - i) * width
        # A block with no points has zero likelihood contribution and is never
        # useful; -inf keeps it out of the argmax rather than letting 0 log 0
        # stand for a density of zero.
        fitness = np.where(mass > 0.0, mass * np.log(np.maximum(mass, 1.0) / length),
                           -np.inf)
        candidate = best[:j] + fitness - prior
        pick = int(np.argmax(candidate))
        best[j] = candidate[pick]
        cut[j] = pick

    Equation (10), vectorised over the cut point \(i\) for each right endpoint \(j\). cumulative is the prefix sum of the grid counts, so mass is the count in \([i, j)\) for every \(i\) at once, and length is the block's width in data units. Subtracting prior per candidate is the per-block penalty, and because it is subtracted once per cut it is equivalent to subtracting it from every block — the constant for the whole partition does not affect the argmax. The mass > 0 guard is what keeps empty blocks out of the solution; without it, 0 log 0 would be nan and the argmax would return the first candidate silently. Backtracking through cut reconstructs the partition, and grid maps the cell indices back to data coordinates.

    3. Fitting one axis #

            for j in range(d):
            column = X[:, j]
            if self.bins is not None:
                low, high = float(column.min()), float(column.max())
                if high <= low:
                    edges = np.array([low - 0.5, high + 0.5])
                else:
                    edges = np.linspace(low, high, self.bins + 1)
            elif self.variant == "bayesian_blocks":
                edges = _bayesian_blocks(column, self.prior, self.n_grid)
            else:
                edges = _rule_edges(column, self.variant)
            counts, _ = np.histogram(column, edges)
            widths = np.diff(edges)
            self.edges_.append(edges)
            self.counts_.append(counts)
            self.densities_.append(counts / (n * widths))

    A loop rather than a single vectorised call because the rules need percentiles per column and the blocks need a dynamic program per column; a joint grid would be the wrong model anyway, per §2. The bins branch comes first, so an explicit bin count overrides the variant — that is how the demo puts two rules on the same grid and how a caller can reproduce a plot. The constant-column branch invents a unit-width bin around the value rather than returning a zero-width edge; the density is then \(1\) over that width, which is a statement about a point mass and is the least surprising finite answer. Storing counts_ as well as densities_ is deliberate: the counts are what the blocks objective is written in, and they are what a reader checks when the density looks wrong.

    4. Scoring, and the floor #

                index = np.searchsorted(edges, column, side="right") - 1
            index = np.clip(index, 0, len(density) - 1)
            outside = (column < edges[0]) | (column > edges[-1])
            value = np.where(outside, 0.0, density[index])
            total += np.log(np.maximum(value, FLOOR))

    searchsorted is the whole lookup: the bins are sorted, so a point's index is a binary search, and side="right" - 1 puts a point exactly on an edge in the bin to its right — the same convention np.histogram uses, which is what makes scoring the training data reproduce the fitted counts. np.clip handles the two endpoints, and the outside mask undoes the clip's optimism: a query beyond the last edge is not in the last bin, it is outside the support, and a histogram has no tail to put it in. The floor then turns that zero into \(-690.8\), the same number for every out-of-range query. total accumulates the log density across axes rather than multiplying densities, so a point outside on one axis adds the floor once and keeps the other axes' contributions — the log is the right space for the product form.

    Complexity #

    For \(n\) points, \(d\) features, \(m\) queries, \(k_j\) bins on axis \(j\) and a blocks grid of \(K\) cells:

    stage time memory
    rule edges, per axis \(O(n)\) plus a sort for percentiles \(O(1)\)
    bin counts, per axis \(O(n + k_j)\) \(O(k_j)\)
    bayesian blocks, one axis \(O(K^2 + n)\) \(O(K)\)
    fit, all axes \(O(nd + n\log n + K^2)\) \(O(\sum_j k_j)\)
    score one query \(O(d \log \max_j k_j)\) \(O(1)\)
    score \(m\) queries \(O(m d \log \max_j k_j)\) \(O(m)\)

    The binary searches are why scoring is fast: a fitted histogram is a lookup table, and a million queries cost a million small binary searches rather than a million distance computations. The blocks step is the only superlinear one, and it is quadratic in the grid, not in the sample: \(K = 512\) is about 260 000 candidate intervals, each one a vector operation. The per-axis memory is the bin count, not the sample — the estimate is a summary, which is the other reason it is used as a baseline.

    Failure modes #

    The bin origin is arbitrary #

    Move the sample half a bin to the right and 12.8% of the probability mass lands in a different bin. The estimate at a fixed \(x\) is a step function of the bin edges, so it is discontinuous in the data's location: x and x + h/2 can be assigned densities that differ by a factor of two. No rule in (2)-(4) fixes this, because each one computes edges from the sample's own minimum and maximum. The fixes are structural — average over several offsets, or use a kernel — and the practical consequence is that a histogram's density numbers should not be compared across batches whose ranges differ slightly.

    No tails, and the floor #

    Outside \([\min x, \max x]\) the estimate is exactly zero, and score_samples returns the floor \(-690.8\) for every such point however far away it is. On the demo's held-out normal sample that affects 0.2% of points in one dimension and 4.0% in ten; in a tail-heavy application it can be most of the interesting queries. A histogram has no model for what happens beyond its last edge, and a "density" of zero is not a density — it is an admission. Smoothing the edges, adding a parametric tail, or using a KDE are the alternatives.

    The curse, measured #

    39 nats between one and ten dimensions at \(n = 1000\), and at \(d = 5\) every point is alone in its cell. This is not a failure of the rule; it is the product grid. A joint histogram makes it worse, not better: the cell count is \(k^d\) and the sample is \(n\), so the number of points per occupied cell falls like \(n/k^d\). The diagnostic is the occupied-cell count in the demo's table. When it approaches the sample size, the estimate has stopped summarising and started enumerating.

    The rules encode a shape #

    Scott's constant is exact for a normal and wrong for anything else; on the lognormal sample it asks for 19 bins where the robust rule asks for 29, and Sturges asks for 10 regardless. A rule of thumb is a prior about the density's shape, and a histogram is more sensitive to it than a KDE because the width is the whole model. The symptom is a plot that is clearly too smooth (a wide lognormal tail collapsed into the last bin) or too spiky (empty cells in the middle of the data). Look at the plot, not just at the rule's number.

    The blocks prior is a model choice #

    The same data give 84, 21 or 2 blocks depending on the penalty, and all three are the exact optimum for their prior. There is no cross-validation in (5) and no asymptotic guarantee that the penalty is identifiable; the method is a way to explore how many change points the data support, not a way to decide. The grid adds a second resolution limit: no block can be narrower than range / n_grid, so a genuine spike narrower than that is invisible.

    Applications #

    • Fast baselines and plots. One pass, no parameters beyond the rule, and an estimate that is exact to compute. Every anomaly-detection pipeline should have a histogram number to compare against before a fancier estimator is believed.
    • Discretisation for information theory. Mutual information, entropy and transfer entropy estimators are usually histogram-based: bin each variable, count the joint table, and plug in. The bin rule matters more than the estimator, and (2)-(4) are the standard choices.
    • Change-point detection and astronomy. Bayesian blocks was developed for photon-counting light curves, where the rate changes abruptly and the blocks are the events. The same dynamic program segments any piecewise-constant signal: counts per window, click-through rates, binned financial data.
    • Feature construction. Histogram counts, bin indices and the distance to the nearest occupied bin are cheap features for downstream models, and a histogram of a residual is often the fastest way to see that a model is misspecified.
    • Streaming summaries. A histogram is a fixed-size sketch: update counts as points arrive, and the density is a lookup. That is why it is the default in databases and monitoring systems, where the sample cannot be kept.
    • Where it is a trap. Above a handful of dimensions, or whenever the tail matters, or when two batches have different ranges, per the failure modes. The KDE is the drop-in replacement at small \(d\); the local scorers later in the chapter are the drop-in replacement when only ranking matters.
