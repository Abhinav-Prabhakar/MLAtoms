    09 · Density & anomaly/tier 2/5 variants/source

    Autoencoder anomaly detection — what the network cannot rebuild

    Intuition #

    An autoencoder is trained to reproduce its own input through a narrow bottleneck. What it learns is the set of directions the data actually use — the manifold — and a point that needs directions the bottleneck cannot represent comes back distorted. The distortion is the score:

    \[e(x) = \frac{1}{d} \| x - g_\phi(f_\theta(x)) \|^2 \qquad (1)\]

    Read that as a distance, not a probability, and everything else follows. The score is large when a point is far from the learned manifold and small when it is on it. Those are different questions. A point in the middle of a hole in the data — a place the manifold spans but the data never occupies — sits on the learned surface and reconstructs perfectly:

            ·   ·   ·   ·
      ·   ·       ·   ·        · real data
     ·   ·    o    ·   ·       o hole: low score, no data
      ·   ·       ·   ·        × shell: high score, no data
        ·   ×   ·   ·

    The o and the × are both anomalies and the model sees only one of them. That is not a defect of the optimiser; it is what "distance to a surface" means.

    The simplest case makes the geometry exact. Take \(f\) and \(g\) linear, so the composition is a single matrix \(W = W_2 W_1\) of rank at most \(k\). The least-squares optimum over rank-\(k\) matrices is the orthogonal projector onto the top-\(k\) principal subspace, so (1) is precisely the squared distance to the \(k\)-dimensional PCA subspace and the total score is the discarded variance. An autoencoder is PCA with a learned, nonlinear projection. Every property of PCA that makes it a poor density model — it cannot tell a hole from a cluster, it has one global scale — carries over, and the depth buys a curved manifold rather than a flat one.

    The five variants are four ways to make the manifold harder to fake and one way to read the residual. The demo's central table is the eight-dimensional version of the picture above: five hundred points on a circle of radius \(0.5\) inside a random plane in \(\mathbb{R}^8\), thirty points in the hole at radius \(0.1\), thirty on a shell at radius \(1.1\).

    variant latent train score hole score AUC hole AUC shell
    ae 2 3.17e-04 3.44e-04 0.542 1.000
    ae 8 2.46e-06 3.74e-06 0.585 1.000
    denoising_ae 2 1.69e-03 5.48e-04 0.026 1.000
    denoising_ae 8 2.01e-03 4.56e-04 0.005 1.000

    The shell is found perfectly by both. The hole is a coin flip for the plain autoencoder and inverted for the denoiser: the hole scores below the data. That second number is the interesting one, and it is a theorem rather than a quirk — see the derivation.

    Variants #

    variant score what it changes
    ae \(\frac{1}{d}|x - g(f(x))|^2\) nothing; the baseline
    denoising_ae same, trained on corrupted input removes the identity shortcut
    vae \(|x - g(\mu)|^2 + \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\) puts a law on the latent code
    lstm_ae mean over \(T\) and \(d\) sequences, with weights shared across time
    thresholding \(\sum_j r_j^2 / v_j\) the residual, per feature scale

    ae is a two-layer encoder and a two-layer decoder with a latent-wide bottleneck between them. With latent equal to the manifold's dimension it is the right model; with latent too small it becomes a prototype detector and scores the wrong points; with latent large enough it can approach the identity and score nothing. The demo pins all three regimes.

    denoising_ae trains on \(x + \sigma\varepsilon\) with the clean \(x\) as the target. Copying is no longer available, so the network is forced to learn the structure that survives the corruption. The optimum of that objective is the conditional mean \(\mathbb{E}[x \mid \tilde x]\), which is a shrinkage operator: it maps every input toward the centre of the manifold. That is what makes it robust and what makes it blind to the hole. Use it when the inputs are genuinely noisy or when the bottleneck cannot be trusted to be narrow.

    vae replaces the deterministic code with a Gaussian posterior \(q(z \mid x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu(x), \mathrm{diag}\,\sigma^2(x))\) and scores the negative ELBO: reconstruction plus the KL divergence from the prior. The KL term is a prior on where a point may sit in code space, so an input that would need an arbitrarily remote code is penalised twice. Use it when you want the score to mean something in nats, or when you want to sample the learned distribution. The beta argument is the weight; 1.0 is the exact ELBO and on a thin manifold it collapses (see failure modes).

    lstm_ae is the same idea for a window of \(T\) steps: an LSTM reads the sequence and returns its final state \((h_T, c_T)\), a second LSTM is run for \(T\) steps on a zero input starting from that state, and a linear map turns each output into a reconstruction of the corresponding step. The weights do not depend on \(t\), so the model represents a phase rather than a list of values, and a window with an unseen phase reconstructs as well as a training window. Use it for telemetry, audio, and anything else where the same pattern can start anywhere.

    thresholding keeps the plain autoencoder and changes only the score. Each feature's squared residual is divided by that feature's training residual variance before summing, so a quiet feature's small absolute error counts as much as a loud one's. The sum is then a quadratic form with a known reference law under the model, which gives a threshold that does not need a contamination budget.

    Derivation #

    The objective and the linear case #

    Training minimises the mean squared reconstruction error,

    \[\min_{\theta,\phi}\; \frac{1}{nd}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_i - g_\phi(f_\theta(x_i))\|^2 \qquad (2)\]

    with \(f_\theta : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^k\) and \(g_\phi : \mathbb{R}^k \to \mathbb{R}^d\). Nothing in (2) mentions a density, and that is the point: the objective asks for a low-dimensional description that loses as little squared error as possible, which is a statement about the data's second moment, not its distribution.

    Let \(f(x) = W_1 x\) and \(g(z) = W_2 z\). Then \(g(f(x)) = Wx\) with \(W = W_2W_1\) and \(\mathrm{rank}\,W \le k\). Write \(\hat\Sigma\) for the sample covariance and \(\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d\) for its eigenvalues. The Eckart–Young theorem gives

    \[\min_{\mathrm{rank}\,W \le k} \frac{1}{n}\|X - XW\|_F^2 = \sum_{j>k}\lambda_j, \qquad W^\star = U_kU_k^\top \qquad (3)\]

    so the linear autoencoder is the projector onto the top-\(k\) principal subspace and its score is \(\|(I - U_kU_k^\top)x\|^2\), the squared distance to that subspace. Two consequences survive the move to nonlinear \(f,g\): the training error is a reconstruction error and therefore bounded by the discarded variance, and the score is a distance to a set rather than a height of a density.

    Why the hole is invisible #

    A point \(x\) on the manifold with noise \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)\) has residual \(x - g(f(x)) \approx (I - J_gJ_f)\epsilon\) if the manifold is learned exactly, so \(e(x) \approx \sigma^2\) and the score measures noise. A point at distance \(\delta\) off the manifold along a direction the bottleneck cannot represent has \(e(x) \approx \sigma^2 + \delta^2\): the score measures distance. A point inside the span of the manifold has \(\delta = 0\) by construction, so it scores like data. There is no \(\delta\) to measure.

    The demo makes the failure quantitative in two dimensions. A circle of radius \(0.5\) has intrinsic dimension one. With a one-dimensional bottleneck the model cannot represent the circle at all — it becomes a prototype, the training error is \(5.88\times10^{-2}\), and the hole at radius \(0.1\) lands closer to the single code than the circle does. The hole's AUC is \(0.093\): it is ranked below every real point. With a two-dimensional bottleneck the training error falls to \(4.30\times10^{-6}\) and the same hole scores \(0.924\). A third dimension buys nothing (\(6.80\times10^{-6}\)). The bottleneck width is the manifold dimension, and it is not something the data tells you.

    In eight dimensions, with a bottleneck of two or more, the hole returns to chance (AUC \(0.52\)–\(0.59\)) no matter how wide the bottleneck gets. Capacity does not rescue it, because the hole is inside the span at every width.

    Denoising estimates a conditional mean #

    The denoising objective is

    \[\min_{g}\; \mathbb{E}_{x,\tilde x}\|x - g(\tilde x)\|^2 , \qquad \tilde x = x + \sigma\varepsilon \qquad (4)\]

    and it separates pointwise: for each \(\tilde x\) the minimiser is

    \[g^\star(\tilde x) = \mathbb{E}[x \mid \tilde x]. \qquad (5)\]

    That is the whole difference. The plain autoencoder's target is the input, so the identity is a perfect solution whenever the bottleneck permits it. The denoiser's target is the clean input, and the best it can do is the posterior mean — a smooth map from the corrupted point to the centre of the mass that could have produced it.

    Equation (5) explains the inverted AUC. Near the hole, the conditional mean of the data given a noisy observation at the centre is the centre itself, so the hole reconstructs with error below the data's own noise level. The denoiser does not merely fail to see the hole; it prefers it. The effect grows with \(\sigma\): at corruption=0.3 the hole's AUC is \(0.026\) at latent=2 and \(0.005\) at latent=8.

    The variational bound #

    For a latent variable model \(p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and decoder \(p(x \mid z) = \mathcal{N}(g(z), \tau^2 I)\),

    \[\log p(x) \;\ge\; \mathbb{E}_{q}\Big[-\frac{1}{2\tau^2}\|x - g(z)\|^2\Big] - \mathrm{KL}\big(q(z \mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big) + \mathrm{const} \qquad (6)\]

    so maximising the bound means minimising reconstruction plus a KL penalty. With \(q = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \mathrm{diag}\,\sigma^2)\) the penalty is closed form:

    \[\mathrm{KL} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k} \left(\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - 1 - \log\sigma_j^2\right) \qquad (7)\]

    which is zero exactly when \(\mu = 0\) and \(\sigma^2 = 1\), and the code checks it to \(10^{-12}\): the demo builds an encoder that emits a fixed \((\mu, \log\sigma^2)\) and a zero decoder, so the score is the KL alone. For \(\mu = (1, 0)\) and unit variance the score is \(0.500000\); for \(\mu = 0\) and \(\sigma^2 = 0.25\) it is \(0.318147\).

    The gradient needs the sampling to be differentiable. The reparameterisation

    \[z = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \qquad (8)\]

    moves the randomness into \(\varepsilon\), so \(\partial z/\partial\mu = 1\) and \(\partial z/\partial\sigma = \varepsilon\) and backpropagation reaches both heads. The code's loss is (6) with \(\tau^2 = 1/2\); beta absorbs the ratio \(1/(2\tau^2)\), so beta=1 is the ELBO for that decoder variance and any other value is a \(\beta\)-VAE.

    Why the VAE collapses #

    The KL is charged per latent dimension; the reconstruction gain is bounded by the data variance. For data on a manifold of intrinsic dimension \(m\) embedded in \(k \ge m\) code dimensions, encoding the position costs roughly \(\frac{1}{2}\sum_j \mu_j^2\) while the gain is at most the variance the code explains, and the sampled code carries noise \(\sigma\) that the decoder must average out. Past a small \(\beta\) the constant solution — \(\mu = 0\), \(\sigma^2 = 1\), KL exactly zero — wins, and no amount of training leaves it because it is a genuine optimum, not a local trap.

    On the circle the transition is sharp and sits between 0.2 and 0.3:

    beta reconstruction mse mean \(|\mu|\)
    0.03 1.23e-03 1.297
    0.10 5.60e-03 1.100
    0.30 1.25e-01 0.004

    At beta=0.3 the latent mean has fallen to \(0.004\) and the reconstruction error is \(0.125\) — the variance of the circle itself. The model has learned to output the mean. The default is beta=0.1 for that reason, and the demo's KL check runs at beta=1 where the formula is the textbook one.

    The LSTM cell #

    The encoder is an LSTM with the standard gates, stacked into one matrix for speed:

    \[\begin{aligned} i_t &= \varsigma(W_i x_t + U_i h_{t-1} + b_i) \\ f_t &= \varsigma(W_f x_t + U_f h_{t-1} + b_f) \\ o_t &= \varsigma(W_o x_t + U_o h_{t-1} + b_o) \\ g_t &= \tanh(W_g x_t + U_g h_{t-1} + b_g) \\ c_t &= f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot g_t, \qquad h_t = o_t \odot \tanh(c_t) \end{aligned} \qquad (9)\]

    The forget gate is what makes the state a summary rather than a queue: \(c_t\) can hold a value across many steps, which is how a phase survives from the start of the window to the end. The decoder is the same cell run on a zero input with \((h_T, c_T)\) as its initial state, so all the information about the window has to pass through \(k\) numbers. Backpropagation through time is the chain rule applied to (9) in reverse; the only subtlety is that the cell gradient has two parents, \(c_t \to c_{t+1}\) and \(c_t \to h_t\), and both must be added.

    Standardised residuals #

    For the thresholding variant, let \(r_j = x_j - y_j\) and let \(v_j\) be the mean squared training residual of feature \(j\). The score is

    \[s(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{r_j^2}{v_j} \qquad (10)\]

    If the \(v_j\) were the true residual variances, each term would be \(\chi^2_1\) and the sum \(\chi^2_d\), giving a threshold with no tuning: \(7.815\) at the \(95\%\) level for \(d = 3\). The demo's quiet-feature probe is five standard deviations out on a feature whose spread is \(0.1\) while another feature's is \(10\). The unweighted score ranks it at the \(37.5\)th percentile; (10) puts it at the \(99.8\)th, with a score of \(26.72\) against a threshold of \(8.29\). The training set's mean standardised score is exactly \(d = 3\) by construction, which the demo asserts.

    The reference law is not exact, because \(v_j\) is estimated from the same data: the in-sample score has a heavier tail than \(\chi^2_3\) and the chi-square threshold flags \(6.2\%\) of the training set instead of \(5\%\). On fresh data from the same distribution the miss is worse — \(7.7\%\) — because the in-sample residuals are smaller than out-of-sample ones. That is the price of a distribution-free threshold, and it is the honest way to report it.

    Code walkthrough #

    The file is a small autodiff engine, an LSTM, and a detector, in that order. No framework is involved: every gradient below is written out and every one of them is checked by finite differences during development.

    The MLP and its two backward passes #

    _mlp stores both the pre-activations and the post-activations at every layer, because the backward pass needs the latter and the input gradient needs both:

        layers = len(params) // 2
    pres, posts = [], [X]
    for i in range(layers):
        z = X @ params[2 * i].T + params[2 * i + 1]
        pres.append(z)
        X = np.tanh(z) if i < layers - 1 else z
        posts.append(X)
    return X, (pres, posts)

    The output layer is linear: a tanh on the last layer would clip the reconstruction and put a ceiling on the score. _mlp_back turns \(\partial L/\partial y\) into parameter gradients, walking backwards:

        for i in range(layers - 1, -1, -1):
        grads[2 * i] = delta.T @ posts[i]
        grads[2 * i + 1] = delta.sum(axis=0)
        if i:
            delta = (delta @ params[2 * i]) * (1.0 - posts[i] ** 2)

    delta.T @ posts[i] is the outer product summed over the batch; delta.sum(axis=0) is the bias. The 1.0 - posts[i] ** 2 is the tanh derivative written in terms of its own output — the one line that is wrong if you differentiate tanh as 1 - tanh(z) on the pre-activation, which is a common slip. _mlp_input is the same walk without the parameter accumulation, returning \(\partial L/\partial x\):

        posts = cache[1]
    for i in range(len(params) // 2 - 1, 0, -1):
        delta = (delta @ params[2 * i]) * (1.0 - posts[i] ** 2)
    return delta @ params[0]

    The loop stops at 1, not 0, because the input has no activation to differentiate. That function is how the encoder learns: the decoder's input gradient is the encoder's output gradient, and without it the encoder would have no gradient at all.

    Adam on a list #

    Parameters are a flat list of arrays, so the optimiser is a loop and nothing else. The bias correction is the part that matters:

            for i, g in enumerate(grads):
            self.m[i] = 0.9 * self.m[i] + 0.1 * g
            self.v[i] = 0.999 * self.v[i] + 0.001 * g * g
            self.p[i] -= rate * (self.m[i] / c1) / (np.sqrt(self.v[i] / c2) + 1e-8)

    Dividing by c1 and c2 is what makes the first steps full-sized instead of \(\sqrt{1 - 0.999} \approx 0.03\) times too small. Without it the network takes thousands of epochs to leave its initialisation, and the demo's 600 would not be enough.

    The LSTM #

    _lstm_forward runs the gates in one matrix product and slices the result:

            z = X[:, t] @ W.T + h @ U.T + b
        i = _sigmoid(z[:, :width])
        f = _sigmoid(z[:, width:2 * width])
        o = _sigmoid(z[:, 2 * width:3 * width])
        g = np.tanh(z[:, 3 * width:])
        c_prev, h_prev = c, h
        c = f * c_prev + i * g
        tc = np.tanh(c)
        h = o * tc

    Four gates in one \(4h \times (d + h)\) product rather than four separate ones: the gate order inside the slice is a contract between forward and backward, and the cache tuple records it. _lstm_backward reverses the chain, and the only non-obvious line is the gate-gradient concatenation:

            dz = np.concatenate([
            dc * g * i * (1.0 - i),
            dc * c_prev * f * (1.0 - f),
            do * o * (1.0 - o),
            dc * i * (1.0 - g ** 2),
        ], axis=1)

    Each entry is \(\partial L/\partial z\) for one gate, in the same order the forward pass sliced them. The input gate multiplies \(g\), the forget gate multiplies \(c_{t-1}\), the output gate multiplies \(\tanh(c_t)\) and the cell gate multiplies \(i\). Getting one of those four wrong still trains — it just trains the wrong model, which is why the development check was a finite-difference comparison on every parameter, not a loss curve.

    The plain autoencoder #

    _fit_ae is one minibatch loop. The two lines that matter are the gradient chain and the loss scale:

                    delta_out = 2.0 * diff / (len(index) * d)
                d_code = _mlp_input(dec, dec_cache, delta_out)
                grads = _mlp_back(enc, enc_cache, d_code)
                grads += _mlp_back(dec, dec_cache, delta_out)
                opt.step(grads)

    2 * diff / (batch * d) is \(\partial/\partial y\) of the mean squared error over both the batch and the features — the d is there because the loss is a mean, and dropping it multiplies the effective learning rate by \(d\). The encoder's gradient is the decoder's input gradient, which is why _mlp_input exists. seen is the corrupted copy when noisy is set, while diff is always against clean:

                    clean = X[index]
                seen = clean
                if noisy:
                    seen = clean + self.corruption * rng.standard_normal(clean.shape)

    That pair of lines is the entire difference between ae and denoising_ae.

    The VAE #

    The VAE adds the reparameterisation, the closed-form KL, and the gradient of the KL with respect to both heads:

                    mu = head[:, :k]
                logvar = np.clip(head[:, k:], -8.0, 8.0)
                sd = np.exp(0.5 * logvar)
                eps = rng.standard_normal(mu.shape)
                code = mu + sd * eps
                out, dec_cache = _mlp(dec, code)
                diff = out - clean
                kl = 0.5 * np.sum(mu ** 2 + np.exp(logvar) - 1.0 - logvar, axis=1)
                total += float(np.sum(diff ** 2)) + self.beta * float(kl.sum())

    The clip on logvar is not cosmetic: \(\exp(\log\sigma^2)\) overflows to inf within a few epochs if the encoder is allowed to drive the variance to zero, and one inf in the KL poisons every parameter on the next step. The loss is a sum over features, not a mean, because (6) is a log-likelihood and a mean would silently change the balance between the two terms by a factor of \(d\).

    The backward pass differentiates through the sampling:

                    d_code = _mlp_input(dec, dec_cache, delta_out)
                d_mu = d_code + self.beta * mu / m
                d_logvar = (d_code * eps * 0.5 * sd
                            + self.beta * 0.5 * (np.exp(logvar) - 1.0) / m)
                delta_head = np.concatenate([d_mu, d_logvar], axis=1)

    d_code * eps * 0.5 * sd is the chain through \(z = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon\) with \(\partial\sigma/\partial\log\sigma^2 = \sigma/2\); the second term in each line is the KL's gradient. m is the batch size because the loss is a batch mean. Concatenating the two heads back into one delta is what lets _mlp_back treat the encoder as an ordinary network with \(2k\) outputs.

    Scoring and the threshold #

    score_samples dispatches on the variant and each branch is one line:

            if self.variant == "lstm_ae":
            return ((X - recon) ** 2).mean(axis=(1, 2))
        if self.variant == "thresholding":
            return (((X - recon) ** 2) / self.residual_ ** 2).sum(axis=1)
        if self.variant == "vae":
            head, _ = _mlp(self.enc_, X)
            mu = head[:, :self.latent]
            logvar = np.clip(head[:, self.latent:], -8.0, 8.0)
            kl = 0.5 * np.sum(mu ** 2 + np.exp(logvar) - 1.0 - logvar, axis=1)
            return ((X - recon) ** 2).sum(axis=1) + self.beta * kl

    The VAE branch scores the mean of the posterior, not a sample: a sampled score would not be deterministic, and the mean is the minimum-expected-error code. thresholding divides before summing, which is (10) exactly. The default branch is the per-feature mean of (1).

    _threshold is deliberately narrow about what it will claim:

            if self.contamination is not None:
            return float(np.quantile(self.scores_, 1.0 - self.contamination))
        if self.variant == "thresholding":
            return float(_chi2_ppf(self.alpha, self.n_features_))
        raise ValueError("contamination=None is only calibrated for the "
                         "thresholding variant; pass a contamination budget")

    Only thresholding has a null distribution to appeal to; the others get a quantile of their own training scores, which is a budget and not a calibration. Asking for contamination=None from ae raises instead of inventing a number. _chi2_ppf is the Wilson–Hilferty transform, accurate to \(0.5\%\) at \(d = 3\) (\(7.775\) against the exact \(7.815\)) and to \(2.5\%\) at \(d = 1\), which is well inside the error of any threshold anyone will use.

    Complexity #

    Let \(n\) be the sample count, \(d\) the features, \(h\) the hidden width, \(k\) the latent width, \(T\) the window length and \(E\) the epochs.

    step time memory
    one ae epoch \(O(n\,(dh + hk + hd)) = O(nh(d+k))\) \(O(nh)\) for the caches
    one vae epoch the same, plus \(O(nk)\) the same
    one lstm_ae epoch \(O(nT(h(d+h) + h^2))\) \(O(nTh)\) for the BPTT caches
    Adam step \(O(dh + hk)\) per layer three copies of the parameters
    scoring one forward pass, \(O(nh(d+k))\) \(O(nk)\)

    The plain autoencoder's cost is linear in every parameter and dominated by the first and last matrices; the bottleneck is never the expensive part. The LSTM is quadratic in \(h\) because of \(U\), and linear in \(T\) because the recurrence cannot be parallelised — that is the real cost of the sequence variant, not the extra parameters.

    The demo's largest fits are \(500 \times 8\) with \(h = 32\) for 600 epochs, six of them, and the whole file runs in a few seconds. Memory is the training set plus the caches; for \(n\) in the millions you would stream minibatches rather than hold data_, which this implementation does not do.

    Failure modes #

    The hole. A point inside the span of the learned manifold scores like data. The eight-dimensional demo puts the AUC at \(0.542\), \(0.524\) and \(0.585\) for bottlenecks of 2, 4 and 8 — no signal at any width. The diagnostic is to compare against a density model on the same data, or to check the score at points interpolated between clusters: if they score low, the model is a distance and not a density. The fix is a different atom (kde, hbos, gmm), not a different autoencoder.

    The denoiser's inversion. denoising_ae scores the hole below the data (AUC \(0.026\) at latent=2, \(0.005\) at latent=8) because the conditional mean pulls the centre of the manifold toward itself. If you use a denoiser and see a suspiciously clean score on your most obviously wrong points, this is why. The diagnostic is to plot score against distance from the data centroid; a denoiser's score is non-monotone in that distance.

    The bottleneck width. Too narrow and the model is a prototype: the circle with latent=1 has a training error of \(5.88\times10^{-2}\) and ranks the hole at AUC \(0.093\), below every real point. Too wide and it can approach the identity; in eight dimensions latent=8 reaches a training score of \(2.46\times10^{-6}\), which is the network memorising rather than modelling. The diagnostic is the training-error curve against latent: the elbow is the manifold dimension, and a score that keeps falling past the elbow is capacity you are spending on noise.

    Calibration. The default threshold is a quantile of the training scores, and those are optimistic: the model has seen the data. The thresholding variant makes the size of the miss visible, because it has a reference law — \(6.2\%\) of the training set flagged at a nominal \(5\%\), and \(7.7\%\) of fresh data. For the other variants the miss is unmeasurable from inside the model, which is the argument for holding out a clean set and setting the quantile there.

    Scale. The loss is on the raw scale, so a feature with variance \(100\) is worth ten thousand times a feature with variance \(0.01\). In the demo the network reproduces the loud feature to \(2\%\) of its spread and the quiet one to \(97\%\), and the quiet feature's five-sigma excursion is invisible to the unweighted score. Standardise before fitting, or use thresholding, which reweights after the fact. Neither fixes a feature that is constant in training and varies in deployment.

    VAE collapse. At beta=1 on a thin manifold the encoder falls into the prior and reconstructs the mean; the demo shows the latent norm collapsing from \(1.30\) at beta=0.03 to \(0.004\) at beta=0.3. The diagnostic is the mean latent norm and the reconstruction error of the training set: if both are flat, the KL has won. Lower beta, or standardise the data so the prior's scale matches.

    Applications #

    Tabular anomaly detection is the honest use: fit on a clean reference set, score new rows, set the threshold on held-out data. It is the right tool when the data lies near a low-dimensional curved manifold and the anomalies are off it — sensor arrays, calibrated instruments, transaction features after a transformation.

    Sequence monitoring is where lstm_ae earns its place. A window of telemetry that no longer looks like any window the model has seen reconstructs badly, and the weights shared across time mean the model does not care where in the window the pattern starts. The demo's sixty unseen phases score \(5.10\times 10^{-4}\) against \(4.84\times10^{-4}\) for the training phases, while a spike scores \(2.68\times10^{-2}\) and a doubled frequency \(9.95\times10^{-1}\).

    Image and audio defect detection is the classical denoising application: train on known-good patches with corruption, score by reconstruction. It works when defects are local and the good class is broad; it fails on the hole, so it is a poor choice for "missing part" defects that sit inside the normal span.

    Dimensionality reduction as a by-product. The latent code is a nonlinear projection, so the same fit gives you a visualisation and a score. Do not confuse the two: the code is optimised for reconstruction, not for separating classes.

    Where it is a trap. As a density model, on data with holes, on data whose anomalies are new combinations of normal features rather than new values, and on anything where a per-feature model already works — hbos will beat it there and you will be able to explain why. If the anomaly is a single coordinate out of range, no amount of manifold learning is needed.
