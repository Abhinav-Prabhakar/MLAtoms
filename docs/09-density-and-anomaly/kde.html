    09 · Density & anomaly/tier 1/8 variants/source

    Kernel density estimation

    Intuition #

    Put the same small bump on every data point and add them up. That is the whole estimator:

    \[\hat{p}(x) = \frac{1}{n h^d} \sum_{i=1}^{n} K\!\left(\frac{x - x_i}{h}\right) \qquad (1)\]

    It has no parameters in the sense that a mixture model has parameters. Nothing is fitted, nothing is iterated, and the sample is kept verbatim. The only decision is the width \(h\) of the bump — and that decision is the model:

      sample     |         |      |  |              |

  h small      /\     /\     /\/\        /\    /\
              /  \   /  \   /    \      /  \  /  \     spikes, gaps, no mass

  h right    _/    \_/    \_/      \____/    \/    \_   the shape of the data

  h large    \____________________.__________________/  one blob, all structure gone

    Every method in this chapter is built on this estimate. A histogram is a KDE with a box kernel and fixed bins; a copula is a KDE after reordering each coordinate; LOF, isolation forest and the autoencoder in the atoms below are answers to "how small is \(\hat{p}\) here", with different definitions of small and different tolerances for computing it. Getting the bandwidth wrong is the one error that propagates into all of them.

    The bias–variance structure is exact, and it is why the estimator has a rule of thumb at all rather than a fitted parameter:

    • Bias grows as \(h^2 \nabla^2 p\): a wide kernel averages the density over a region, so it can only see curvature, and it flattens peaks by an amount that depends on the local second derivative.
    • Variance falls as \(1/(n h^d)\): a wide kernel averages more points.

    Their sum is minimised at \(h \propto n^{-1/(d+4)}\), which is the whole content of the Silverman and Scott rules. Two consequences are unavoidable and both are measured below. The constant in that optimum contains \(\nabla^2 p\), which is what you were trying to estimate, so every rule of thumb is a guess about shape made from scale. And the exponent degrades with dimension: in \(d\) dimensions, halving the error needs \(2^{d+4}\) times as many points, which is 32 in one dimension and 16 384 in ten.

    Variants #

    variant is a triple — kernel, bandwidth rule, evaluator — and each row changes one of the three.

    variant kernel bandwidth evaluator the trade
    gaussian Gaussian (2) Scott (6) dense smooth everywhere, mass in the tails
    epanechnikov Epanechnikov (3) Scott dense minimum-variance order-2 kernel
    tophat top-hat (4) Scott dense fastest, discontinuous estimate, 0.930 efficiency
    silverman Gaussian Silverman (5) dense survives a heavy tail
    scott Gaussian Scott (6) dense the default, and fooled by one outlier
    cv_bandwidth Gaussian leave-one-out (7) dense shape-aware, and it undersmooths
    tree_kde Gaussian scalar or fixed ball tree the same estimate for less work
    product_kernel Gaussian per dimension (6) dense a different scale on each axis

    The kernel choice is the cheap one, and the demo measures how cheap. Two hundred points from a bimodal mixture, all three kernels at Scott's bandwidth \(h = 0.415\), against the true density on 901 grid points:

    kernel integrated squared error
    Gaussian 8.76e-03
    Epanechnikov 1.05e-02
    top-hat 8.22e-03

    A factor of 1.3 between the best and the worst kernel — and the top-hat wins, which is noise, since the three asymptotic efficiencies are 1.000, 0.951 and 0.930. Changing \(h\) instead, to the cross-validated 0.119, costs a factor of 2.4. One number decides the estimate; the kernel only decides the constant in front of the error.

    The two rules disagree by more than that on a skewed sample. Two hundred lognormal points, where the standard deviation is inflated by the tail:

    rule bandwidth leave-one-out log-likelihood
    Scott (6) 0.976 −2.0984
    Silverman (5) 0.420 −8.6035
    cross-validated (7) 1.266 −2.0616

    Silverman's robust scale is 1.347 against a standard deviation of 2.816, so the two formulas differ by a factor of 2.3 on the same data, and the likelihood prefers the wider of the two — on this sample the tail is signal, not contamination. That is the honest summary of bandwidth selection: a formula encodes an assumption about shape, cross-validation encodes the data, and neither is free. The formulas cost one pass over the sample. Cross-validation costs sixty likelihoods of an \(n \times n\) kernel, which is why it is not the default.

    Derivation #

    1. The estimator as a convolution #

    \(\hat{p}\) is the empirical measure convolved with the scaled kernel, \(\hat{p} = \mathbb{P}_n * K_h\). Taking expectations under the true density,

    \[\mathbb{E}\,\hat{p}(x) = \int p(y)\, K_h(x - y)\, dy = (p * K_h)(x)\]

    so the bias is the error of a convolution approximation, which Taylor expands to

    \[\mathbb{E}\,\hat{p}(x) - p(x) = \tfrac{1}{2} h^2 \sigma_K^2 \nabla^2 p(x) + O(h^4), \qquad \sigma_K^2 = \int \lVert u \rVert^2 K(u)\,du\]

    and the variance follows from independence of the summands:

    \[\mathrm{Var}\,\hat{p}(x) = \frac{1}{n h^d} R(K)\, p(x) + O(n^{-1}), \qquad R(K) = \int K(u)^2\,du\]

    Integrating squared bias plus variance gives the asymptotic mean integrated squared error, and differentiating it in \(h\) gives the optimum:

    \[\mathrm{AMISE}(h) = \tfrac{1}{4} h^4 \sigma_K^4 R(\nabla^2 p) + \frac{R(K)}{n h^d}, \qquad h^\star = \left[\frac{d\, R(K)}{n\, \sigma_K^4\, R(\nabla^2 p)}\right]^{1/(d+4)}\]

    with the minimum AMISE falling like \(n^{-4/(d+4)}\). That pair is why every rule in the file has the same \(n^{-1/(d+4)}\) shape and only the constant differs: the constant is \(R(\nabla^2 p)^{-1/(d+4)}\), and no rule can know it. Scott's rule replaces it with the value it takes for a Gaussian, which is where the \(1.06\) of the one-dimensional version comes from; Silverman's replaces it with a robust scale so that one far outlier cannot set the width of the whole sample.

    The exponent is the part with teeth. To halve the error you multiply \(n\) by \(2^{d+4}\):

    dimension \(d\) 1 2 3 5 10
    points needed to halve the error 32 64 128 512 16 384

    2. The three kernels #

    With \(V_d = \pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2 + 1)\) the volume of the unit ball,

    \[K(u) = (2\pi)^{-d/2} \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert u\rVert^2\right) \qquad (2)\]
    \[K(u) = \frac{d+2}{2 V_d}\left(1 - \lVert u\rVert^2\right)_+ \qquad (3)\]
    \[K(u) = \frac{1}{V_d}\,\mathbf{1}\!\left[\lVert u\rVert \le 1\right] \qquad (4)\]

    The constants are not decoration. For (3), integrate in spherical shells with surface area \(S_{d-1} = d V_d\):

    \[\int K = \frac{d+2}{2 V_d} \cdot d V_d \int_0^1 (1 - r^2) r^{d-1} dr = \frac{d+2}{2}\left(\frac{1}{d} - \frac{1}{d+2}\right) = 1\]

    which is the one line a from-scratch implementation usually gets wrong, in the same way that (4) is wrong without \(V_d\): a unit cube's worth of intuition in three dimensions is off by \(4\pi/3\). The demo checks all three kernels by radial quadrature in \(d = 1, 2, 3\) and the worst error is \(2.1 \times 10^{-10}\), which is the quadrature's error and not the constants'.

    All three are non-negative, integrate to one, have their second moment fixed by the bandwidth, and are of order two — they reproduce linear functions exactly, so the bias starts at \(h^2\) and no kernel is better than another asymptotically except through the constant in the AMISE. Epanechnikov's minimises \(R(K)\) subject to that constraint, which is the sense in which it is optimal; the Gaussian's advantage is that it is smooth, so the estimate is differentiable everywhere and the constants in the next order of the bias expansion are finite.

    3. Rules of thumb #

    Silverman's rule is the one-dimensional rule applied per column:

    \[h_S = 0.9 \min\!\left(\hat{\sigma},\ \frac{\mathrm{IQR}}{1.349}\right)\, n^{-1/(d+4)} \qquad (5)\]

    and Scott's is its non-robust sibling,

    \[h_{\text{scott}} = \hat{\sigma}\, n^{-1/(d+4)} \qquad (6)\]

    The \(1.349\) is \(2\Phi^{-1}(0.75)\), so \(\mathrm{IQR}/1.349\) is a standard deviation estimated from the middle half of the sample, and it is what makes (5) survive a heavy tail. The \(1.06\) that appears in some textbook versions of Scott's rule is the one-dimensional Gaussian constant; here the coefficient is \(1\) and the exponent already contains the dimension, which is the multivariate form.

    Both are scale-equivariant: multiplying the data by \(c\) multiplies \(h\) by \(c\) and leaves the estimate unchanged. Neither is shape-equivariant, and that is the failure the demo measures — a bimodal sample with modes at \(-1.2\) and \(1.0\) has \(\hat\sigma = 1.197\), which is mostly the gap between the modes, so Scott's rule returns \(h = 0.415\) and smooths two peaks into one hump. Silverman's rule does the same thing whenever the modes are further apart than a standard deviation.

    4. Bandwidth by cross-validation #

    The alternative to a formula is to choose \(h\) by the criterion you will be judged on, and the natural one is the likelihood of the data under the model. The in-sample likelihood is useless — it increases without bound as \(h \to 0\), since the estimate becomes a sum of delta functions — so each point is scored against the estimate built from the other points:

    \[\hat{h} = \arg\max_h \sum_i \log\!\left(\frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{j \ne i} K_h(x_i - x_j)\right) \qquad (7)\]

    Computing the inner sum for all \(i\) at once is one \(n \times n\) kernel matrix with its diagonal removed, which is why (7) is a matrix expression rather than \(n\) separate estimates. The optimisation is one-dimensional, and the objective is smooth and unimodal in \(\log h\) on any sensible range, so sixty golden-section steps find the argmax to machine precision without a grid.

    Equation (7) is not a good estimator of the ISE-optimal bandwidth, and the demo shows it failing by a factor of 2.4. The reason is in the expression: each term contains \(-\log h\) from the normalisation, so the objective rewards shrinking \(h\) until the nearest neighbour falls outside the kernel. On the bimodal sample it chooses \(h = 0.119\) — less than a third of Scott's — where the leave-one-out likelihood really is higher, and the integrated squared error is \(2.11 \times 10^{-2}\) against \(8.76 \times 10^{-3}\). The likelihood is a local criterion: it weights relative error where the data actually is, so it chases sharp modes and does not care that the gaps between them are wrong. Least-squares cross-validation, which compares \(\hat{p}\) with a held-out estimate instead of evaluating it at the sample, tracks ISE and is what a production selector should use; it is not implemented here because the point of this atom is the estimator, not the selector zoo.

    5. Pruning a tree #

    The kernel is a decreasing function of distance, so a ball gives a bound for free. For a node with centre \(c\), radius \(r\) and \(m\) points, no point in the node is closer to a query \(x\) than \(\max(\lVert x - c\rVert - r, 0)\), so

    \[\sum_{i \in \text{node}} K_h(x - x_i) \;\le\; \frac{m}{h^d}\, K\!\left(\left(\frac{\max(\lVert x - c\rVert - r, 0)}{h}\right)^2\right) \qquad (8)\]

    If that bound is negligible against the sum accumulated so far, the whole subtree is skipped. Two details make the difference between a bound that helps and one that does not. The comparison is against the running sum, so a node's worth is judged relative to what is already known rather than to an absolute threshold. And the bound can be exactly zero: for the Gaussian, radii beyond about \(38h\) underflow, and a far query then prunes everything. Both cases are in the demo, and the second one is a bug in the obvious implementation — a purely relative test never fires while the running sum is still zero, which is exactly the far field.

    The guarantee is per node: each skipped node contributes less than the tolerance times the sum at the moment it was tested, so the total relative error is bounded by the tolerance times the number of skipped nodes, not by the tolerance itself. With tol = 1e-8 and a few hundred nodes the worst case is \(10^{-5}\); measured, the tree agrees with the brute-force sum to \(4.1 \times 10^{-11}\) in log density.

    6. The product kernel #

    A per-dimension bandwidth replaces the isotropic scaling with a diagonal one:

    \[K_h(x - x_i) = \prod_{j=1}^{d} \frac{1}{h_j}\, k\!\left(\frac{x_j - x_{ij}}{h_j}\right) \qquad (9)\]

    For the Gaussian kernel this is a Mahalanobis distance with \(\Sigma = \mathrm{diag}(h_1^2, \dots, h_d^2)\): dividing the difference by \(h\) before squaring is all the code needs, and (9) then falls out of the same three lines. With all \(h_j\) equal it is the isotropic estimator exactly — the demo asserts bit-for-bit agreement — so product_kernel is not a different algorithm, it is the same algorithm with a scale per axis. What it cannot do is follow a correlation: a ridge at 45° in two dimensions needs an off-diagonal term, and the diagonal family can only widen both axes until the ridge is a blob. Whiten the data first, or use a full-bandwidth estimator.

    Code walkthrough #

    1. The kernels and their constants #

        if name == "gaussian":
        return np.exp(-0.5 * u2) / (2.0 * math.pi) ** (n_features / 2.0)
    volume = ball_volume(n_features)
    if name == "tophat":
        return np.where(u2 <= 1.0, 1.0 / volume, 0.0)
    if name == "epanechnikov":
        return np.where(u2 <= 1.0, (n_features + 2.0) / (2.0 * volume) * (1.0 - u2), 0.0)

    The function takes a squared radius because that is what the distance code produces, and it saves a square root per pair. ball_volume is \(\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2+1)\), so the tophat's constant is \(1/V_d\) and the Epanechnikov's is \((d+2)/(2V_d)\) — the normalisation integral above, read backwards. np.where evaluates both branches and then selects: for the compact kernels the discarded branch is finite, so this is safe, and it lets the caller pass a whole distance matrix instead of looping. A Python branch would be marginally faster and would force that loop, which is the design constraint that matters.

    2. Distances in bandwidth units #

        offset = (A[:, None, :] - B[None, :, :]) / bandwidth
    return np.einsum("ijk,ijk->ij", offset, offset)

    Three lines, and the order of operations is the point: divide by the bandwidth, then square. Squaring first and dividing by \(h^2\) is the same number for a scalar bandwidth and a different algorithm for a vector one — the product kernel in (9) is exactly the decision to divide per dimension. The broadcast produces an \((m, n, d)\) difference array, and einsum contracts the last axis without materialising a second array; (offset ** 2).sum(axis=2) is the readable version and allocates one more \(m n d\) block.

    3. The rules of thumb #

        return X.std(axis=0, ddof=1) * n ** (-1.0 / (d + 4.0))

        q75, q25 = np.percentile(X, [75.0, 25.0], axis=0)
    robust = np.minimum(X.std(axis=0, ddof=1), (q75 - q25) / 1.349)
    return 0.9 * robust * n ** (-1.0 / (d + 4.0))

    ddof=1 because this is a sample standard deviation; the difference between \(n\) and \(n-1\) is not the point, but the convention should match every other statistic in the chapter. The exponent is written with float literals so the expression does not depend on the caller's integer type. The np.minimum in the robust version is the entire content of Silverman's rule: on the lognormal sample the two candidate scales are 2.816 and 1.347, so the rule takes 1.347 and the bandwidth drops from 0.976 to 0.420.

    4. Choosing, with a precedence order #

            if self.bandwidth is not None:
            given = np.asarray(self.bandwidth, dtype=float)
            if given.ndim == 0:
                return np.full(d, float(given))
            if given.shape != (d,):
                raise ValueError(f"bandwidth must be a scalar or shape ({d},)")
            return given.copy()
        if self.variant == "silverman":
            return silverman_factor(X)

    An explicit bandwidth wins over the variant, always. That is what makes the variant list usable as a set of defaults rather than a set of algorithms: the demo fits gaussian, epanechnikov and tophat at the same \(h\) to isolate the kernel, and fits tree_kde at a fixed 0.15 because Scott's rule reads the spread of all three blobs and returns a bandwidth wider than a blob. The copy() is not paranoia — the returned array is stored and later used to divide, so an aliased caller array would be a silent mutation bug.

            if self.variant == "tree_kde":
            # One bandwidth for every dimension is what the node bound in
            # `_tree_sum` assumes; the median is the least surprising summary.
            return np.full(d, float(np.median(base)))

    The tree's bound is only valid for an isotropic kernel, so the variant reduces Scott's vector to its median rather than pretending a per-axis bandwidth is fine. Passing a genuine vector raises instead, which the failure-mode demo checks: the alternative is a tree that is fast and quietly wrong.

    5. The leave-one-out likelihood #

            sq = _sq_distances(X, X, np.full(X.shape[1], h))
        kernel = kernel_value(sq, X.shape[1], "gaussian") / h ** X.shape[1]
        held_out = kernel.sum(axis=1) - np.diag(kernel)
        return float(np.mean(np.log(np.maximum(held_out / (len(X) - 1), 1e-300))))

    Equation (7) as one matrix expression. kernel.sum(axis=1) includes \(x_i\)'s own bump, and subtracting the diagonal removes the only term that must not be there — cheaper than building the matrix with a hole in it, and exact, since the diagonal entries are exactly \(K(0)/h^d\) and not a floating-point approximation of it. The np.maximum guards the log: a point far from every other point has a held-out density of zero, and log 0 would make the objective \(-\infty\) and break the golden section. Dividing by \(n-1\) rather than \(n\) keeps the number comparable to a log density.

            return math.exp(_golden_max(
            lambda log_h: self._loo_loglik(X, math.exp(log_h)),
            math.log(h0 / 4.0), math.log(h0 * 4.0)))

    The search is in \(\log h\) because the objective is asymptotically a function of \(\log h\) and its curvature is far more uniform there, which is what lets sixty steps of golden section be enough. The bracket is a factor of four either side of the rule of thumb, which is a prior: if the optimum lay outside it the search would return the boundary and the model would silently use a bandwidth nobody chose. That is a real risk with multimodal data and the reason the returned bandwidth should be inspected rather than trusted.

    6. Scoring #

            scale = float(np.prod(self.bandwidth_))
        if self.variant == "tree_kde":
            self.evaluations_ = 0
            sums = np.array([self._tree_sum(row) for row in X])
        else:
            sq = _sq_distances(X, self.data_, self.bandwidth_)
            kernel = kernel_value(sq, self.n_features_, self.kernel_)
            sums = kernel.sum(axis=1)
        density = sums / (len(self.data_) * scale)

    The normaliser is \(\prod_j h_j\), not \(h^d\): with a per-dimension bandwidth the Jacobian of the scaling is the product, and using a mean instead would silently produce something that does not integrate to one. The tree branch and the dense branch compute the same quantity — that is the assertion in the demo — and differ only in how many kernel values they evaluate. The 1e-300 floor is the only place the code gives up on exactness: it turns a genuine zero density into \(-690.8\), which is finite and therefore comparable, and it is also why a far-away query reports the same number however far away it is.

    7. The pruning test #

                gap = max(float(np.linalg.norm(row - node.centre)) - node.radius, 0.0) / h
            # Every kernel above is a decreasing function of radius, so the
            # nearest point in the node is the best case for the node's whole
            # contribution -- and if even that is negligible, so is the node.
            bound = node.count * kernel_value(gap * gap, d, self.kernel_) / h ** d
            # Exact when the bound underflows, relative once the running sum is
            # non-zero. Both are needed: in the far field every node evaluates to
            # zero and a relative test alone would never fire.
            if bound == 0.0 or (total > 0.0 and bound < self.tol * total):
                continue

    Equation (8), and then the two clauses that make it work. max(..., 0.0) is the case where the query is inside the node: the closest point could be the query itself, so the gap is zero and the bound is the kernel's maximum. The bound == 0.0 clause looks like a micro-optimisation and is not: for a query twenty units from a cloud with \(h = 0.15\), every radius is beyond \(100h\), the Gaussian underflows, and the bound is exactly zero. Without that clause the condition is 0.0 < 0.0, which is false, so nothing is pruned and the tree evaluates all 45 000 kernels — the demo measured that before the clause existed.

    8. Growing the tree #

        spread = np.ptp(points, axis=0)
    axis = int(np.argmax(spread))
    if len(indices) <= leaf_size or spread[axis] == 0.0:
        return node
    order = indices[np.argsort(points[:, axis], kind="stable")]
    middle = len(order) // 2
    node.indices = None  # interior nodes are summarised by centre and radius

    A median split on the widest axis: \(O(n \log n)\) to build, balanced by construction, and — the part that matters for (8) — each node's centre and radius are the tightest ball around its own points, computed once. stable sorting makes the build deterministic for tied coordinates, which is what lets the demo be reproducible. Setting indices = None on interior nodes is a memory decision with a diagnostic value: a leaf is the only kind of node that can be evaluated, so a bug that tries to read indices off an interior node fails loudly instead of summing a subset.

    Complexity #

    For \(n\) points in \(d\) dimensions, \(m\) queries, and a cross-validation run of \(T\) objective evaluations:

    stage time memory
    Scott or Silverman bandwidth \(O(nd)\) \(O(d)\)
    cross-validated bandwidth \(O(T n^2 d)\) \(O(n^2)\)
    tree build \(O(n \log n)\) \(O(n \log n)\)
    score, dense \(O(nmd)\) \(O(nmd)\)
    score, tree between \(O(m \log n)\) and \(O(nmd)\) \(O(n \log n)\)

    The dense path allocates the whole \(m \times n \times d\) difference block, which is why the demo is sized in the thousands and why every production KDE loops over chunks of queries. The tree's \(\log n\) term is the traversal; the range is the honest one, because how much of the tree is visited is a property of the query's position, not of \(n\) — the demo measured 37%, 65% and 100% of the kernel evaluations skipped for queries in a blob, between blobs, and far away. A dual-tree implementation, which prunes on node pairs instead of point-to-node, is what turns this into \(O(n)\) for a whole data set and is the version in the published libraries.

    Failure modes #

    Likelihood cross-validation undersmooths #

    This is the one to know, because it looks like a success. On the bimodal sample the leave-one-out likelihood peaks at \(h = 0.119\) and every step of the optimisation is correct: the objective really is higher there. The integrated squared error is 2.4 times the rule-of-thumb value. The mechanism is the \(-\log h\) in each term: narrow kernels are rewarded for being narrow, and the reward is only cancelled once the nearest neighbour falls outside the kernel, which on this sample is three orders of magnitude below the chosen \(h\). If a selector based on (7) returns a bandwidth much smaller than a rule of thumb, that is not a discovery about the data; it is the objective doing what it is defined to do. Use least-squares cross-validation, or compare the two estimates by eye.

    Boundaries #

    A kernel centred near the edge of the support puts half its mass where there is no data, so the estimate is biased down by roughly the missing mass. Five hundred uniform points on \([0, 1]\), \(h = 0.1\): the estimate is \(0.542\) at \(x = 0\) and \(1.039\) at \(x = 0.5\), against a truth of \(1.000\). That is a 46% deficit at exactly the place where an anomaly detector is most likely to be asked a question — "is this value unusually small" — and no bandwidth fixes it: widening \(h\) spreads the deficit and shrinking it sharpens it. The fixes are structural: reflect the data about the boundary, fit on a transformed scale, or use a local likelihood estimator that knows where the support ends.

    Empty space in high dimensions #

    \(h^\star \propto n^{-1/(d+4)}\) is not a curiosity; it is the reason the next ten atoms exist. At \(d = 10\) the bandwidth shrinks as \(n^{-1/14}\), so the number of points inside a kernel of width \(h\) around a query grows like \(n h^d = n^{4/(d+4)}\) — that is \(n^{0.29}\) — and the estimate's relative error falls at that rate instead of the \(n^{-1/2}\) a parametric model gets. With \(n = 10^4\) that is a relative error around 7%; with \(n = 10^6\), 2%. The diagnostic is not the error, it is the count: if the 20 nearest neighbours are all further than \(h\), the local density estimate is an interpolation between points that are nowhere near, and the number it returns is arithmetic rather than evidence.

    A zero density and the floor #

    score_samples returns \(\log \max(\hat p, 10^{-300})\), so a query with no neighbours within reach reports \(-690.8\) — and so does every other such query, however far away it is. Two points a hundred units and a million units from the data are indistinguishable in the output, and so are "extremely unlikely" and "impossible". That is fine for ranking and for thresholding at a quantile; it is not fine for the likelihood of a model comparison or for anything that averages log densities, where the floor contributes a constant that has nothing to do with the data. The tree demo shows the mechanism from the other side: the 75 far queries evaluate no kernels at all and return the floor exactly.

    Collinearity and duplicated columns #

    Nothing checks that the columns are independent, and a duplicated column is silently a different estimator: it turns the density of \(x_1\) into the density of the diagonal \(x_1 = x_2\), and the same point scores 0.259 nats differently with no information added. A product kernel on correlated data has the same problem in a milder form — the diagonal bandwidth family cannot follow a ridge, so it widens both axes until the ridge is isotropic. Whiten first, then fit.

    Applications #

    • Anomaly detection. Score \(-\log \hat{p}(x_i)\) and threshold at a quantile of the training scores; this is the baseline every detector in this chapter is compared against, and on low-dimensional data it is hard to beat. The threshold is where KDE and the rest of the chapter meet: the estimator gives a number, the quantile gives a decision, and the false-positive rate is the quantile by construction.
    • Class-conditional densities. A generative classifier fits one KDE per class and applies Bayes' rule; it handles multimodal classes that a Gaussian naive Bayes cannot, at the cost of \(n_c\) points per class rather than \(d^2\) parameters.
    • Mode seeking. The gradient of (1) points uphill, and following it is mean shift — which is why bandwidth selection and the number of clusters in mean shift are the same question.
    • Mutual information and entropy. The Kozachenko–Leonenko estimator is a KDE in disguise: \(-\log \hat p(x_i)\) averaged over the sample estimates the differential entropy, and the nearest-neighbour form drops the kernel entirely. Every "estimate MI by binning" pipeline is a histogram version of the same identity.
    • Smoothing as an operation. Convolution with \(K_h\) is a low-pass filter, so the same code smooths a spatial field, a histogram or a spectral estimate. The bandwidth is a scale, and choosing it is a choice about which scales are signal.
    • Where it is a trap. Above roughly five dimensions the estimator's convergence rate collapses, the boundary bias is everywhere, and the sample size needed is not available. That is the regime of the scorers: LOF compares local densities instead of estimating them, isolation forest measures isolation instead of density, and COPOD uses a parametric copula. They are not better estimators; they are better questions.
