    09 · Density & anomaly/tier 2/3 variants/source

    HBOS — one histogram per feature

    Intuition #

    Fit a histogram to each feature separately. A point's score is how surprising each of its coordinates is under its own marginal, added up:

      feature 1                    feature 2                  score
 ┌────────────────┐           ┌────────────────┐
 │   ▁▃▇█▇▃▁      │           │  ▁▂▅█▅▂▁       │
 └────────────────┘           └────────────────┘
     ↓ x₁ = 0.4                   ↓ x₂ = −1.7
  −log p̂₁ = 0.9                −log p̂₂ = 3.1      →  4.0

    Two assumptions do all the work. The first is that the joint density is the product of the marginals, so the negative log density is their sum — that is what makes the score cheap. The second is that a histogram is an adequate summary of a marginal. Both are false in general, and the first is false in an interesting way, which is why the method is worth a chapter slot: it is the fastest multivariate detector that is not a distance, it is linear in both \(n\) and \(d\), and its failures are exactly the failures of the product model.

    The demo plants the failure. Two features correlated at 0.971, and forty points on the anti-diagonal at radius 0.8 to 1.5 — every coordinate inside the data's range and near its mode, but jointly impossible. Each variant scores them below the ordinary points:

    variant bins AUC planted normals flagged
    static_bins 20 0.365 2.54 2.93 0 of 40
    dynamic_bins 32 0.474 2.41 2.90 0 of 40
    adaptive 45 0.420 2.31 2.89 0 of 40

    The score of \((1.2, 1.2)\) and \((1.2, -1.2)\) differs by 1.13 nats — the sampling noise in two bin counts — while their true bivariate log-density differs by 46.9. A product model has no representation of "ordinary in each coordinate, impossible together", so it cannot rank such a point at all. Everything HBOS is good at comes from the same place: it notices when one coordinate is in a region no training point occupied, and it notices it cheaply.

    The three variants are three answers to the only real question, which is how many bins:

    • static_bins uses a fixed count for every feature, ten by default, which is the original paper's choice. It is indifferent to \(n\) and to the shape of the data: ten bins over a million rows throw away almost all the resolution, and ten bins over a peaky feature put the entire mode in one bin.
    • dynamic_bins sets a count per feature from the Freedman–Diaconis width, so the count scales like \(n^{1/3}\) and is measured in units of the feature's own robust spread. A flat feature gets few bins, a narrow one gets many — sometimes so many that max_bins is what actually decides.
    • adaptive chooses the count by maximising a leave-one-out log-likelihood and places the edges at quantiles rather than at equal widths, so resolution follows the density instead of the range.

    Neither data-driven rule is uniformly better, and the demo measures that too. On a sample that is 85% a narrow Gaussian and 15% a wide one, ten equal-width bins cost 3.02 nats per held-out point and quantile bins 0.94; on a plain Gaussian the same comparison is 2.89 against 2.96. A fixed count is a fixed resolution, and resolution is adequate only when the data's scale agrees with it.

    Variants #

    variant bin count edges fit cost measured
    static_bins n_bins, 10 equal width \(O(nd)\) 3.02 nats on peaky data
    dynamic_bins Freedman–Diaconis, capped equal width \(O(nd)\) 1.01 nats on peaky data
    adaptive leave-one-out maximiser quantiles \(O(Knd)\) 0.94 nats on peaky data

    The measured column is held-out mean negative log density on a 2-feature sample of 600 training points scored on 3000 fresh ones — lower is a better density model. On that sample static_bins chooses 20 bins in total, dynamic_bins 128 (both features hit the cap of 64) and adaptive 84. On a plain Gaussian the same three give 2.89, 2.88 and 2.96 over 20, 38 and 53 bins: the ordering reverses, adaptive spending its extra 33 bins on variance to model a shape 20 bins already cover.

    static_bins is the right choice when the data are smooth, roughly unimodal and already on a comparable scale, and when you want a number that does not depend on \(n\) — a detector fitted on a 200-row calibration window and then applied to live traffic should not change its notion of resolution because the window grew. dynamic_bins is the sane default: it adapts to \(n\) and to each feature's robust spread at no measurable cost, and it degrades gracefully. adaptive is for data whose density varies by orders of magnitude across its range — a mixture, a lognormal, a count — where equal-width edges are structurally wrong.

    On the lognormal feature in the demo the three rules choose 10, 45 and 22 bins, and the fixed count puts 68% of the sample in its largest bin against 5% for quantile bins. The leave-one-out criterion behind adaptive is \(-1.4572\) at \(k = 16\), \(-1.4571\) at \(k = 24\) and \(-1.4638\) at \(k = 48\): the optimum is flat, which is why the variant can land anywhere inside it and still be right.

    Derivation #

    1. From the product model to the score #

    Under independence the joint density factorises, so

    \[-\log p(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{d} -\log p_j(x_j)\]

    and replacing each marginal by its histogram estimate gives the score. The estimator itself is the usual one, with two floors that matter:

    \[\hat p_j(v) = \frac{\max(N_b, 1)}{n\, w_b}, \quad v \in \text{bin } b \qquad (1)\]
    \[\mathrm{HBOS}(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{d} -\log \hat p_j(x_j) \qquad (2)\]

    The \(\max(N_b, 1)\) turns an empty bin from an infinite score into a finite one. It is a modelling decision rather than a numerical guard: a region with no training points is not infinitely improbable, only unobserved, and one pseudo-count says so. The demo shows the consequence in the degenerate case — a constant column gets one bin holding everything, so its density is exactly 1 and its contribution exactly 0, which is the right answer for a feature that cannot distinguish anything.

    Values outside the fitted range have no bin at all. They get half a point in the adjacent edge bin,

    \[\hat p_j(v) = \frac{1}{2\,n\,w_{\text{edge}}}, \qquad v \notin [\min x_j, \max x_j] \qquad (3)\]

    which is "less plausible than anything observed, still finite". The number is arbitrary and the arbitrariness is visible: with n_bins=1 every training point scores 3.76 and a point outside scores 16.91, exactly \(3\log 80 = 13.15\) more — the same whether it is one unit or a thousand beyond the edge. That is what a histogram knows about the tail: nothing.

    2. Why the chi-square law fails #

    The paper's calibration argument is that under the null each \(-\log \hat p_j(x_j)\) is exponential with rate one, so twice the score is a sum of \(d\) chi-squares with two degrees of freedom each:

    \[2\,\mathrm{HBOS}(x) \sim \chi^2_{2d} \qquad (4)\]

    This is not a theorem, and the reason is dimensional. The mean of \(-\log \hat p_j\) is the differential entropy of the marginal plus a discretisation term:

    \[\mathbb{E}\left[-\log \hat p_j(X_j)\right] = H_{\text{bin}} + \log w = h(p_j) + O(w^2) \qquad (5)\]

    with \(H_{\text{bin}} = -\sum_b p_b \log p_b\) the entropy of the binned variable. For a standard normal \(h = \tfrac12 \log(2\pi e) = 1.4189\), and ten bins over the sample range measure 1.443 — the \(O(w^2)\) term from \(\hat p\) being a step function. So each term has mean 1.443, not the 1 that a unit-rate exponential has, and the score is centred \(0.443\,d\) above where (4) puts it. The threshold allows only \(1.645\sqrt{d}\) of tail room above a mean of \(d\), so that offset eats a growing share of the budget as \(d\) rises — and the variance is not chi-square's either, since each term is a handful of atoms rather than an exponential. The result is measured: at a nominal 5%, static_bins flags 5.9% of held-out points in two dimensions, 10.5% in four and 20.7% in eight.

    There is a stronger objection, and the demo makes it in one line of arithmetic. Density has units, so negative log density does too:

    \[\hat p_j(v/s) = s\,\hat p_j(v) \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathrm{HBOS}(x \text{ after } x_j \mapsto x_j/s) = \mathrm{HBOS}(x) + \log s \qquad (6)\]

    Multiply one feature by 1000 and every score rises by exactly \(\log 1000 = 6.91\) nats, measured to \(10^{-6}\), while the threshold in (4) is a constant. The demo's detector then flags 100% of the same data. No threshold that does not move with the score can be calibrated for all data, and a constant cannot move. This is the real content of (4): it is a rule of thumb that happens to land near the right answer when the marginals have entropy near one in the units you happen to use.

    The fix costs twenty lines and is exact where (4) is approximate. Under the fitted model a point lands in bin \(b\) of feature \(j\) with probability \(N_b/n\) and contributes a known quantity there, so the null distribution of (2) can be sampled without any data:

            value = -np.log(np.maximum(model.counts_[j], 1) / (model.n_ * widths))
        mass = model.counts_[j] / model.n_
        total += value[rng.choice(len(mass), size=reps, p=mass)]

    That threshold is calibrated by construction — 4.9%, 7.1% and 9.3% at \(d\) = 2, 4, 8 instead of 5.9%, 10.5% and 20.7% — and it shifts with the units because the score it is derived from does. The residue is real and does not vanish with \(n\): at \(d=8\) it falls from 7.1% at \(n = 500\) to 5.3% at \(n = 2000\) and then sits at 5.5% at \(n = 8000\), because what is left is the gap between a ten-bin histogram and the Gaussian it is modelling, not estimation error. The chi-square rate is 19.4% at every \(n\): it is not measuring the model at all.

    3. The bin counts #

    dynamic_bins is the Freedman–Diaconis rule, which is the same rule the histogram atom uses. The width that minimises integrated squared error for a smooth density is of order \(\mathrm{IQR}\, n^{-1/3}\), so

    \[k_j = \left\lceil \frac{\max x_j - \min x_j}{2\,\mathrm{IQR}_j\, n^{-1/3}} \right\rceil \qquad (7)\]

    clipped to \([1, \texttt{max\_bins}]\). The rule is invariant to the units of the feature, which is the point: it expresses a width in units of the data's own robust spread. It is also blind to shape beyond the IQR, which is why a peaky sample gets a count that hits the cap and a flat one gets a handful.

    adaptive maximises a leave-one-out likelihood. For a histogram this is free of resampling, because removing point \(i\) from its own bin changes that bin from \(N\) to \(N-1\) and nothing else:

    \[\hat p_j^{(-i)}(x_i) = \frac{N_{b(i)} - 1}{(n-1)\, w_{b(i)}} \qquad (8)\]

    so the criterion is

    \[k_j = \arg\max_k \; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \hat p_j^{(-i)}(x_i) \qquad (9)\]

    evaluated over every \(k\) from 2 to \(\min(\texttt{max\_bins}, \lfloor n/2 \rfloor)\) (floored at three), with the edges placed at quantiles so each bin carries the same mass. Two things follow. The count is chosen by the data rather than by a formula, and the placement follows the density rather than the range — on a lognormal, quantile edges put 5% of the sample in the largest bin against 68% for equal widths.

    The criterion has a subtlety that is easy to miss. (8) is exact, but the edges were chosen using every point including \(i\), so the criterion is not a held-out estimate of the model's performance; it removes the count's dependence on the point and not the edges'. That is why the optimum is flat and why adaptive can lose to a fixed count on data where a fixed count is already right.

    Code walkthrough #

    1. Scoring one feature #

                index = np.clip(np.searchsorted(edges, X[:, j], side="right") - 1,
                            0, k - 1)
            # Equation (1): never a zero density, so one point in a bin is
            # enough to make it plausible and an empty bin is not a wall.
            density = np.maximum(counts[index], 1) / (self.n_ * widths[index])
            outside = (X[:, j] < edges[0]) | (X[:, j] > edges[-1])
            # A value beyond the fitted range is worth half a point in the edge
            # bin: less plausible than anything observed, still finite.
            edge = np.where(X[:, j] < edges[0], 0, k - 1)
            density = np.where(outside, 0.5 / (self.n_ * widths[edge]), density)
            out[:, j] = -np.log(np.maximum(density, FLOOR))

    The whole method is these seven lines, repeated per feature. searchsorted with side="right" reproduces np.histogram's convention that the last bin includes its right edge, which is why the counts stored at fit time and the indices computed at score time agree; getting the side wrong silently sends every value that equals an internal edge into the bin above and skews the score. The clip is needed because a value exactly equal to the largest edge sorts past the last bin. FLOOR is a guard rather than part of the method: no reachable density is zero, since (1) floors the count, so it cannot fire unless a bin width has collapsed — and one np.maximum is cheaper than an argument about whether that is impossible after a future edit.

    2. The leave-one-out criterion #

        counts, _ = np.histogram(x, edges)
    widths = np.diff(edges)
    index = np.clip(np.searchsorted(edges, x, side="right") - 1, 0, k - 1)
    held = counts[index] - 1
    if np.any(held <= 0):
        return -np.inf
    density = held / ((n - 1.0) * widths[index])
    return float(np.mean(np.log(density)))

    Equation (8), one line per term. The held <= 0 guard is doing real work: a bin with a single point gives its occupant zero held-out density and a log of \(-\infty\), so any partition that isolates a point is rejected outright. That is what stops the criterion from running to \(k = n/2\) and declaring a perfect fit, and it is why the optimum in the demo is an interior \(k\) rather than the largest allowed one. Note that the denominator is \(n-1\), not \(n\): every point is scored against a histogram built from the other \(n-1\).

    3. Choosing the count #

            best_score, best = -np.inf, _frequency_edges(column, 2)
        for k in range(2, min(self.max_bins, max(3, len(column) // 2)) + 1):
            edges = _frequency_edges(column, k)
            score = _loo_score(column, edges)
            if score > best_score:
                best_score, best = score, edges
        return best

    A linear scan over every allowed count, which is affordable because each evaluation is one np.histogram and one vectorised logarithm. The initial best matters when every count scores \(-\infty\) — with three distinct values no \(k\) can satisfy the guard — in which case the two-bin quantile split is returned rather than an unbound variable. The upper limit \(n/2\) is a sanity bound: beyond it bins are guaranteed to be nearly empty. Strict improvement (>) rather than >= makes the choice deterministic at the flat part of the optimum, and it picks the smallest \(k\) among ties, which is the conservative direction.

    4. The threshold #

        def _threshold(self) -> float:
        if self.contamination is not None:
            return float(np.quantile(self.scores_, 1.0 - self.contamination))
        dof = 2 * self.n_features_
        return float(_chi2_ppf(1.0 - self.alpha, dof) / 2.0)

    Two regimes. contamination is a quantile of the training scores and therefore flags at most the requested share of any sample, clean or not — 4.8% here, where ties at the quantile resolve outward. contamination=None asks for the distribution-free threshold of (4), which the demo shows is neither distribution-free nor a threshold. Both are supplied because the honest empirical option makes no claim that can be wrong, and the analytic option is what the literature uses.

    Complexity #

    For \(n\) points, \(d\) features, \(m\) queries, \(k\) bins and \(K\) = max_bins:

    stage time memory
    static_bins / dynamic_bins fit \(O(nd)\) \(O(dk)\)
    adaptive fit \(O(Knd)\) \(O(dK)\)
    score \(m\) points \(O(md\log k)\) \(O(m)\)

    Fitting is a pass over the data per feature, plus one sort for quantile edges. The adaptive factor of \(K\) is the whole extra cost, and it is why the default max_bins is 64 rather than \(n/2\): the criterion is flat near its optimum, so searching further buys nothing and costs linearly. Scoring is a binary search per feature per point, so it is logarithmic in the bin count and linear in the dimension: the same order as a Euclidean distance, and a factor of \(n\) cheaper than a kernel density estimate, which is the entire reason the method exists. Returning the per-feature terms rather than the sum costs no extra time and \(O(md)\) instead of \(O(m)\) memory — usually the better trade, since the breakdown is the only explanation a product model can offer.

    The memory figure for adaptive counts the stored edges and counts; the search itself reuses one histogram at a time.

    Failure modes #

    Correlation blindness #

    The demo's headline. Two features correlated at 0.971, anomalies on the anti-diagonal at radius 0.8 to 1.5, and the AUC is 0.365 to 0.474 — the planted points score lower than the ordinary ones and none of the forty is flagged at a 10% budget. This is not a tuning problem, it is the product model working exactly as specified: the score never sees the pair. Any dataset with real dependence between features — which is most of them — will contain anomalies of this shape, and they are precisely the anomalies that a naive detector misses. If the dependence matters, transform first (PCA or a whitening step), or use a joint method.

    The threshold is not the one in the paper #

    contamination=None uses (4), and the demo shows what that is worth: 5.9% at \(d=2\), 20.7% at \(d=8\) on clean standard normal data at a nominal 5%, and 100% after rescaling one feature by 1000. The score's mean is the marginal's differential entropy, not 1, and the score has units. Both defects are structural and neither shrinks with \(n\). Use contamination, or resample the null from the fitted model.

    Resolution versus shape #

    A fixed count is a fixed width, and a width that suits a Gaussian is wrong for a mixture. On the demo's peaky sample static_bins costs 3.02 nats per held-out point against 0.94 for quantile bins, a factor of eight in likelihood. The symptom is a score that is nearly constant over a large region — the mode is one bin, so every point near it is tied — followed by a cliff between adjacent bins. Ties are also the mechanism by which a fine histogram overfits: with \(n\) points and \(k\) bins, \(k \gtrsim n/10\) leaves about ten counts per bin on average and one or two in the tail bins, so the density estimate there is a single observation.

    Contamination stretches the range #

    Fitting on data that contains a few far outliers widens every edge, so every density falls and the resolution available to the bulk collapses: the good data is compressed into a handful of bins while empty space gets bins of its own. HBOS has no notion of robustness — the fit is a set of counts, and a count is an average. The practical remedy is to fit on a window known to be clean, or to clip the feature at robust quantiles before fitting, and to remember that the score is therefore conditional on that window.

    Saturation outside the range #

    Every point beyond the fitted range gets the same score, (3), whatever its distance. That is one of the reasons HBOS is a triage tool rather than a measurement: it tells you a coordinate went somewhere unseen, not how far. It also means a drifted feature — one whose whole distribution has moved — produces a constant, undifferentiated alarm rather than a graded one.

    Degenerate and discrete features #

    A constant column contributes exactly zero, which is correct and also means it silently disappears from the score. A feature with few distinct values gets few quantile bins, because duplicate edges are removed — four distinct values repeated ten times each occupy four bins — and adaptive narrowing itself is the right response rather than a division by a zero-width bin. But it does mean the effective dimension can be smaller than X.shape[1], and a score threshold calibrated on n_features_ may be wrong about the number of terms that actually vary.

    Applications #

    • Network intrusion detection. HBOS was proposed for exactly this: many features, a need for speed, and a stream where the model must be refitted often. Per-feature histograms are cheap to update and the score is additive, so a stale fit degrades one coordinate at a time.
    • Credit and fraud triage. Each feature's contribution is interpretable — "this transaction is unusual in amount and ordinary in everything else" — which matters more in a regulated setting than a slightly better AUC.
    • Manufacturing and sensor monitoring. Independent channels, each with its own scale, is the setting the method was designed for; dynamic_bins handles the differing spreads without any per-channel tuning.
    • A first pass over high-volume logs. Aggregate counts, latencies and payload sizes per entity, score every entity, and send the top fraction to something expensive. Linear cost is the whole point.
    • Feature attribution inside a bigger detector. feature_scores returns the per-feature terms, so HBOS is often the explainable layer wrapped around, or underneath, a joint model — the terms say which coordinate moved.
    • Benchmark baseline. Goldstein and Uchida score it against much slower multivariate detectors, and it is the reference anything more elaborate has to beat on independent features — which is the profile this atom derives.
    • Where it is a trap. Correlated or redundant features, strong interaction effects, anomalies defined by combination rather than by magnitude, and any threshold taken from (4) without checking the units.
