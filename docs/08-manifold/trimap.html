    08 · Manifolds & embeddings/tier 3/3 variants/source

    TriMap

    Intuition #

    t-SNE and UMAP ask the embedding to reproduce similarities. TriMap asks it to reproduce an ordering. The difference sounds small and is not, because a similarity carries a magnitude and an ordering does not:

          x:  0 ──────── 1 ──────────────────── 4

      triplet (i, j, k) = (0, 1, 4)      "0 is closer to 1 than to 4"

      ideal  y = (0, 1, 4)   ω = 6   ℓ = 0.632
      wrong  y = (0, 4, 1)   ω = 6   ℓ = 5.368

    Both embeddings place the three points at exactly the same distances. Only the labelling differs, and only one of them respects the claim in the data. A method that matched distance matrices would have to decide how far apart to put these three points; a method that matches orderings does not, which is exactly the freedom that keeps the shape of a manifold when it cannot keep its scale.

    The second half of the idea is where the triplets come from, and it is where TriMap differs from the triplet-embedding literature. In t-STE the triplets are given by people ("I think A is more similar to B than to C"). Here they are sampled from the data, locally and globally:

    graph LR
    A["X ∈ ℝⁿˣᵈ"] --> B["m nearest<br/>neighbours"]
    B --> C["m·m′ local triplets<br/>per point"]
    A --> D["r uniform pairs<br/>per point"]
    D --> E["r global triplets<br/>per point"]
    C --> F["ω from d²_ik − d²_ij<br/>shifted, tempered log"]
    E --> F
    F --> G["Adam on PCA init<br/>Y ∈ ℝⁿˣ²"]

    Every point i contributes m·m′ triplets whose near point j is one of its m nearest neighbours and whose far point k is drawn uniformly from everything farther from i than j is; plus r triplets where both j and k are drawn uniformly from the whole data set and then ordered by distance. The default budget is 51 triplets per point — twelve neighbours, four draws each, three random pairs — so 15 300 for 300 points. The cost per iteration is linear in that count and therefore linear in n.

    What the two kinds buy, measured on the S-curve in the demo:

    method neighbourhood overlap @10 @40 distance corr. global score
    TriMap (51 triplets/point) 0.774 0.813 0.895 0.638
    t-SNE, perplexity 30 0.865 0.867 0.987 0.257
    UMAP, 10 neighbours 0.803 0.771 0.705 0.097
    PCA, two components 0.539 0.761 0.860 1.000

    300 points on a sheet folded twice in three dimensions. Local accuracy is nearest-neighbour overlap with the sheet's own parameters; global accuracy is the paper's score, \(1\) for PCA by construction. Both baselines are this chapter's own t-SNE and UMAP, so the comparison is like for like rather than against another library's defaults: t-SNE wins the neighbourhoods and comes last but one globally, UMAP gives up both, and TriMap keeps three quarters of the neighbourhoods and two thirds of the global structure. Written as a trade: \(+0.235\) overlap over PCA for \(-0.362\) global score, while t-SNE takes \(+0.326\) overlap for \(-0.743\).

    That is the whole method. The rest of this document is the details of the weights, the gradient, and the ways the trade can go wrong.

    Variants #

    variant near point j far point k triplets overlap @10 1-NN accuracy global score
    trimap one of m NN uniform, farther than j 15 300 0.774 91.7% 0.638
    supervised same class another class 14 400 — 100.0% 0.407
    knn one of m NN inside the 2-hop ring 14 400 0.553 67.0% 0.002

    The overlap and global-score columns are the S-curve; the accuracy column is five overlapping Gaussian clusters in eight dimensions, where the accuracy is one-nearest-neighbour voting in the embedding. Every number is printed by the demo.

    trimap is the paper's method: local triplets fix each neighbourhood, uniform pairs fix where the neighbourhoods sit relative to each other. supervised replaces the sampler, not the loss: j is drawn from the same label and k from a different one, at the same budget of \(m\cdot m'\) triplets per point. It does exactly what it is asked — perfect cluster separation — and pays for it in global score, because a triplet that says "same class" says nothing about where the class belongs. knn is the controlled ablation: both j and k are restricted to the two-hop neighbourhood \(\bigcup_{j \in \mathrm{NN}(i)} \mathrm{NN}(j)\), so every triplet is a ranking within one neighbourhood. It keeps the fewest neighbours of all and its global score is zero to three decimals — a crumpled embedding — and the demo proves the optimiser is not at fault by scoring the good embedding on the knn variant's own triplets (11 649 against the crumple's 7 806). The objective prefers the crumple. The global pairs are not a refinement of the local ones; they are the only thing carrying global information.

    A fourth knob deserves its own row in a table like this, because it is the one that trades the two columns against each other. Growing the neighbourhood:

    n_neighbors (m) triplets overlap @10 distance corr. global score
    6 8 100 0.780 0.797 0.614
    12 15 300 0.774 0.895 0.638
    24 29 700 0.712 0.963 0.478
    48 58 500 0.704 0.982 0.297

    Larger m makes every triplet more local in relative terms — the far point is compared against a slightly less near point — so the embedding optimises distance correlation and loses the global score. The default is not a sweet spot for every dataset; it is the paper's choice after exactly this experiment on MNIST.

    Derivation #

    1. Similarity and loss #

    Let \(d_{ij} = \lVert \mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{y}_j \rVert\) in the embedding and use the Student-t similarity that t-SNE also uses:

    \[s(\mathbf{y}_i, \mathbf{y}_j) = \frac{1}{1 + \lVert \mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{y}_j \rVert^2} \qquad (1)\]

    The loss for a triplet is the weighted probability that the order is wrong:

    \[\ell_{ijk} = \omega_{ijk}\,\frac{s(\mathbf{y}_i, \mathbf{y}_k)}{s(\mathbf{y}_i, \mathbf{y}_j) + s(\mathbf{y}_i, \mathbf{y}_k)} \qquad (2)\]

    Substituting \(a = s_{ij} = (1+d_{ij}^2)^{-1}\) and \(b = s_{ik} = (1+d_{ik}^2)^{-1}\), the fraction is \(b/(a+b)\), which is one minus a two-way softmax over the logits \(\log a\) and \(\log b\):

    \[\ell_{ijk} = \omega_{ijk}\Big(1 - \frac{a}{a+b}\Big), \qquad \frac{a}{a+b} = \frac{1 + d_{ik}^2}{2 + d_{ij}^2 + d_{ik}^2} \qquad (3)\]

    Equation (3) is worth reading twice, because it explains both what the method can and cannot do. At zero scale, \(d_{ij}^2 = d_{ik}^2 = 0\) and the probability is \(1/2\): a collapsed embedding is indifferent to every triplet. Along a ray \(\mathbf{y} \mapsto s\mathbf{y}\) with \(s \to \infty\) the probability tends to \(d_{ik}^2/(d_{ij}^2 + d_{ik}^2)\), so only the ratio of squared distances survives. In between, for a correctly ordered triplet (\(d_{ij} < d_{ik}\)) the loss is monotonically decreasing in \(s\): inflating the embedding always helps, and nothing in the objective pulls back. The failure-modes section measures the consequence.

    2. Weights #

    A triplet is not a yes/no fact. "0 is closer to 1 than to 4" is a weaker claim than "0 is closer to 1 than to 40", and the weight says so:

    \[\tilde{\omega}_{ijk} = d_{ik}^2 - d_{ij}^2 \ge 0, \qquad d_{ij}^2 = \frac{\lVert \mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j \rVert^2}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} \qquad (4)\]

    The scaling is Zelnik-Manor and Perona's self-tuning factor, with

    \[\sigma_i = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{r=4}^{6} d\big(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_{(r)}\big) \qquad (5)\]

    the mean distance to the 4th, 5th and 6th nearest neighbours — far enough out to ignore duplicates, near enough to describe the local density. Its effect is measured: on 250 points packed in a ball of radius 0.05 plus 50 points spread over radius 3, \(\sigma_i\) ranges over a factor of 372, and replacing it by the global mean drops the dense cluster's share of the total weight from 79% to 56% and the effective sample size from 11 731 of 15 300 to 2 720. Without the normalisation a handful of triplets in the sparse tail carry the gradient.

    Raw weights still span two orders of magnitude, so they are shifted and passed through a tempered logarithm:

    \[\omega_{ijk} = \log_t\!\big(1 + \tilde{\omega}_{ijk} - \omega_{\min}\big), \qquad \log_t(u) = \frac{u^{1-t} - 1}{1-t} \qquad (6)\]

    with \(t = 1/2\), where \(\log_{1/2}(u) = 2(\sqrt{u} - 1)\) is exactly concave in \(\tilde{\omega}\). Concavity is the point: a squared distance difference grows quadratically, its square root grows linearly, and the heaviest triplets stop dominating. Measured on the S-curve, the top-to-median weight ratio falls from 7.6 to 3.0, the effective sample size rises from 8 953 to 12 376 of 15 300, and the heaviest tenth of the constraints carries 20% of the weight instead of 29%. The plain logarithm (\(t = 1\)) is flatter still — 95% effective sample size — and the paper's \(t = 1/2\) sits deliberately between the raw weights and full flattening.

    3. The gradient #

    Write \(\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{y}_j\) and \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{y}_k\), so that \(a = (1+\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u})^{-1}\) and \(b = (1+\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v})^{-1}\). From \(a'(\mathbf{u}) = -2a^2\mathbf{u}\) and \(b'(\mathbf{v}) = -2b^2\mathbf{v}\),

    \[\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial a} = -\frac{\omega b}{(a+b)^2}, \qquad \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial b} = \frac{\omega a}{(a+b)^2} \qquad (7)\]

    and the chain rule gives three forces, one per role:

    \[\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mathbf{y}_i} = \frac{2\omega ab}{(a+b)^2}\,(a\,\mathbf{u} - b\,\mathbf{v}), \qquad \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mathbf{y}_j} = -\frac{2\omega a^2 b}{(a+b)^2}\,\mathbf{u}, \qquad \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mathbf{y}_k} = \frac{2\omega a b^2}{(a+b)^2}\,\mathbf{v} \qquad (8)\]

    The three sum to zero identically:

    \[\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mathbf{y}_i} + \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mathbf{y}_j} + \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mathbf{y}_k} = \mathbf{0} \qquad (9)\]

    because the loss depends only on the two difference vectors. Equation (9) is not a curiosity: it is translation invariance, and it means the gradient scatter has the same structure as a graph Laplacian — every triplet pushes and pulls three vertices with zero net force. The demo checks (8) against central differences to \(1.1 \times 10^{-9}\) and (9) to \(6 \times 10^{-17}\), and it checks the direction that matters: with \(\omega = 6\) the correctly ordered line \(0, 1, 4\) costs 0.6316 and the swapped one costs 5.3684. The same line inflated four-fold costs 0.3723, sixteen-fold 0.3542, monotonically toward the limit \(6/17 = 0.3529\) of equation (3) — the loss has nothing to say about scale, and the demo asserts the monotonicity rather than describing it.

    4. The sampling budget #

    Each of the m neighbours contributes m′ far points, and each point adds r uniform pairs, so

    \[|\mathcal{T}| = (m\,m' + r)\,n = (12\cdot4 + 3)\,n = 51\,n \qquad (10)\]

    The gradient is a sum of \(|\mathcal{T}|\) vector terms scattered onto \(n\) rows, which is \(O(|\mathcal{T}| d)\) per iteration — linear in \(n\) for fixed hyperparameters. Nothing in the optimisation is quadratic; only the neighbour search and, in this implementation, the distance matrix are.

    5. The global score #

    The paper needs a number for "kept the global structure", and defines it against PCA. With \(\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}\) centred and \(\mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) centred, the best linear reconstruction of the data from the embedding is

    \[\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{Y} \mid \mathbf{X}) = \min_{\mathbf{A}} \lVert \mathbf{X} - \mathbf{Y}\mathbf{A}^{\top} \rVert_F^2, \qquad \mathbf{A}^{*} = \mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}\,(\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{Y})^{-1} \qquad (11)\]

    which is ordinary least squares applied to every feature at once. PCA minimises the same quantity over all rank-\(d\) embeddings, so its residual \(\mathcal{E}_{\text{PCA}}\) is the floor, and the score is a ratio in log space:

    \[\mathrm{GS}(\mathbf{Y} \mid \mathbf{X}) = \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{Y} \mid \mathbf{X}) - \mathcal{E}_{\text{PCA}}}{\mathcal{E}_{\text{PCA}}}\Big) \in [0, 1] \qquad (12)\]

    PCA scores 1, an embedding twice as bad scores \(e^{-1} \approx 0.37\), and the score treats rotations and scalings of \(\mathbf{Y}\) as free because \(\mathbf{A}^{*}\) absorbs them. That is the right invariance for a global measure and a warning about its limits: it assumes a linear map can connect the two spaces, so it under-rewards embeddings that unfold a manifold PCA cannot.

    6. Optimisation #

    The paper initialises at scaled PCA and runs 400 full-batch steps with momentum and delta-bar-delta. This implementation keeps the initialisation —

    \[\mathbf{Y}_0 = \frac{\mathbf{U}_{:,:d}\,\mathbf{S}_{:d}}{\mathrm{rms}(\mathbf{Y})}, \qquad (13)\]

    the top principal components rescaled to unit root-mean-square — and swaps the optimiser for Adam, which needs no gain heuristic:

    \[\mathbf{Y} \leftarrow \mathbf{Y} - \eta\, \frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t} + \epsilon} \qquad (14)\]

    The initialisation is not cosmetic, but it is not required either: the paper reports that random initialisation also works, and contrasts this with t-SNE, which "does not converge well with any initial solution other than small random initialization around the origin". What PCA buys is speed and a head start on the global layout — at the PCA start 3.1% of the sampled triplets are violated, so the optimiser only has to fix those orderings rather than discover the layout from a ball of noise. After 400 steps 1.8% are still violated.

    Code walkthrough #

    1. Distances without a subtraction #

    def _pairwise(A: np.ndarray, B: np.ndarray | None = None) -> np.ndarray:
    B = A if B is None else B
    sa = np.einsum("ij,ij->i", A, A)
    sb = np.einsum("ij,ij->i", B, B)
    return np.sqrt(np.maximum(sa[:, None] + sb[None, :] - 2.0 * (A @ B.T), 0.0))

    The identity \(\lVert a-b \rVert^2 = \lVert a \rVert^2 + \lVert b \rVert^2 - 2a\cdot b\) turns the pairwise distances into one matrix product plus two vectors, which is what BLAS is good at. The np.maximum(..., 0.0) is not defensive padding: for near-duplicate rows the identity can return a tiny negative number, and a square root of that is nan that spreads through every downstream sum.

    2. The self-tuning scale #

        scale = D[np.arange(D.shape[0])[:, None], neighbours[:, 3:6]].mean(axis=1)
    return np.maximum(scale, 1e-10)

    neighbours[:, 3:6] is the 4th to 6th nearest neighbour — Python's zero-based slice of the paper's 4-to-6 — and the fancy index picks, for every row, that row's own three distances. One expression, no loop, and it is (5). The floor at \(10^{-10}\) is what stops a dataset with exact duplicates from producing a zero scale and a division by zero two lines later.

    3. Three samplers in one loop #

            for j in neighbours[i]:
            candidates = (ring[i] if variant == "knn"
                          else np.flatnonzero(D[i] > D[i, j]))
            if candidates.size == 0:
                continue
            k = candidates[rng.integers(candidates.size, size=n_triplets)]

    np.flatnonzero(D[i] > D[i, j]) is the literal definition of the far set: every point strictly farther from i than j is. Drawing n_triplets of them with replacement is the paper's m′. The knn variant swaps that candidate set for the two-hop ring, computed once per row outside the loop, and that single substitution is the entire difference between a working method and the crumpled one in the Variants table. Note that j is iterated in neighbour order, so the draw is not uniform over pairs — the near neighbours are always represented, which is deliberate: a uniform draw over the same set would give a very different weight distribution.

    4. The global pairs #

                for _ in range(n_random):
                j, k = rng.choice(n, size=2, replace=False)
                if D[i, k] < D[i, j]:
                    j, k = k, j

    Two indices without replacement, then one comparison to put the nearer one in the j slot. replace=False matters: with replacement the two indices could coincide, the triplet would be (i, j, j) and its weight exactly zero, wasting a slot. The swap is what makes the pair a triplet at all — the ordering comes from the distances, not from the sampling order.

    5. Shift and compress #

        weights = np.concatenate(w_parts)
    weights = weights - weights.min()
    weights = _tempered_log(1.0 + weights, 0.5)
    return triplets, weights

    Two lines implement (6), and their order is the whole specification. The shift makes the minimum weight exactly zero, which keeps every weight non-negative — a negative weight would mean "this ordering is wrong", and that is what the loss is for. The 1.0 + inside the logarithm is what makes the smallest weight zero rather than \(-\infty\). The tempered log then compresses: at \(t = 1/2\) it is \(2(\sqrt{u}-1)\), so a weight that was 16 becomes 6 and one that was 1 becomes 0.83 — the ratio 16:1 becomes 6:0.83.

    6. The tempered logarithm #

        if abs(t - 1.0) < 1e-6:
        return np.log(x)
    return (np.power(x, 1.0 - t) - 1.0) / (1.0 - t)

    The branch is the \(t \to 1\) limit, and it needs the epsilon because the expression is \(0/0\) there: L'Hôpital gives \(\log x\). Writing the limit as a branch instead of computing it symbolically is the standard numerical answer, and the tolerance is \(10^{-6}\) so that a caller passing \(t = 1 + 10^{-9}\) gets the stable form. For \(t = 1/2\) the np.power is a square root, which is why the weights land on the scale of a distance difference rather than its square.

    7. Loss and gradient in one pass #

        a = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.einsum("ij,ij->i", yi - yj, yi - yj))
    b = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.einsum("ij,ij->i", yi - yk, yi - yk))
    loss = float(np.sum(weights * b / (a + b)))

    yi - yj is an (T, d) array of difference vectors and einsum("ij,ij->i", ...) takes their row-wise squared norms, so \(a\) and \(b\) are (T,) — no loop over triplets anywhere. b / (a + b) is (2) and the sum is the objective of the paper. The gradients reuse the same quantities:

        denom = (a + b) ** 2
    coef = 2.0 * weights * a * b / denom
    g_i = coef[:, None] * (a[:, None] * (yi - yj) - b[:, None] * (yi - yk))
    g_j = -2.0 * (weights * a * a * b / denom)[:, None] * (yi - yj)
    g_k = 2.0 * (weights * a * b * b / denom)[:, None] * (yi - yk)

    These are (8) term by term, with coef the common factor \(2\omega ab/(a+b)^2\). Since all three are computed from the same (T,) arrays, one pass over the triplets produces the whole gradient; the cost is set by the memory for three (T, d) arrays, not by arithmetic.

    8. Scattering with duplicates #

        grad = np.zeros_like(Y)
    np.add.at(grad, i, g_i)
    np.add.at(grad, j, g_j)
    np.add.at(grad, k, g_k)
    return loss, grad / len(triplets)

    np.add.at is the unbuffered scatter-add, and it is required here rather than plain fancy indexing: one point appears in hundreds of triplets, and grad[i] += g_i with repeated indices would keep only the last write. It is also the slowest line in the whole method, because it runs in Python-level loops inside NumPy — which is the reason the reference implementation uses Numba. The division by the triplet count makes the gradient an average rather than a sum; Adam is invariant to that constant, so it only affects the size of the epsilon.

    9. PCA initialisation #

            centred = X - X.mean(axis=0)
        left, spectrum, _ = np.linalg.svd(centred, full_matrices=False)
        Y = left[:, :self.n_components] * spectrum[:self.n_components]
        Y = Y / np.sqrt(np.mean(np.einsum("ij,ij->i", Y, Y)))

    The first three lines are PCA written as an SVD of the centred data — no covariance matrix, no eigendecomposition, better conditioning — and the fourth is (13): divide by the root-mean-square radius so the initial embedding has unit scale regardless of the data's units. That normalisation is the only thing anchoring the scale at all, and the optimiser is free to undo it within a few steps.

    10. Adam, four lines #

                first = 0.9 * first + 0.1 * grad
            second = 0.999 * second + 0.001 * grad * grad
            m_hat = first / (1.0 - 0.9**step)
            v_hat = second / (1.0 - 0.999**step)
            Y = Y - self.learning_rate * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + 1e-8)

    The bias correction uses step starting at 1, which is the only fiddly part of Adam: at step = 1, 1 - 0.9**1 = 0.1 exactly cancels the 0.1 in the accumulator, so the first step is a normalised gradient step of length learning_rate. The gradient itself is divided by the triplet count, so learning_rate is per-triplet-mean and comparable across dataset sizes — the demo's 0.1 gives an embedding of radius 9.4 on the S-curve.

    Complexity #

    With \(n\) points, \(d\) embedding dimensions, \(m\) neighbours, and \(T = (mm' + r)n\) triplets:

    stage time memory
    pairwise distances, this implementation \(O(n^2 d)\) \(O(n^2)\)
    neighbour selection (argsort of each row) \(O(n^2 \log n)\) \(O(nm)\)
    triplet sampling \(O(T)\) \(O(T)\)
    one gradient step \(O(Td)\) \(O(Td + nd)\)
    400 steps \(O(Tdn)\) —

    Measured on the S-curve, per iteration and end to end:

    n triplets per iteration 400 iterations
    100 5 100 0.96 ms 0.38 s
    300 15 300 2.79 ms 1.12 s
    600 30 600 5.59 ms 2.24 s
    1200 61 200 11.46 ms 4.58 s

    Both columns scale linearly in n to within the noise of a laptop, which is the claim worth checking: the optimisation is linear because the triplet budget is proportional to n, not to \(n^2\), and the gradient never touches a pair that was not sampled. The quadratic part is entirely in the two rows this implementation cannot avoid. The paper's version uses an approximate nearest-neighbour index (Annoy, then PyNNDescent) so that neither the distance matrix nor the full sort exists, with memory \(O(nm + T)\); that is what carries it to the 11 million points in the HIGGS benchmark. Here, n = 1200 already allocates a 11.5 MB distance matrix, and n = 100 000 would need 80 GB — the method scales, the file does not.

    Failure modes #

    The local-only objective prefers the wrong embedding #

    This is the one worth internalising. Restrict k to the two-hop ring and the loss still decreases nicely — 14 400 triplets, loss falling every iteration — while the embedding crumples: overlap 0.553 against 0.774, distance correlation 0.055 against 0.895, global score 0.002 against 0.638. It is not a bug and not a bad local minimum. Scored on its own triplets the crumple is strictly better than the good embedding (7 806 against 11 649), because every triplet it was given is a claim about relative order inside a neighbourhood, and squashing neighbourhoods flat satisfies all of them. Any triplet method is only as global as its far points.

    The loss has no scale anchor #

    The derivation showed that for a correctly ordered triplet the loss decreases monotonically with the embedding's overall scale, and 98% of triplets are correctly ordered at convergence. Nothing in (2) counteracts it, so the radius is set by the learning rate and the iteration count rather than by the data:

    learning rate final radius overlap @10 global score
    0.02 4.72 0.731 0.561
    0.05 7.04 0.762 0.521
    0.10 9.40 0.774 0.638
    0.20 12.61 0.783 0.651
    0.50 18.29 0.788 0.583

    Read the last row as the failure: the neighbourhoods get better (0.788 is the best in the table) while the global score falls, because the far points are being pushed to a scale where equation (3) has saturated and the loss is nearly indifferent to them. The default 0.1 is not tuned to this dataset; it is a middle value that keeps the radius near the PCA initialisation's.

    The model is a sample #

    Two fits with different seeds produce different triplets, different losses and embeddings that are not comparable without alignment. On the S-curve the global score ranges from 0.476 (seed 2) to 0.669 (seed 3) at otherwise identical settings, and the seed with the lowest loss (seed 0, 6 926) is not the seed with the best geometry. The loss is a mean over sampled constraints; the global score is a property of the data. Optimising the first does not guarantee the second, and the gap between them is sampling variance that no number of iterations removes.

    Neighbour count and degeneracies #

    m must be at least 6, because the self-tuning scale reads the 4th to 6th neighbours; the constructor raises rather than silently using a different scale. m must also be smaller than n, and a dataset of 6 points or fewer cannot be embedded at all. Pushing m up is the other way to lose the global structure — 48 neighbours gives a global score of 0.297 against 0.638 — for the reason in the Variants section: every triplet becomes local.

    The global score is a linear measure #

    Equation (12) assumes a linear map rebuilds X from Y. On data with strong non-linear structure the score is small for every method, including good embeddings, and the paper says so in its appendix. The score also cannot see a single displaced outlier: one point moving to the wrong cluster changes the residual by \(O(1)\) in a sum of \(n\) terms. Use it to compare methods on the same data, not as an absolute quality claim, and pair it with a neighbourhood measure as the demo does.

    Applications #

    • Single-cell transcriptomes. The motivating use case: atlases of 50 000 to a million cells, where the question is not only "which cells are similar" but "where does this cluster sit relative to the others". The Tabula Muris benchmark in the paper is exactly this, and TriMap's advantage there is preserving the outlier populations that t-SNE folds into the main mass.
    • Large image and text embeddings. MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR feature layers, TF-IDF of 360 000 song lyrics. The linear-in-n iteration cost and the approximate-neighbour search are what make the million-point end of that list possible.
    • Diagnosing a representation. The paper's CIFAR experiment embeds the first two fully-connected layers of a trained network and reads the class hierarchy off the picture: animal versus vehicle at the top, then deer/horse and cat/dog inside. A method that only preserved neighbourhoods would show ten blobs and no hierarchy; the hierarchy is the global placement, which is the thing being measured.
    • Semi-supervised embedding. The supervised variant is the cheap version of this: when labels exist for some points, use them to choose triplets. It sharpens clusters at the cost of global geometry, which is the right trade when the plot is being used to check a classifier rather than to explore.
    • Ordinal data. The loss (2) is the same one t-STE uses for human triplet judgements. TriMap's contribution is the sampler and the weights, not the loss, so it can be pointed at any source of "A is more like B than C" — similarity judgements, click logs, ranked retrieval results.

    • E. Amid and M. K. Warmuth, TriMap: Large-scale Dimensionality Reduction Using Triplets, arXiv:1910.00204 (2019, revised 2022). The source for the loss, the weight scheme, the global score and every benchmark quoted above.
    • The reference implementation, eamid/trimap on GitHub, and the JAX rewrite at google/trimap. Both were read for this file: the gradient in trimap_grad, the violation counter, and the n_inliers × n_outliers sampling budget come from there.
    • L. van der Maaten and G. Hinton, Visualizing Data using t-SNE, JMLR 9 (2008). The Student-t similarity in (1), and the local-accuracy opponent in the Variants table.
    • L. McInnes, J. Healy and J. Melville, UMAP: Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection, arXiv:1802.03426 (2018). The other opponent, and the source of the fuzzy-set view of neighbourhoods that TriMap deliberately does not take.
    • L. Zelnik-Manor and P. Perona, Self-Tuning Spectral Clustering, NeurIPS (2005). The \(\sigma_i\) of equation (5).
    • J. Naudts, Deformed exponentials and logarithms in generalized thermostatistics, Physica A 316 (2002). The tempered logarithm of (6).
    • L. van der Maaten and K. Weinberger, Stochastic Triplet Embedding, MLSP (2012). The triplet loss as a likelihood, and the reason TriMap's sampler is the interesting part.
    • J. Tang, J. Liu, M. Zhang and Q. Mei, LargeVis, WWW (2016). The neighbour-graph-first pipeline that TriMap's neighbour search shares.
