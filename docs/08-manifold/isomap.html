    08 · Manifolds & embeddings/tier 1/4 variants/source

    Isomap

    Intuition #

    A swiss roll is the standard picture of why Euclidean distance is the wrong question.

            ┌──────────────────────────────┐
       ╱   ·  ·        ·   ·            ╲      two points on neighbouring turns
      │  ·      ·   ·        ·     ·     │     are close in R³  (a)
      │ ·    ·        ·   ·       ·      │     and far along the sheet (b)
       ╲  ·   ·    ·       ·   ·        ╱
        └──────────────────────────────┘
             a: ‖x_i − x_j‖₃ small
             b: length of the path on the sheet large

    Every method in this chapter is a different answer to "close in what?" This one answers: close along the data. Build a graph whose edges are the distances between nearby points, and replace every distance by the length of the shortest path through that graph.

    That is the whole algorithm, and it is two lines:

    1. connect each point to its \(k\) nearest neighbours, weighting edges by Euclidean distance;
    2. run classical MDS on the resulting geodesic distance matrix.

    The effect is large because the graph cannot leave the surface. On the roll, the shortest path between two points on adjacent turns has to walk out to the end of the sheet and back, which is exactly what the true distance along the surface does. The demo measures it: 600 points on a swiss roll, which is exactly isometric to a flat rectangle, are recovered at distance correlation \(0.9996\) by Isomap and \(0.2930\) by classical MDS on the ambient distances.

    graph LR
    A["X ∈ ℝⁿˣᵈ<br/>point cloud"] --> B["kNN graph<br/>w_ij = ‖x_i − x_j‖"]
    B --> C["all-pairs shortest paths<br/>d_ij = geodesic"]
    C --> D["classical MDS<br/>double-centre, eigendecompose"]
    D --> E["Y ∈ ℝⁿˣᵏ"]

    The price is a knife edge. The graph only knows about edges between points it connected, so an edge that jumps between turns — a short-circuit — makes every geodesic that crosses it too short, and no amount of downstream linear algebra recovers. The demo's \(k = 14\) gives \(0.9999\) and \(k = 16\) gives \(0.7433\) on the same data. Isomap is the clearest case in machine learning of a method whose hyper-parameter is not a regularisation strength but a topological condition.

    Variants #

    Variant Distances used What changes Cost
    isomap all pairs, exact the reference \(n\) shortest-path runs
    landmark to \(m\) landmarks embed landmarks, triangulate the rest \(m\) runs, \(m \ll n\)
    conformal rescaled geodesics divide by a local scale from the sampling density one extra pass, no extra runs
    sparse all pairs, completed exact rows from \(m\) sources, min-plus closure \(m\) runs + \(mn^2\)

    isomap is the 2000 paper: kNN graph, all-pairs shortest paths, classical MDS. The demo uses a vectorised Floyd–Warshall, which is \(O(n^3)\) but has no Python loop over sources; the alternative is \(n\) Dijkstra runs, which is what the sparse graph actually wants and what the other two variants use.

    landmark (de Silva & Tenenbaum 2003) attacks the \(O(n^3)\) directly. Pick \(m\) landmarks, compute geodesics from those only, embed the \(m \times m\) landmark submatrix, and place every other point by least-squares triangulation from its distances to the landmarks. The demo runs 70 shortest-path searches instead of 700, and the embedding is indistinguishable: correlation with the truth \(0.9997\) against \(0.9997\), agreement between the two embeddings \(0.99997\). It is also the variant whose inference cost is bounded by \(m\): placing one new point needs its distances to the landmarks, not to every sample.

    conformal (C-Isomap, same paper) is for data whose metric differs from the one you want by a smooth local scale factor — a conformal distortion, which is what you get when a manifold is stretched unevenly or sampled through a map that magnifies some regions more than others. Estimate the local scale from the mean kNN radius, divide each geodesic by \(\sqrt{\lambda_i\lambda_j}\), and re-embed. On the demo — a disk pushed onto the sphere by stereographic projection, which is exactly conformal — raw Isomap reaches \(0.8152\) and the corrected one \(0.9289\). The same estimator, given a density gradient on flat data, takes \(0.9992\) down to \(0.1880\). The method cannot tell the two apart, and that is the honest summary of it.

    sparse computes exact geodesics from a subset of source nodes and fills the rest by \(D_{ij} \approx \min_l (D_{il} + D_{lj})\) over the sources. Every term is a walk through a source, so the completion is an upper bound on the true geodesic; the demo's mean error falls from \(0.6124\) to \(0.0123\) as the source fraction goes from 5% to 40%. It is the right variant when you already have distances from a few nodes — a sensor network with a few anchors, say — and want the rest.

    Derivation #

    1. The graph and the geodesic #

    Let \(G\) be the graph with vertex set the samples and an edge \((i,j)\) whenever \(j\) is among the \(k\) nearest neighbours of \(i\) (or the reverse — the union, so that the graph is undirected):

    \[w_{ij} \;=\; \lVert x_i - x_j \rVert, \qquad (i,j)\in E. \qquad (1)\]

    The graph distance is the length of the shortest path,

    \[d_{ij} \;=\; \min_{\gamma:\, i\to j}\ \sum_{(a,b)\in\gamma} w_{ab}, \qquad (2)\]

    and because every graph path is a curve in the ambient space, \(d_{ij} \ge d_{\mathcal{M}}(i,j)\): the graph distance is always an over-estimate of the geodesic on the manifold, and it is a lower bound on the ambient distance only in the sense that it is the shortest such curve through the data.

    2. Why the graph distance is the right one #

    If the data are dense on a manifold and the edges are short enough that none of them cuts across a fold, the shortest path in \(G\) converges to the geodesic on \(\mathcal{M}\) as \(n\to\infty\). The precise statement (Bernstein, de Silva, Langford & Tenenbaum 2000) is that the graph distance converges uniformly when the kNN radius is bounded by the manifold's reach — its minimum radius of curvature and its minimum separation between distinct sheets — divided by a constant.

    That is the whole theory, and it is a statement about geometry, not about algorithms. It also tells you exactly how Isomap fails: when the kNN radius exceeds the local reach, an edge connects two sheets, and every distance that crosses that edge is shortened.

    3. How big can \(k\) be? #

    Make the failure condition quantitative. For \(n\) points uniform on a surface of area \(A\), the expected number of points within radius \(r\) is \(\pi r^2 n/A\), so the kNN radius is

    \[r_k \;\approx\; \sqrt{\frac{kA}{\pi n}}. \qquad (3)\]

    The roll has sheets separated by \(2\pi\) in radius, so an edge can jump a turn once \(r_k\) reaches about \(\pi\) from the middle of a sheet — and solving (3) for that,

    \[k^{*} \;\approx\; \frac{\pi^3 n}{A}. \qquad (4)\]

    The demo's sheet has \(A \approx 89.4 \times 12 = 1072\) and \(n = 600\), giving \(k^* \approx 17.3\). The measurement: \(k = 14\) scores \(0.9999\) and \(k = 16\) scores \(0.7433\), one step below the estimate. Equation (4) is an approximation to a phase transition, not a theorem, but it gets the order of magnitude and the scaling right: more samples means a larger safe \(k\), since \(k^*\) grows linearly with \(n\).

    4. The MDS step #

    Given \(d\), the embedding is the classical MDS solution of mds:

    \[Y \;=\; \arg\min_{Y}\ \sum_{i

    solved in closed form by double-centring the squared distances,

    \[B \;=\; -\tfrac12\, J\, d^{(2)} J, \qquad B = V\Lambda V^{\mathsf T}, \qquad Y = V_k \Lambda_k^{1/2}, \qquad (6)\]

    with \(J = I - \tfrac1n \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}\). Equation (6) minimises strain, not the stress of (5); they agree when \(d\) is Euclidean, which is what the graph approximation is trying to make it. Nothing in this step is Isomap's contribution — the whole method is the choice of \(d\).

    5. Landmarks and triangulation #

    Write \(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_m\) for the landmarks and \(D_{li}\) for the geodesic distance from landmark \(l\) to point \(i\). The landmark submatrix \(D[\ell,\ell]\) is an \(m\times m\) distance matrix, so (6) embeds the landmarks. For any other point, expand the squared distance to landmark \(l\),

    \[\lVert y_i - y_{\ell}\rVert^2 \;=\; D_{\ell i}^2 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \lVert y_i\rVert^2 - 2\,y_{\ell}^{\mathsf T}y_i + \lVert y_{\ell}\rVert^2 = D_{\ell i}^2. \qquad (7)\]

    Subtract the equation for landmark \(\ell_1\) and the quadratic term cancels, leaving a linear system in \(y_i\):

    \[2\,(y_{\ell} - y_{\ell_1})^{\mathsf T} y_i \;=\; D_{\ell_1 i}^2 - D_{\ell i}^2 + \lVert y_{\ell}\rVert^2 - \lVert y_{\ell_1}\rVert^2. \qquad (8)\]

    That is \(k\) unknowns and \(m-1\) equations per point, solved by least squares. When \(d\) is exactly Euclidean and \(m \ge k+1\), (8) recovers \(y_i\) exactly — which is why the demo's 12 landmarks already give \(0.9996\): the residual error is not the number of landmarks, it is the error in the geodesics themselves.

    6. Conformal distortion #

    A conformal map multiplies the metric by a smooth positive function,

    \[\tilde g_{ij}(x) \;=\; \lambda^2(x)\, g_{ij}(x), \qquad (9)\]

    so distances near \(x\) are \(\lambda(x)\) times what they should be. C-Isomap's correction is the symmetric one,

    \[d'_{ij} \;=\; \frac{d_{ij}}{\sqrt{\lambda_i \lambda_j}}, \qquad (10)\]

    which is exact infinitesimally and a first-order approximation along a finite path: the true geodesic accumulates \(\lambda\) continuously, while (10) only looks at the endpoints. The constant in \(\lambda\) does not matter, because (6) is scale-equivariant: \(Y(c\,d) = c\,Y(d)\).

    The factor has to be estimated from the data, and the estimate is where the assumption enters. If the sample is uniform with respect to the undistorted metric, the number of points in a small region is proportional to the true area, so the local spacing in the distorted space is \(\lambda\) times the spacing in the true one. Hence

    \[\hat\lambda_i \;\propto\; \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\in N_k(i)} \lVert x_i - x_j\rVert, \qquad (11)\]

    the mean radius of a \(k\)-neighbourhood. The demo's estimator tracks the true factor at correlation \(0.9433\) on the sphere data — and interprets a density gradient on flat data as a factor that is not there, which is failure mode 3.

    7. Sparse completion #

    Let \(S\) be the set of source nodes whose distance rows were computed. Define

    \[\hat D_{ij} \;=\; \min_{l\in S}\big(D_{il} + D_{lj}\big). \qquad (12)\]

    Each term in the minimum is the length of a walk \(i\to l\to j\), so \(\hat D_{ij} \ge d_{ij}\) by the triangle inequality, with equality when \(l=i\) or \(l=j\); and a path through several sources never beats the best single one, again by the triangle inequality, so (12) is already the shortest-path metric of the source-hub graph. It is a metric, it agrees with \(d\) on every pair that touches a source, and its error on the rest is the detour through the nearest useful hub.

    8. What is identified #

    As with every distance-based embedding, only the distances are data:

    \[Y \;\mapsto\; YQ + \mathbf{1}t^{\mathsf T}, \qquad Q^{\mathsf T}Q = I, \qquad (13)\]

    so the demo compares distance matrices, distance correlations and neighbourhoods — never coordinates. This is not a technicality. A plot of an Isomap embedding is a picture of a metric, and which way it is rotated is not a property of the data.

    Code walkthrough #

    Isomap(n_components=2, *, variant="isomap", n_neighbors=10, n_landmarks=None, seed=0) with fit(X), fit_transform(X), and the attributes embedding_ (n, n_components), distances_ (the matrix the embedding fits), stress_, landmarks_ and scales_. X is a (samples, features) point cloud: unlike MDS, Isomap needs the points, because the graph is built from them.

    1. The graph, symmetrised by union #

        rows = np.repeat(np.arange(n), k)
    cols = idx.ravel()
    W = np.full((n, n), np.inf)
    np.fill_diagonal(W, 0.0)
    np.minimum.at(W, (rows, cols), D[rows, cols])
    np.minimum.at(W, (cols, rows), D[rows, cols])

    (1). Both directions are written because the kNN relation is not symmetric: \(j\) can be among \(i\)'s neighbours while \(i\) is not among \(j\)'s. Taking the union rather than the intersection is what keeps the graph connected on the boundary of a sample, where a one-sided rule leaves points with no outgoing edge at all. The minimum.at is needed rather than plain fancy assignment because the same \((i,j)\) can be reached twice; it takes the smaller weight, which is the one the edge should have.

    2. All-pairs shortest paths without a source loop #

        D = W.copy()
    for k in range(D.shape[0]):
        np.minimum(D, D[:, k][:, None] + D[k][None, :], out=D)
    return D

    (2), by Floyd–Warshall. Each iteration asks one question: "is going through \(k\) shorter than what I have?" — and doing it for every \(k\) is the dynamic program. The whole thing is three lines because the inner loop is a rank-1 update of an \(n\times n\) matrix, and out=D keeps it to one allocation per pass. inf is the right initial value rather than a large number: it makes "no edge" arithmetically inert and lets the final matrix be tested for connectivity directly.

    3. The sparse alternative #

        dist = np.full(n, np.inf)
    dist[source] = 0.0
    heap = [(0.0, source)]
    while heap:
        d, i = heapq.heappop(heap)
        if d > dist[i]:
            continue                       # stale heap entry, already improved
        for j, w in adj[i]:
            candidate = d + w
            if candidate < dist[j]:
                dist[j] = candidate
                heapq.heappush(heap, (candidate, j))
    return dist

    The same distances from one source, in \(O(E\log n)\) instead of \(O(n^2)\) per source. The if d > dist[i]: continue is not an optimisation — a lazy heap contains one entry per successful relaxation, so most entries are stale, and without the check the algorithm is still correct but re-expands nodes many times. This is the routine the landmark and sparse variants call, and the reason a neighbourhood of 70 landmarks costs 70 searches rather than a full \(O(n^3)\).

    4. The embedding step #

        n = D.shape[0]
    J = np.eye(n) - np.full((n, n), 1.0 / n)
    B = -0.5 * J @ (D ** 2) @ J
    evals, evecs = np.linalg.eigh(B)
    order = np.argsort(evals)[::-1][:n_components]
    return evecs[:, order] * np.sqrt(np.maximum(evals[order], 0.0))

    (6), and the three places it can go silently wrong are all visible: eigh returns ascending eigenvalues, so the [::-1] is load-bearing; a graph geodesic matrix is only approximately Euclidean, so \(B\) has negative eigenvalues and the clamp is a projection onto the nearest positive semidefinite matrix rather than an error; and D ** 2 on a matrix that still contains inf (a disconnected graph) produces nan two lines later, which is why connectivity is checked before this is reached.

    5. Landmarks: distances to a few, geometry for all #

            land = self._farthest_point(_pairwise(X), m)
        adj = _adjacency(W)
        D_land = np.stack([_dijkstra(adj, int(s), n) for s in land])   # (m, n)
        Y = np.empty((n, self.n_components))
        Y[land] = _classical(D_land[:, land], self.n_components)
        rest = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(n), land, assume_unique=False)
        Y[rest] = _triangulate(D_land[:, rest], Y[land])

    The whole variant in six lines: choose, search, embed the square submatrix, triangulate. Farthest-point sampling is deterministic — there is no seed here — which matters because a random landmark set would make every reported number a random variable, and the demo's job is to report a measurement.

    6. The triangulation, which is just linear algebra #

        base = np.sum(Y[1:] ** 2, axis=1) - float(np.sum(Y[0] ** 2))
    rows = 2.0 * (Y[1:] - Y[0])                              # (m − 1, k)
    rhs = D[0][None, :] ** 2 - D[1:] ** 2 + base[:, None]    # (m − 1, points)
    coef, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(rows, rhs, rcond=None)
    return coef.T

    (7)–(8), for all points at once. The \((m-1)\times k\) matrix rows is the same for every point, so one lstsq with a matrix of right-hand sides solves the whole batch. Subtracting landmark 0 rather than solving the quadratic system is what makes it linear; the cost is that landmark 0's position is taken as exact, which is fine because it came from the same MDS solve as the others.

    7. The conformal scale #

            idx = _knn(X, min(self.n_neighbors, n - 1))
        radius = _pairwise(X)[np.arange(n)[:, None], idx].mean(axis=1)
        smoothed = radius[idx].mean(axis=1)
        self.scales_ = smoothed / max(float(np.exp(np.mean(np.log(smoothed)))), EPS)

    (11). The second line is the estimator and the third is a one-hop average over the same graph. That average is not cosmetic: a single neighbourhood's mean radius has a standard error of order \(1/\sqrt{k}\) under Poisson sampling, and the first version of this file used it unsmoothed and measured \(0.8832\) where the smoothed one measures \(0.9289\) on the same data. Normalising by the geometric mean only fixes the arbitrary scale; it has no effect on the result, since (6) is scale-equivariant, and it is there so scales_ can be read as a relative factor.

            scaled = D / np.sqrt(self.scales_[:, None] * self.scales_[None, :])
        self.embedding_ = _classical(scaled, self.n_components)

    (10). distances_ is set to scaled, not to D: the embedding fits the rescaled matrix, and a stress_ computed against the raw one would be reporting the wrong residual.

    8. The sparse closure #

            rows = np.stack([_dijkstra(adj, int(s), n) for s in sources])  # (m, n)

        D = np.full((n, n), np.inf)
        for row in rows:
            np.minimum(D, row[:, None] + row[None, :], out=D)
        np.fill_diagonal(D, 0.0)

    (12). Starting from inf rather than from zeros is the bug this file shipped first: a min against an all-zero matrix returns zeros, the embedding is the embedding of the zero matrix, and the failure looks like a nan correlation three functions away. The diagonal is overwritten afterwards because \(D_{ii}\) would otherwise be \(2\min_l D_{il}\), the distance to the nearest source doubled, which is a small positive number where the metric requires zero.

    9. The check that turns a crash into a sentence #

            if _n_components(W) > 1:
            raise ValueError(
                f"the {self.n_neighbors}-neighbour graph is disconnected; "
                "increase n_neighbors or sample more densely"
            )

    A disconnected graph is not an edge case to be patched with nan_to_num. Each component would be embedded in its own arbitrary position, and the resulting picture would look like a successful embedding with a mysterious cluster structure. The demo hits this at \(k=6\) on the roll, and the message says what to do about it.

    Complexity #

    \(n\) samples, \(d\) input dimensions, \(k\) neighbours, \(m\) landmarks or sources, \(E \approx nk\) edges.

    Operation Time Memory Note
    pairwise distances \(O(n^2d)\) \(O(n^2)\) Gram identity, no \((n,n,d)\) array
    kNN \(O(n^2\log n)\) \(O(n^2)\) full argsort; a tree would be \(O(n\log n)\)
    Floyd–Warshall \(O(n^3)\) \(O(n^2)\) vectorised, one pass per node
    Dijkstra, all sources \(O(nE\log n)\) \(O(n)\) per source the sparse graph's natural cost
    Dijkstra, \(m\) sources \(O(mE\log n)\) \(O(n)\) per source landmark, sparse
    min-plus closure \(O(mn^2)\) \(O(n^2)\) one rank-1 min per source
    classical MDS \(O(n^3)\) \(O(n^2)\) one symmetric eigendecomposition
    triangulation \(O\big(mk(n-m) + mk^2\big)\) \(O(mn)\) one batched lstsq
    full demo — — 14 fits, 3.0 s

    The two \(O(n^3)\) rows decide the design. Floyd–Warshall and the eigendecomposition are the same order, so isomap is an \(O(n^3)\) method end to end and stops being practical somewhere around several thousand points. landmark removes both: \(m\) Dijkstra runs are \(O(mE\log n)\) and the landmark eigendecomposition is \(O(m^3)\), leaving the triangulation, which is linear in \(n\). That is the variant to use at scale, and it is what every production Isomap does.

    Memory is \(O(n^2)\) throughout, and it is the real wall. A geodesic matrix for \(10^5\) points is 80 GB; the graph itself is \(O(nk)\) and sparse, so the honest large-scale version never materialises the matrix and does the MDS step by stochastic descent on sampled pairs.

    Failure modes #

    1. A short-circuit, which is a topological failure and not a tuning problem. The measurement is not subtle: \(k=14\) gives \(0.9999\), \(k=16\) gives \(0.7433\), \(k=32\) gives \(0.1760\), and the maximum graph distance falls from \(91.5\) to \(37.8\) as edges open between turns. Nothing downstream can detect this — the matrix is a valid distance matrix and the embedding is a valid embedding of the wrong metric. Use (4) to place \(k\) below the predicted transition, and plot the geodesic distances against the ambient ones as a check: a straight line that falls below \(y = x\) is a shortcut.

    2. A disconnected graph. At \(k=6\) on the same roll the graph is in pieces and the code raises. The tempting fix — filling the missing distances with a large number — embeds each component independently and produces a picture with a made-up layout between them. Increase \(k\), or increase the sample, or accept that the data are not one manifold.

    3. Conformal correction on data that is not conformally distorted. The demo takes the flat plane, where the metric is Euclidean and nothing needs correcting, samples it with density \(\propto 1/r\), and the estimator reads the gradient as a scale factor: correlation falls from \(0.9992\) to \(0.1880\). "The metric is stretched here" and "there are more points here" are the same observation, and the method has no way to prefer one. C-Isomap is safe when the sampling is uniform by design and the distortion is real.

    4. The estimate is noisy at small \(k\). A single neighbourhood radius has standard error \(\sim 1/\sqrt{k}\) under Poisson sampling; at \(k=10\) that is a 30% error per point, which is why (11) is smoothed over one hop. Below \(k \approx 5\) the correction is dominated by that noise, and the demo's own numbers improved from \(0.8832\) to \(0.9289\) when the smoothing was added — a reminder that an estimator with the right expectation can still be worse than doing nothing.

    5. Landmark errors are geodesic errors, not triangulation errors. With 12 landmarks the demo still scores \(0.9996\): (8) is exact given exact distances, and \(k+1\) of them suffice. So the landmark count is a cost knob, not an accuracy knob — spending more landmarks buys you a better-conditioned triangulation and nothing else. What does hurt is choosing landmarks badly, which is why they are spread by farthest-point sampling rather than drawn at random.

    6. Sparse completion is biased upward, and unevenly. The mean gap is positive by construction — every completion is a detour — but the maximum gap is what matters: \(20.2\) at 5% of sources against a mean of \(0.61\). A handful of pairs are badly over-estimated, and in an embedding those are the pairs that decide the large-scale layout. More sources is the only fix; the closure cannot invent information.

    7. Noise creates edges that are not geodesics. A point jittered off the surface is connected to its jittered neighbours, and the path through the noise is shorter than the path along the manifold. Isomap assumes noise-free samples on a smooth manifold; on real data the usual repair is to denoise first or to use a robust graph (mutual kNN, or a threshold on edge length relative to the local scale).

    8. There is no out-of-sample transform. fit embeds the training points and nothing else. A new point needs its distances to the graph, which means inserting it, recomputing shortest paths, and re-solving — not a projection. The landmark variant is the exception in principle — distances to the landmarks plus (8) place a new point directly — though this implementation does not expose a transform, because a new point still has to be attached to the graph before its distances to the landmarks exist.

    9. The intrinsic dimension is an assumption, not an output. n_components is chosen by you; Isomap does not estimate it, and asking for one dimension fewer than the manifold has produces a plot with no warning and a stress that looks acceptable. Compare the stress against n_components + 1 the way the mds demo does.

    Applications #

    • Manifold learning and visualisation. The original use: recovering the intrinsic coordinates of a data set that lies on a curved surface — robot joint angles from images of an arm, pose from pixel vectors, a low-dimensional parameterisation of a family of shapes.
    • Embedding a graph's metric. Any graph with edge lengths has an Isomap embedding, and it is one of the standard layouts for a network whose edges are real distances (road networks, mesh parameterisation) rather than affinities. This is the same computation as Kamada–Kawai, which is SMACOF on graph geodesics.
    • Sensor and network localisation. Landmark Isomap is exactly the structure of the problem: distances from a few anchors, positions for everything else, triangulated linearly. The distances can be hops or time-of-flight.
    • Feature construction before a supervised model. The embedding coordinates are a compact, geometry-aware representation, and landmark makes the transform available for new points.
    • Sanity check on other embeddings. Because Isomap fits only the geodesic distances, its stress is a direct measure of how far a manifold is from flat. A high stress on data you believe is low-dimensional is information.
    • Conformally distorted data. C-Isomap was built for exactly one situation: you know the sampling is uniform in the metric you want and the measured space is a conformal image of it. Under that assumption it is the only method in this chapter that corrects the distortion explicitly.

    Where it is a trap: choosing \(k\) by the look of the plot; applying the conformal variant to data whose density varies for any other reason; using Isomap on data with more than one connected component; trusting an embedding whose graph diameter collapsed; and expecting Isomap to denoise, which it does not.
