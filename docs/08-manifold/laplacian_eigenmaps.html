    08 · Manifolds & embeddings/tier 2/3 variants/source

    Laplacian eigenmaps

    Intuition #

    Locally linear embedding spends its first half deciding what the graph is: it solves a reconstruction problem per neighbourhood and gets a weight matrix. This method refuses that half. The graph is given — you or your nearest-neighbour rule built it — and the only question left is what to put on the vertices. The answer is a coordinate that changes as little as possible across every edge:

            x₁ ·──0.9──· x₂ ·──0.4──· x₃

        f = (0.0, 0.1, 0.2)   fᵀLf = ½[0.9·0.1² + 0.4·0.1²] = 0.0065   smooth
        f = (0.0, 0.9, 0.1)   fᵀLf = ½[0.9·0.9² + 0.4·0.8²] = 0.4925   rough

    Both assignments are perfectly legal coordinates on three points. The first is a slow ramp; the second jumps across an edge that the graph says is close. The method takes the second kind of assignment away from you, because on a proximity graph a coordinate that jumps across an edge is a coordinate that ignores the data's own notion of nearness.

    graph LR
    A["X ∈ ℝⁿˣᵈ"] --> B["kNN graph"]
    B --> C["W: heat kernel<br/>or ones"]
    C --> D["L = D − W"]
    D --> E["low eigenvectors<br/>of L f = λ D f"]
    E --> F["drop the flat mode<br/>Y ∈ ℝⁿˣ²"]

    What makes this an embedding rather than a smoothing filter is that the smoothness cost is a quadratic form, so its minimisers are eigenvectors, and the eigenvectors come in a hierarchy: the flattest non-constant function, then the next flattest orthogonal to it, and so on. That hierarchy is a Fourier basis of the graph. For a circle it is exactly the classical Fourier basis, which is the one case where the whole calculation has a closed form — and the demo checks it. 200 points uniformly spaced on a circle, \(k = 2\), so the graph is a cycle and every degree is exactly 2. The generalized eigenvalues are \(1 - \cos(2\pi j/n)\), each with multiplicity two; the six returned ones match that formula to \(3 \times 10^{-16}\), the gap inside each degenerate pair is \(4 \times 10^{-16}\), and the embedding correlates \(1.0000\) with \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\).

    On data that is not a circle, the same eigenproblem is an approximation to the Laplace–Beltrami operator of the surface the points were sampled from — the continuum limit in section 7 — which is the sense in which the method "recovers the manifold". It is also the sense in which it cannot: what it really recovers is the spectrum of the graph you built, and a graph that cuts the sheet has a different spectrum.

    The demo's first measurement is that the algebra is exact where it can be. 200 points on a random plane in three dimensions, \(k = 10\), the unweighted graph: the constant vector is in the kernel to \(0\), with eigenvalue \(2 \times 10^{-16}\); the two returned coordinates satisfy the generalized problem to \(9 \times 10^{-16}\) and are \(D\)-orthonormal to \(10^{-15}\); and their Dirichlet quotients \(f^\top L f / f^\top D f\) equal their eigenvalues, \(0.019623\) and \(0.027960\), to every digit printed. The energy identity is not a property the embedding happens to have — it is the objective.

    Variants #

    Three ways to write the same eigenproblem, and the demo measures how much of the difference is real.

    variant graph operator overlap @8 @40 distance corr.
    standard unweighted \(L = D - W\), generalized problem 0.455 0.749 0.834
    normalized heat kernel \(I - D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}\) 0.510 0.755 0.837
    diffusion_link heat kernel \(P = D^{-1}W\), decay \(\mu_k^s\) 0.510 0.755 0.839
    — PCA baseline — 0.525 0.761 0.860

    300 points on an S-curve, \(k = 10\), heat scale at its default. standard is the textbook Belkin–Niyogi formulation with the combinatorial Laplacian and an unweighted graph. normalized changes two things at once — a heat kernel instead of ones, and the symmetric normalization instead of the generalized problem — and the second of those changes nothing mathematically: \(L f = \lambda D f\) with \(f = D^{-1/2}v\) is exactly the symmetric problem in \(v\). So the entire gap between the first two rows is the kernel, and it is small: the second eigenvalue moves from \(0.0032\) to \(0.0022\), the neighbour overlap from \(0.455\) to \(0.510\), the distance correlation from \(0.834\) to \(0.837\).

    That is not an accident of this dataset. As the bandwidth grows, \(\exp(-d^2/t) \to 1\) and the heat graph becomes the unweighted graph: at \(25\times\) the default scale, \(\lambda_2\) settles at \(0.0032\) and matches standard to three digits. standard is the \(t \to \infty\) limit of normalized, which is why the two cannot disagree much on well-sampled data, and why the choice between them is mostly about conditioning and about the one scale you are willing to set.

    diffusion_link is a different kind of variant. It computes the same eigenvectors of the same matrix and then multiplies coordinate \(k\) by \(\mu_k^s\) with \(\mu_k = 1 - \lambda_k\). At \(s = 1\) that is a per-axis factor of \(0.9978\) and \(0.9918\) — the two layouts agree to machine precision up to that rescaling, and the demo asserts it. The variant is only interesting for \(s > 1\), where the high modes are damped geometrically and the layout becomes a coarser, more global view of the same graph. That is the diffusion-map construction, and the scaling is the whole of it.

    The fourth line is the honest comparison for this kind of data: the leading two principal components keep \(0.525\) at eight neighbours and correlate \(0.860\). On a smooth sheet the graph buys essentially nothing over a rotation, and the numbers say so. Where it buys a great deal is when the low modes are close to indicators — separated clusters — which is the spectral-clustering case in the applications.

    Derivation #

    1. The graph, and its Laplacian #

    Given \(X = (x_1, \dots, x_n)\) and \(k\), the graph is the symmetric \(k\)-nearest neighbour graph with heat-kernel weights,

    \[W_{ij} = \exp\left(-\frac{\lVert x_i - x_j \rVert^2}{t}\right) \quad \text{if } j \in \mathrm{kNN}(i) \text{ or } i \in \mathrm{kNN}(j), \qquad W_{ij} = 0 \text{ otherwise} \qquad (1)\]
    \[D = \mathrm{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_n), \qquad d_i = \sum_j W_{ij}, \qquad L = D - W \qquad (2)\]

    Two details in (1) are decisions rather than notation. The symmetrisation takes the union — an edge survives if either endpoint chose it — so every vertex keeps its own \(k\) neighbours and the result is connected at least as often as the mutual version. The bandwidth \(t\) has units of squared distance; the default is the mean squared distance to the \(k\)-th neighbour, which makes it scale with the data. Neither choice is forced, and both change the answer.

    2. The Dirichlet form #

    For any vector \(f \in \mathbb{R}^n\) assigned to the vertices,

    \[f^\top L f = f^\top D f - f^\top W f = \sum_i d_i f_i^2 - \sum_{i,j} W_{ij} f_i f_j = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j} W_{ij}\,(f_i - f_j)^2 \qquad (3)\]

    using \(d_i = \sum_j W_{ij}\) and the symmetry of \(W\). Every term is non-negative, so \(L\) is positive semidefinite and \(f^\top L f\) is a sum of squared differences weighted by edge strength — an energy, in the physicist's sense. A small value means \(f\) is nearly constant across every strong edge. Equivalently, \(L f\) is the vector of "how much above your neighbours' average you are", weighted by degree; \(f\) is smooth exactly when that vector is small.

    3. From energy to eigenvectors #

    The flat function \(\mathbf{1}\) has zero energy and so does the zero vector, so a pure minimisation is meaningless. Fix the scale in the metric that the energy respects, the degree:

    \[f = \arg\min_{g^\top D g = 1} g^\top L g \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad L f = \lambda D f \qquad (4)\]

    The constraint is \(f^\top D f = 1\) rather than \(\lVert f \rVert = 1\) because \(D\) is the natural inner product of this problem: it is the inner product in which \(L\) is self-adjoint, and it weights a vertex by how many edges it has. Using it is what makes the answer independent of duplicating a densely connected region of the graph. The stationary condition comes from the Lagrangian \(g^\top L g - \lambda (g^\top D g - 1)\), whose gradient is \(2Lg - 2\lambda Dg\).

    Minimising (4) returns the flat mode, which is useless. The useful coordinates are the next \(d\) eigenfunctions, and they are obtained by minimising the same quotient subject to being orthogonal, in the \(D\) inner product, to the modes already chosen — which is exactly what taking the next \(d\) eigenvalues of (4) does.

    4. The flat mode, and how many of them there are #

    Because every row of \(L\) sums to zero, \(L\mathbf{1} = 0\):

    \[L\,\mathbf{1} = (D - W)\mathbf{1} = D\mathbf{1} - D\mathbf{1} = 0 \qquad (5)\]

    so \(\lambda_1 = 0\) with eigenvector \(\mathbf{1}\), on any graph. This is the direction that (4) cannot see, and it is dropped for the same reason as in LLE: a cost built from differences is blind to an additive constant. Its multiplicity is the number of connected components, which turns the spectrum into a connectivity test — failure mode 1.

    5. The symmetric form #

    (4) is a generalized problem, and there is no generalized eigh in numpy. The substitution \(f = D^{-1/2}v\) removes \(D\) from both sides:

    \[L f = \lambda D f \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \left(I - D^{-1/2} W D^{-1/2}\right) v = \lambda v \;\Longleftrightarrow\; L_{\mathrm{sym}}\, v = \lambda v \qquad (6)\]

    and the embedding is recovered as \(Y = D^{-1/2} V\), one column per retained eigenvalue. \(L_{\mathrm{sym}}\) is symmetric, so one eigh solves it, the eigenvalues are real and the eigenvectors orthonormal in the ordinary sense. The round trip costs a diagonal scaling and buys the entire numerical stability of the implementation: the matrix being decomposed has norm at most 2, whatever the units of the data.

    6. The same thing as a Markov chain #

    Divide \(L f = \lambda D f\) by \(D\) and rearrange:

    \[P = D^{-1} W, \qquad P f = (1 - \lambda) f = \mu f \qquad (7)\]

    \(P\) is row-stochastic — it is the transition matrix of a random walk that steps from \(i\) to a neighbour with probability proportional to that edge's weight — so its eigenvalues lie in \([-1, 1]\) and \(\mu_1 = 1\). One step of the walk smooths a function by \(P\); \(s\) steps smooth it by \(P^s\), which has the same eigenvectors and eigenvalues \(\mu_k^s\). Written out,

    \[P^s = \sum_k \mu_k^s\, \psi_k \phi_k^\top, \qquad \psi_k = D^{-1/2} v_k, \qquad Y_k = \mu_k^s \psi_k \qquad (8)\]
    \[\lVert Y(i) - Y(j) \rVert^2 = \sum_k \mu_k^{2s}\,\bigl(\psi_k(i) - \psi_k(j)\bigr)^2 = D_s(i,j)^2 \qquad (9)\]

    so the \(s\)-step embedding is a distance — the diffusion distance, the random-walk analogue of geodesic distance and the quantity diffusion maps are named after. The truncation to \(d\) coordinates is what makes it an embedding rather than a table.

    7. What the spectrum converges to #

    The reason this is a manifold method and not just a graph method: with points sampled from an \(m\)-dimensional manifold and a Gaussian kernel of bandwidth \(t\),

    \[\frac{1}{n\,t^{m/2+2}}\,L \;\longrightarrow\; c\,\Delta_{\mathcal{M}} \qquad \text{as } n \to \infty,\; t \to 0,\; \frac{n t^{m/2+2}}{\log n} \to \infty \qquad (10)\]

    in the sense that the operator applied to a smooth function converges to a multiple of its Laplace–Beltrami operator, so the low eigenvectors converge to its low eigenfunctions. The constant \(c\) and the power of \(t\) come from the Taylor expansion of the kernel and are not worth memorising; the content is that the eigenvalue ordering is the ordering of the surface's own vibration modes. That is also why \(t\) must shrink as \(n\) grows — with a fixed kernel the limit is a different operator — and why the flat mode of the graph is the constant function of the manifold.

    Code walkthrough #

    1. The graph #

        np.fill_diagonal(D, np.inf)
    idx = np.argpartition(D, k, axis=1)[:, :k]
    dist = np.take_along_axis(D, idx, axis=1)
    order = np.argsort(dist, axis=1)

    argpartition finds the \(k\) smallest entries of each row without ordering the rest, and the argsort then orders only those \(k\), so distances_[:, 0] is the nearest neighbour. The diagonal is set to inf first, which is the entire mechanism that stops a point from being its own neighbour; the dense distance matrix is what makes this affordable and what makes the method \(O(n^2)\) in memory.

    2. Scattering the edges #

        rows = np.repeat(np.arange(n), k)
    vals = (np.ones(rows.size) if heat is None
            else np.exp(-dist.ravel() ** 2 / heat))
    W[rows, idx.ravel()] = vals
    W = np.maximum(W, W.T)

    W[rows, idx.ravel()] is a single fancy-index assignment that writes all \(nk\) edges at once — no Python loop over vertices anywhere in the graph construction. Then np.maximum(W, W.T) symmetrises by union: an edge that only one endpoint chose appears with that endpoint's weight, and an edge both chose appears once with the same weight either way. The alternative, np.minimum, keeps only mutual neighbours; it bounds the degree in both directions and disconnects more easily.

    3. The bandwidth default #

            heat = None if self.variant == "standard" else self.t
        if heat is None and self.variant != "standard":
            heat = float(np.mean(dist[:, -1] ** 2))

    The default is the mean squared distance to the \(k\)-th neighbour: the scale at which the graph is a graph and not a set of isolated points. It is a mean rather than a minimum so that a handful of very close pairs cannot set the scale, and a mean rather than a maximum so that one outlier cannot either. standard passes None through to the scatter and gets ones, which is the \(t \to \infty\) limit of the heat kernel from the variants section.

    4. One eigh #

            S = inv[:, None] * W * inv[None, :]
        mu, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(S)
        order = np.argsort(mu)[::-1]
        mu = mu[order]
        vecs = vecs[:, order]

    This is (6) and (7) at once: S is \(D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}\), whose eigenvalues are \(\mu = 1 - \lambda\), so sorting \(\mu\) descending is sorting the Laplacian ascending and the flat mode comes first. eigh returns ascending order, hence the reversal and the explicit argsort — the reversal alone would be enough for eigh, but the sort is written out because the rest of the class treats mu as an ordered spectrum.

            keep = slice(1, 1 + self.n_components)
        coords = vecs[:, keep] * inv[:, None]
        if self.variant == "diffusion_link":
            coords = coords * (mu[keep] ** self.n_steps)[None, :]

    The slice is the drop-the-flat-mode rule of section 4, inv[:, None] is the \(D^{-1/2}\) of (6), and the last two lines are the entire difference between normalized and diffusion_link. Note what is not here: no rescaling to unit variance, no whitening, no sign convention. The generalized problem fixes the scale through \(f^\top D f = 1\), and leaving it alone is what makes the coordinates comparable between two fits on the same graph.

    5. The one guard #

            deg = W.sum(axis=1)
        if np.any(deg <= 0.0):
            raise ValueError("every vertex needs at least one neighbour")

    A vertex with no edges has degree zero, so \(D^{-1/2}\) is infinite and the whole matrix is not a matrix. This happens when n_neighbors is small relative to the spread of the data and a point is isolated, and it is worth failing loudly rather than producing nan downstream. The test is against exactly zero rather than a tolerance: the heat kernel is allowed to produce weights of \(10^{-80}\) (the bandwidth demo does), and a \(10^{-12}\) threshold would refuse a graph that is perfectly well defined.

    Complexity #

    stage work implementation
    distances \(O(n^2 d)\) one Gram matrix, sqrt of the clipped complement
    neighbours \(O(n^2)\) argpartition per row
    graph \(O(nk)\) one fancy-index scatter, np.maximum
    normalization \(O(n^2)\) two diagonal scalings of a dense matrix
    eigenproblem \(O(n^3)\) time, \(O(n^2)\) memory dense eigh

    Measured on the S-curve with normalized and \(k = 10\), one fit:

    \(n\) 250 500 1000 2000 4000
    time 0.006 s 0.023 s 0.110 s 0.681 s 8.99 s

    At \(n = 2000\) the breakdown is \(0.026\) s for the distances, \(0.030\) s for the neighbours, \(0.010\) s to assemble \(W\), \(0.007\) s to normalize, and \(0.618\) s for eigh — 91% of the run in the eigen-solver. Doubling from 1000 to 2000 costs \(6.2\times\) and doubling again \(13.2\times\), the signature of a dense cubic with a memory hierarchy on top.

    The comparison that matters is with LLE at the same size: \(0.735\) s against \(0.681\) s at \(n = 2000\), and \(9.38\) s against \(8.99\) s at \(n = 4000\). Almost all of that difference is the per-neighbourhood solve LLE needs and this method does not; both then pay the same cubic. A production implementation replaces eigh with a shift-invert Lanczos solver for the few smallest eigenvectors of a sparse \(L\), which is \(O(nk)\) per iteration and never forms the dense matrix at all. This file pays \(O(n^3)\) to stay inside numpy, and the dense matrix is also what makes the full spectrum available to inspect — which is the diagnostic the failure modes depend on.

    Failure modes #

    1. A graph that does not connect #

    Two clouds of 100 points twelve units apart, \(k = 8\): every neighbourhood lies inside one cloud, the Laplacian is block diagonal, and it has 2 eigenvalues at zero — one per component. The first coordinate is the indicator of the two clouds, constant within each to \(9 \times 10^{-17}\). The method has produced a perfect embedding of a graph that cannot see across the gap, and the objective agrees that it is perfect, because it is.

    Worse, the spectrum is the only thing that reports it. On the 300-point S-curve, \(k = 4\) tears the graph along the fold, \(\lambda_2\) is exactly \(0\), and both of the usual quality scores improve: \(0.621\) at eight neighbours and \(0.879\) distance correlation, against \(0.510\) and \(0.837\) at \(k = 10\). The indicator of the two halves happens to be close to the sign of the sheet's own first parameter, so the collapse is rewarded by the metric that was supposed to catch it. Count the eigenvalues below tolerance and compare with the number of components before believing any score.

    2. A bandwidth that underflows #

    The heat kernel is exponential in \(-d^2/t\), so a small \(t\) does not merely down-weight distant pairs, it annihilates them. At \(0.02\times\) the default scale on the S-curve the surviving weights span \(2 \times 10^{-80}\) to \(0.96\) and seven eigenvalues sit at \(10^{-10}\) or below where the graph wants one. The layout is the indicator of seven near-isolated clumps: overlap at eight neighbours still reads \(0.624\), but distance correlation collapses to \(0.318\). Local neighbours survive a clumped layout; global structure does not.

    The safe direction is the other one. Large \(t\) flattens the kernel to ones, the graph becomes unweighted, and \(\lambda_2\) converges to the standard value. A bandwidth that is too large costs resolution, not correctness — but note that it also loses the kernel's density adaptation entirely, and that a fixed t is not scale-free: it has units, so the same number on data in different units is a different graph.

    3. Degenerate eigenvalues, and the gauge that remains #

    On the cycle the eigenvalues arrive in exact pairs, and the eigenvectors inside a pair are only determined up to a rotation: any orthonormal basis of the two-dimensional eigenspace is equally valid. The gap measured on the demo's circle is \(4 \times 10^{-16}\), so which rotation you get is a property of LAPACK, not of the data.

    This is a much smaller gauge group than LLE's. There the eigenproblem fixed only a subspace and the coordinates were related by an arbitrary invertible matrix; here the problem fixes the coordinates up to an orthogonal rotation within each degenerate eigenspace, and up to a sign. Distance-based comparisons are invariant under both; coordinate-by-coordinate comparisons are not, and on symmetric data they are meaningless. If you need stable coordinates, fix a sign convention and rotate each degenerate block by a deterministic rule — or accept that the layout is an object with a symmetry.

    4. \(k\) is a graph decision, and more is not safer #

    On the S-curve at \(k = 4, 6, 8, 10, 15, 20, 30, 50\) the eight-neighbour overlap is \(0.621\), \(0.599\), \(0.547\), \(0.510\), \(0.465\), \(0.443\), \(0.435\), \(0.388\) and the distance correlation \(0.879\), \(0.834\), \(0.822\), \(0.837\), \(0.843\), \(0.841\), \(0.835\), \(0.817\). Both curves are flat and then fall; neither has an interior optimum worth finding. And the best row in the table is the disconnected one. There is no cross-validation-style answer here — \(k\) is the resolution at which you have declared two points to be related, and the honest move is to report it and check the spectrum.

    5. No out-of-sample extension #

    The eigenvectors are defined on vertices. A new point has no row in \(L\) and therefore no coordinate, and neither fit nor transform will invent one. The usual patches are Nyström extension (interpolate the eigenvectors to the new point from its neighbours) or a regression from \(X\) to \(Y\); both are approximations that the method itself does not contain. If new data arrives continuously, this is the wrong atom and a parametric method is the right one.

    6. Sampling density is not geometry #

    If the sample is denser in one region than another, the unnormalized Laplacian's low modes can describe the sampling rather than the surface: a region with more points has larger degrees, and the generalized problem weights it more heavily. This is the standard argument for the normalized Laplacians, whose eigenvectors are invariant to a non-uniform density in the limit, and it is a reason to prefer normalized over standard on data that is not uniformly sampled. Neither is a complete correction — the fully density-corrected form is the \(lpha\)-normalisation used in diffusion maps, which is outside this file. What is not a density correction is the heat kernel itself: changing \(t\) changes the kernel's absolute scale, which is a different axis of sensitivity (failure mode 2).

    Applications #

    • Spectral clustering. Ng, Jordan and Weiss build exactly the normalized embedding of this file, normalize each row to unit length, and run \(k\)-means on the result. The reason it works is the one the intuition section describes: for well-separated clusters the low modes are close to indicator functions, and \(k\)-means on indicators is trivial. 07-clustering/spectral_clustering.py ships this embedding as its ng_jordan_weiss variant, with the clustering attached.
    • Semi-supervised learning. Clamp the labels on a few vertices and extend them by the minimiser of \(f^\top L f\) subject to the clamped values — a harmonic function on the graph, solved by the same linear system the eigenproblem comes from. Zhu, Ghahramani and Lafferty's label propagation is this method with the labels kept in the boundary condition.
    • Manifold learning of images. Belkin and Niyogi's own demonstration: a set of photographs of one object, embedded by the graph spectrum, with the low coordinates tracking pose. The advantage over PCA there is the same as here — the graph respects nearness in image space, which need not be linear.
    • Shape and mesh analysis. On a triangulated surface, the Laplace–Beltrami eigenfunctions computed from the mesh are the same objects, and their low eigenvalues are used as shape descriptors and for correspondence between meshes. The circle demo is the one-dimensional case where the answer is a Fourier basis.
    • Graph drawing and relational data. When the input is a graph rather than points, this is one of the few methods that applies unchanged: build \(W\) from the adjacency and take the spectrum. Force-directed layouts and spectral layouts are the two standard answers, and this is the second.
    • As an ancestor. Diffusion maps, PHATE and the Laplacian eigenmaps inside spectral clustering are all this eigenproblem with a different operator or a different scaling. The diffusion_link variant is the one-line version of that step; diffusion_maps and phate later in this chapter take the rest.
