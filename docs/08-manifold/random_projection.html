    08 · Manifolds & embeddings/tier 1/4 variants/source

    Random projection

    Intuition #

    Every other method in this chapter learns something from the data: neighbours, eigenvectors, weights, an autoencoder. Random projection learns nothing. It multiplies the data by a matrix of random numbers and keeps the result.

    The reason that works is a theorem with no assumptions about the data at all. Johnson and Lindenstrauss (1984) proved that for any \(n\) points in any Euclidean space, a projection onto

    \[k \;=\; O\!\left(\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^2}\right) \qquad (1)\]

    random dimensions preserves all pairwise distances within a factor \(1\pm \varepsilon\) with high probability. The bound does not contain the input dimension. A dataset in \(10^6\) dimensions and a dataset in \(10\) need the same number of random coordinates to keep their geometry, because the guarantee is about \(n\) points, and \(n\) points have at most \(n(n-1)/2\) distances to preserve.

       200 points, 2000 dimensions          200 points, 64 dimensions
   ┌──────────────────────────┐         ┌────────────┐
   │  ·    ·        ·    ·    │         │  ·   ·     │
   │      ·    ·  ·           │   ──▶   │    ·   ·   │   distances within 1±ε
   │   ·       ·       ·   ·  │  R x    │ ·    ·     │   and nothing else
   └──────────────────────────┘         └────────────┘

    It is worth being precise about what is not promised. Distances are preserved; directions, clusters, densities, margins and nearest-neighbour identities are not. The projection cannot exploit structure, so on data that is genuinely low-rank it is beaten by the directions that structure defines: the demo's 300 points lie exactly in a 10-dimensional subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^{800}\), and ten principal directions reconstruct them to \(1.9\times10^{-14}\) while ten random directions distort distances by \(0.776\).

    What the random projection buys is speed, and a property that no learned method has: it is data-oblivious. The matrix can be drawn before the data exists, so the projection can be applied at the point of collection, inside a database, or before a privacy budget is spent — and the sketch of a dataset can be computed by sketching its parts and adding.

    Variants #

    Variant Entries of \(R\) Non-zero fraction Multiply cost
    gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1/k)\) 1 \(O(ndk)\)
    achlioptas \(\pm 1/\sqrt{k}\) 1 \(O(ndk)\), no RNG for values
    sparse \(\pm 1/\sqrt{sk}\), prob. \(s = 1/3\) \(1/3\) \(O(ndk/3)\)
    very_sparse \(\pm 1/\sqrt{sk}\), prob. \(s = 1/\sqrt{d}\) \(1/\sqrt{d}\) \(O(nk\sqrt{d})\)

    gaussian is the textbook construction and the one the proofs are usually written for: i.i.d. normal entries scaled by \(1/\sqrt{k}\). The demo projects 400 points from 1000 to 64 dimensions and the worst pairwise distortion is \(0.396\).

    achlioptas replaces the normal draws with Rademacher signs, \(\pm 1/\sqrt{k}\). The rows already have norm exactly 1, the analysis goes through the same concentration inequalities, and generating \(\pm1\) is cheaper than generating normals. Its measured distortion is \(0.375\) — indistinguishable from Gaussian at this size, which is the point.

    sparse sets each entry to zero with probability \(2/3\) and to \(\pm 1/\sqrt{sk}\) otherwise, with \(s = 1/3\) (Achlioptas 2003). The row norm is still 1 in expectation, so the guarantee is unchanged, but two thirds of the matrix is gone: the multiply becomes several scatter-adds instead of one dense product, and on sparse input it touches only the non-zeros.

    very_sparse pushes the density down to \(1/\sqrt{d}\) (Li, Hastie & Church 2006). At \(d = 20000\) that is \(0.7\%\) non-zero and the demo's \(128\times20000\) matrix holds 17,945 non-zeros against 2.56 million for the Gaussian one — a factor 143 fewer entries to touch. The price is a heavier-tailed distribution, so the constant in the concentration bound is worse and the same \(k\) gives slightly more distortion (\(0.438\) against \(0.396\) in the demo). At large \(d\) that is a trade worth making; at small \(d\) it is not.

    All four are the same three lines of code with a different distribution. The one thing they must share is the scaling: the entries' variance has to be \(1/k\), not "about right", and §2 shows exactly what goes wrong otherwise.

    Derivation #

    1. What has to be true for a random matrix to preserve norms #

    Let \(R\) be \(k\times d\) with i.i.d. entries of mean zero, and consider one vector \(x\in\mathbb{R}^d\). Then

    \[\mathbb{E}\lVert Rx\rVert^2 \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[x^{\mathsf T}R^{\mathsf T}Rx\big] \;=\; x^{\mathsf T}\big(k\,\mathbb{E}[rr^{\mathsf T}]\big)x, \qquad (2)\]

    where \(r\) is a row of \(R\). So the projection is unbiased — preserves squared norms in expectation — exactly when

    \[\mathbb{E}[rr^{\mathsf T}] \;=\; \frac{1}{k}I, \qquad (3)\]

    that is, when each entry has variance \(1/k\) and the entries are uncorrelated. Every distribution in the table is a way of satisfying (3) with a different amount of work.

    The variance requirement is where sparse constructions get subtle, and where this file had a bug for one run. If a fraction \(s\) of the entries is non-zero and each non-zero is \(\pm a\), then the variance is \(s a^2\), so (3) demands

    \[a \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{sk}}, \qquad (4)\]

    not \(\sqrt{s/k}\). The wrong scaling inflates every row's norm by \(1/\sqrt{s}\), which at \(s = 1/3\) is a factor \(1.7\): the demo's first attempt measured distortions of \(0.78\) and \(0.98\) for the two sparse variants against \(0.40\) and \(0.38\) for the dense ones, and it was the assertion — not the eye — that caught it.

    2. Concentration #

    Unbiased is not enough; the actual norm has to be close to its expectation for every point. For Gaussian entries, \(k\lVert Rx\rVert^2/\lVert x\rVert^2\) is \(\chi^2_k\), and the standard tail bound gives, for \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\),

    \[\mathbb{P}\Big(\big|\,\lVert Rx\rVert^2/\lVert x\rVert^2 - 1\,\big| > \varepsilon\Big) \;\le\; 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{k(\varepsilon^2-\varepsilon^3)}{4}\right). \qquad (5)\]

    Rademacher and sparse entries have the same shape of bound with worse constants, which is why they behave identically in the demo.

    For a pair of points, apply (5) to \(x_i - x_j\): the difference of two projections is the projection of the difference, so distance preservation is norm preservation of the difference vector. Preserving all \(\binom{n}{2}\) pairs at once is a union bound:

    \[\mathbb{P}\big(\text{some pair distorted}\big) \;\le\; n^2 \cdot 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{k(\varepsilon^2-\varepsilon^3)}{4}\right) \;\le\; \delta \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; k \;\ge\; \frac{4\ln(2n^2/\delta)}{\varepsilon^2-\varepsilon^3}. \qquad (6)\]

    Equation (6) is the lemma in the form you actually use: fix \(n\), \(\varepsilon\) and a failure probability \(\delta\), and read off \(k\). It depends on \(\ln n\), which is why the number of dimensions needed is so small and why it grows so slowly.

    3. The distortion the demo measures #

    Everything above is about squared norms; the demo reports the worst relative change in a distance,

    \[\Delta \;=\; \max_{i

    which is the quantity a downstream algorithm actually sees. On 200 points in 2000 dimensions it reads \(0.976\) at \(k=8\), \(0.473\) at \(k=32\), \(0.262\) at \(k=128\) and \(0.112\) at \(k=512\): each four-fold increase in \(k\) halves the worst distortion, which is the \(\sqrt{1/k}\) rate that (5) implies. The typical distortion at \(k\) is about \(\sqrt{2/k}\) — \(0.18\) at \(k = 64\) — and the maximum over 20,000 pairs lands at roughly twice that. The union bound of (6) is much more pessimistic: with \(n = 200\), \(\delta = 0.05\) and \(\varepsilon = 0.5\) it asks for \(k \approx 460\). The lemma is a guarantee, not a prediction, and at these sizes the measured distortion is far better than the guarantee requires.

    4. The bound has no \(d\) in it #

    The demo's sharpest check is to multiply the input dimension by ten and change nothing else. At \(k = 64\) the distortion goes from \(0.346\) at \(d = 2000\) to \(0.391\) at \(d = 20000\) — the same number, twice measured with different noise. The ambient dimension enters only through the arithmetic of the multiply; it does not enter the guarantee.

    That is counter-intuitive enough to be worth restating: a random projection from \(10^6\) dimensions to \(10^3\) preserves the distances between \(10^6\) points, because there are only \(10^6\) of them and each distance is a one-dimensional quantity.

    5. Why it loses to PCA on low-rank data #

    Suppose the data lies in an \(r\)-dimensional subspace, \(r \ll d\). PCA chooses that subspace and needs exactly \(k = r\) coordinates to represent it with no error at all. A random \(k\)-dimensional projection cannot choose, and — this is the point that is easy to get wrong — it does not systematically lose the subspace either. For \(x\) inside the subspace, the projected norm is still unbiased,

    \[\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\lVert Rx\rVert^2}{\lVert x\rVert^2}\right] = 1, \qquad \mathrm{Var}\left[\frac{\lVert Rx\rVert^2}{\lVert x\rVert^2}\right] = \frac{2}{k}, \qquad (8)\]

    whatever \(r\) is, because \(R\) mixes the \(r\) coordinates rather than discarding them. The difference is not bias, it is exactness: PCA's error is identically zero once \(k \ge r\), while a random projection's relative error stays of order \(\sqrt{2/k}\) no matter how simple the data is. The demo's numbers are the measured version: at \(k = r = 10\), random directions give \(0.776\) and the principal directions give \(1.9\times10^{-14}\).

    So the comparison is not "random projection is worse". It is: random projection is \(O(ndk)\) and data-oblivious; PCA is \(O(nd\min(n,d))\), needs the data, and is exact on low-rank data. Which is cheaper depends on whether the data has exploitable structure, whether \(k\) can be as small as \(r\), and whether you are allowed to look at the data before you project it.

    6. What the sparse constructions cost #

    A dense \(k\times d\) projection of an \(n\times d\) matrix is \(ndk\) multiply-adds. A sparse one with density \(s\) is \(ndks\) multiply-adds but the same number of memory touches per non-zero, so the win is proportional to \(1/s\) only when the data is dense; on sparse data the touches are proportional to the data's non-zeros.

    The demo's \(128\times20000\) example makes the scale concrete: 2.56 million non-zeros for Gaussian, 853,215 for sparse, 17,945 for very sparse. The dense multiply is \(5.1\) billion multiply-adds; the very sparse one touches 36 million entries — a factor 143 fewer. That is what "cheaper" means here, and it is why feature hashing (which is a random projection with a fixed sparse structure) is the default in large-scale text pipelines.

    7. What is preserved, and what is not #

    Distances are preserved, and by the polarisation identity so are inner products:

    \[\langle Rx, Ry\rangle \;=\; \tfrac14\big(\lVert R(x+y)\rVert^2 - \lVert R(x-y)\rVert^2\big), \qquad (9)\]

    so a bound on norms gives a bound on inner products with the same \(\varepsilon\) up to constants. Norms are preserved, hence so is the norm of the centroid, but the identity of the nearest neighbour is not: two points at nearly equal distance from a query can swap order under any perturbation of size \(\varepsilon\), and a random projection is a perturbation of exactly that size. Clustering structure, margins, and density estimates all degrade faster than distances do.

    Code walkthrough #

    RandomProjection(n_components=50, *, variant="gaussian", density=None, seed=0) with fit(X), transform(X), fit_transform(X) and the attribute components_ (n_components, d). The only thing fit uses from X is its number of columns — which is the point.

    1. Two dense distributions, and the scaling that matters #

            if self.variant == "gaussian":
            return rng.standard_normal((k, d)) / np.sqrt(k)
        if self.variant == "achlioptas":
            return rng.choice([-1.0, 1.0], size=(k, d)) / np.sqrt(k)

    (3). Both divide by \(\sqrt{k}\), which sets the entry variance to \(1/k\); the Rademacher version's rows then have norm exactly 1, not just in expectation, because every entry is \(\pm 1/\sqrt{k}\). Dropping the division is the classic mistake: the projection still preserves distances up to a global scale factor, which looks like a successful embedding and is wrong by \(\sqrt{k}\) — the demo's first assertion would pass on ranks and fail on values.

    2. The sparse construction, and the variance #

            s = self._density(d)
        # variance must be 1/k, so a non-zero of density s has magnitude
        # 1/√(sk); using √(s/k) inflates the row norm by 1/√s
        values = rng.choice([-1.0, 1.0], size=(k, d)) * np.sqrt(1.0 / (s * k))
        mask = rng.random((k, d)) < s
        return values * mask

    (4). Generating the signs and the mask separately and multiplying is clearer than a three-way np.where, and it makes the two independent random draws explicit. The comment is there because this file shipped the wrong line first: with \(\sqrt{s/k}\) the very sparse variant distorted distances by \(0.98\), which still looks like a projection and is not one. density is exposed so a caller can tune \(s\); the defaults are the ones from the papers.

    3. Fit uses only the shape #

            X = np.asarray(X, dtype=float)
        if X.ndim != 2:
            raise ValueError(f"X must be (samples, features), got shape {X.shape}")
        if self.n_components > X.shape[1]:
            raise ValueError("n_components cannot exceed the input dimension")
        self.components_ = self._draw(X.shape[1])
        self.n_features_in_ = X.shape[1]
        return self

    This is the only fit in the chapter that does not look at the values, and the guard on n_components is the one that matters: projecting up is a valid matrix product and a meaningless operation, so it raises instead of returning a higher-dimensional embedding that looks like it did something.

    4. The projection, and the transpose #

            X = np.asarray(X, dtype=float)
        if X.shape[1] != self.n_features_in_:
            raise ValueError(f"expected {self.n_features_in_} features, got {X.shape[1]}")
        return X @ self.components_.T

    components_ is (k, d) so that the matrix reads as \(k\) rows of \(d\) coefficients — the natural orientation for inspecting a projection direction — and the transform therefore needs the transpose. Storing it as (d, k) and writing X @ components_ is the same computation and one fewer mental step, at the cost of a matrix whose rows are the features of the projection, which is the wrong thing to look at.

    5. Distances without an \((n,n,d)\) array #

        sq = np.einsum("ij,ij->i", X, X)
    d2 = np.maximum(sq[:, None] + sq[None, :] - 2.0 * X @ X.T, 0.0)
    return np.sqrt(d2)

    (7) needs distances, and the obvious implementation — X[:, None, :] - X[None, :, :] — allocates \(n^2d\) floats. At the demo's \(400\times400\times1000\) that is 1.3 GB for a scalar. The Gram identity \(\lVert x_i-x_j\rVert^2 = \lVert x_i\rVert^2 + \lVert x_j\rVert^2 - 2x_i^{\mathsf T}x_j\) is \(O(n^2)\) memory and \(O(n^2d)\) time, and the maximum(..., 0.0) is not cosmetic: the identity is exact in reals and can go negative in floating point for nearly identical points, and sqrt of a small negative is nan.

    6. The comparison that keeps the atom honest #

        rp = RandomProjection(10, variant="gaussian", seed=7)
    rp_dist = _distortion(rp.fit_transform(X), X)

    centred = X - X.mean(axis=0)
    _, _, vt = np.linalg.svd(centred, full_matrices=False)
    pca = centred @ vt[:10].T
    pca_dist = _distortion(pca, centred)

    The demo would be marketing without this section: on data with an exact 10-dimensional structure, the random projection is \(0.776\) and PCA is \(1.9\times10^{-14}\). The SVD is used here as a reference implementation, not as part of the atom — it is the cheapest honest way to show what an informed choice of ten directions achieves, and it makes the case for random projection narrower than "it works": it works when there is nothing to learn, or when learning is too expensive.

    Complexity #

    \(n\) samples, \(d\) input dimensions, \(k\) output dimensions, density \(s\) of the projection matrix.

    Operation Time Memory Note
    draw components_ \(O(kd)\) \(O(kd)\) once, independent of \(n\)
    gaussian / achlioptas \(O(ndk)\) \(O(nk)\) one dense product
    sparse \(O(ndks)\) \(O(nk)\) \(s = 1/3\) by default
    very_sparse \(O(nk\sqrt{d})\) \(O(nk)\) \(s = 1/\sqrt d\)
    _distortion (demo) \(O(n^2d)\) \(O(n^2)\) the Gram identity
    PCA, for comparison \(O(nd\min(n,d))\) \(O(nd)\) and it needs the data

    Three numbers decide whether to use this. The first is \(k\) from (6): for \(n = 10^6\), \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), \(\delta = 0.05\), the bound gives \(k \approx 4\ln(2\times10^{12}/0.05)/0.009 \approx 14{,}000\) — large, and still an order of magnitude below the vocabulary a bag-of-words model would carry. The second is the \(O(ndk)\) product, which is linear in everything and therefore the cheapest reduction in this chapter. The third is that the projection matrix is \(kd\) numbers: at \(k = 10^3\) and \(d = 10^6\) that is \(10^9\) entries, which is where the very sparse construction stops being an optimisation and becomes the only option that fits.

    Failure modes #

    1. The scaling is wrong and everything still "works". Using \(\sqrt{s/k}\) for a sparse projection inflates row norms by \(1/\sqrt{s}\); the embedding is still a linear map, still rank \(k\), still looks like a cloud. The demo's first run measured \(0.98\) against \(0.40\) and the assertion caught it. Check (3): entry variance exactly \(1/k\).

    2. \(k\) chosen by the input dimension. The guarantee depends on \(n\), not \(d\), so "keep 10% of the features" is not a rule. At \(n = 10^3\) and \(k = 64\) the distortion is already \(0.35\); at \(n = 10^6\) it needs thousands of dimensions for \(\varepsilon = 0.1\). Compute \(k\) from (6) and the tolerance you need.

    3. Random projection is not a substitute for PCA on low-rank data. \(0.776\) against \(1.9\times10^{-14}\) at the same \(k\), measured, and the gap is structural: a random projection is unbiased but it cannot be exact. If a covariance pass is affordable, PCA (or a randomised SVD) is exact as soon as \(k\) reaches the data's rank, and it dominates the sketch once \(k\) is large enough to matter.

    4. Distances preserved does not mean structure preserved. Nearest-neighbour identity, cluster membership, margins and density estimates all shift under an \(\varepsilon\) perturbation, and the pairs most likely to flip are exactly the ones a classifier cares about — the close ones. Test the downstream task, not the distance distortion.

    5. The projection is oblivious to the future. A sketch of a dataset and a sketch of its new points are addable only if they use the same \(R\), which means \(k\) must be chosen for the eventual \(n\), not today's. Re-drawing \(R\) per batch produces embeddings that cannot be concatenated, and the error looks like a modelling failure rather than a bookkeeping one.

    6. Streaming order matters for nothing, and for everything. Projection itself is order-free — that is the appeal — but the centring is not: if you centre before projecting you need the global mean, and if you centre after you must remember that centring the projected data is equivalent to projecting the centred data. Mixing the two gives a systematic offset in the sketch.

    7. Very sparse has heavier tails. The same \(k\) gives more distortion (\(0.438\) against \(0.396\) in the demo) because the entries' fourth moment is larger. At large \(d\) the cost win dominates; at small \(d\) the very sparse construction is strictly worse and the "sparse" default is the right one.

    8. Do not interpret the coordinates. A random projection's axes are random directions; a loading has no meaning. That is fine for distances and features fed to a model, and fatal for a plot with labelled axes. If you need interpretable dimensions, you wanted PCA, NMF or a topic model.

    Applications #

    • Feature hashing. A random projection with a fixed, sparse, hash-defined structure: text features are mapped to a fixed number of buckets before training. The demo's sparse variants are the same idea with a drawn matrix instead of a hash, and the reason it is standard at web scale is the \(O(nk\sqrt d)\) cost.
    • Sketching for linear algebra. Count-sketch and sparse Johnson-Lindenstrauss transforms are the sampling step inside randomised least-squares and low-rank-approximation algorithms; the same bound (6) is what makes their error guarantees unconditional.
    • Privacy and federated settings. The projection is data-independent, so it can be applied before the data reaches a server and before a differential privacy budget is spent; the sketch is not invertible in any practical sense, and the geometry survives.
    • Approximate nearest neighbours. Random projection trees, and the LSH family built from random hyperplanes, use the same distance-preservation argument to bucket points so that near neighbours collide with high probability.
    • Compression before clustering. k-means on a sketch costs \(O(ndk)\) to build and \(O(nk_{\text{centres}}k)\) per iteration instead of \(O(ndk_{\text{centres}})\); for large \(d\) this is a large constant-factor win with a bounded distortion of the objective.
    • Sanity checks and baselines. Because it learns nothing, a random projection is the right baseline for a learned embedding: if a trained autoencoder cannot beat it on the downstream task, the training is not buying structure.

    Where it is a trap: choosing \(k\) from \(d\); comparing coordinates across runs; using it on low-rank data where PCA is exact; assuming nearest neighbours are preserved; and forgetting that the failure probability in (6) is a union bound over \(n^2\) pairs, so it degrades quadratically with dataset size unless \(k\) grows logarithmically.

    References #

    • Johnson & Lindenstrauss (1984), Extensions of Lipschitz mappings into a Hilbert space — the original lemma, in a paper about fixed points, with a constant far worse than anything used today.
    • Frankl & Maehara (1988), The Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma and the sphericity of some convex bodies — the geometric form and a much better constant, which is where the \(4\ln(2n^2/\delta)/\varepsilon^2\) shape of (6) comes from.
    • Dasgupta & Gupta (2003), An elementary proof of a theorem of Johnson and Lindenstrauss — the proof by random rotations, and the source of the \(\chi^2\) tail bound in (5).
    • Achlioptas (2003), Database-friendly random projections — the \(\pm1\) and sparse constructions of the achlioptas and sparse variants, including the variance argument of (4).
    • Li, Hastie & Church (2006), Very sparse random projections — the \(1/\sqrt d\) density of very_sparse, with the cost analysis that makes it the default at scale.
    • Indyk & Motwani (1998), Approximate nearest neighbors: towards removing the curse of dimensionality — the locality-sensitive-hashing use of the lemma, and the reason random projections are in every ANN library.
    • Bingham & Mannila (2001), Random projection in dimensionality reduction — the empirical study that made the method standard in data mining, including the comparison against PCA that §5 formalises.
    • Vempala (2004), The Random Projection Method — the book-length treatment, with the linear-algebra applications (sketching, regression) that the bound underpins.
    • Kane & Nelson (2014), Sparser Johnson-Lindenstrauss transforms — the sharp sparse construction, with the \(O(\log n)\) non-zeros per column that (4) only approximates.
    • Larsen & Nelson (2017), Optimality of the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma — the matching lower bound: no distribution and no non-linear map does better than \(k = \Theta(\log n/\varepsilon^2)\), which is what makes (1) tight rather than merely sufficient.
