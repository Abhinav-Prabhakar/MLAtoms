    08 · Manifolds & embeddings/tier 2/4 variants/source

    PaCMAP

    Intuition #

    Every neighbour-embedding method is an argument about which pairs of points the layout is allowed to care about. t-SNE cares about the k-nearest graph; UMAP cares about the same graph and about every other pair, through a sampled complement. PaCMAP says the argument is the other way round: the graph is too narrow to carry global structure, and "every non-edge" is too coarse to carry anything. It splits the pairs into three sets and gives each one its own weight and its own weight at each moment of the run.

            neighbour ring        mid-near ring         further pairs
        k ≈ 10, d ≈ 0.44      1/3 quantile, 1.54    uniform, 2.18
          ·  ·  ·                 ·                     ·
        ·  ·█·  ·  ·          ·       ·          ·            ·
          ·  ·  ·                 ·                     ·
        ·         ·           ·        ·        ·                  ·

        attractive,           attractive,           repulsive,
        saturates at 3        ~ a spring           short-ranged

    The three rings are what the demo measures: on a 250-point swiss roll the mean pair distance inside each set is \(0.44\), \(1.54\) and \(2.18\), against \(2.12\) for a pair drawn at random. Only the first is a neighbourhood; only the third is global; the middle one is the scale that neither t-SNE nor UMAP ever samples.

    The schedule is the part that people skip. The weights are not a fixed triple with a large number in it — they are a curriculum:

    \[(w_{\mathrm{NP}}, w_{\mathrm{MN}}, w_{\mathrm{FP}}) = (2,\;1000 \to 3,\;1) \;\to\; (3,\;3,\;1) \;\to\; (1,\;0,\;1) \qquad (1)\]

    Phase one puts a weight of a thousand on the mid-near pairs, so the layout arranges regions before it arranges points. Phase two brings the neighbour weight up and lets the mid-near pairs keep the regions apart while the neighbour term fixes local placement. Phase three drops the mid-near term to zero and leaves the neighbour and further pairs at equal weight, which is where the finest structure is set — at the cost of the distances.

    That trade is measurable at every step of the demo's run:

    step phase \(w\) std (mean of axes) overlap @8 overlap @40 distance corr.
    0 1 (2, 1000, 1) 1.01 0.458 0.620 0.090
    49 1 (2, 511, 1) 1.60 0.607 0.760 0.302
    99 1 (2, 12.97, 1) 2.73 0.719 0.827 0.407
    199 2 (3, 3, 1) 3.67 0.718 0.861 0.910
    449 3 (1, 0, 1) 7.54 0.773 0.852 0.799

    Read the columns. Phase one builds the mid range: forty-neighbour overlap goes from \(0.620\) to \(0.827\) while the distances are still wrong. Phase two is the one that fixes distances: correlation \(0.407 \to 0.910\) with local overlap flat. Phase three buys \(0.055\) of eight-neighbour overlap for \(0.111\) of distance correlation. The last phase is a refinement that is paid for.

    The result against the chapter's other methods, on the same roll and the same truth:

    method overlap @8 overlap @40 distance corr.
    PCA (2 components) 0.489 — 0.280
    t-SNE (perplexity 30) 0.857 0.769 0.335
    UMAP (\(k = 15\)) 0.833 0.834 0.534
    Isomap — — 0.9996
    PaCMAP (default) 0.773 0.852 0.799
    PaCMAP, fp_ratio=1.0 0.719 0.849 0.969

    PaCMAP is not the best at the finest scale — t-SNE wins that and UMAP is close. It is the best of the three at the middle scale and, by a wide margin, at distances; and unlike every other method in the table its global fidelity is a knob, not a consequence of the hyperparameter you happened to pick.

    Variants #

    Variant The pair sets What changes Cost per step
    pacmap NP, MN, uniform FP the reference \(n k (1 + m + f)\) pairs
    knn_graph NP, uniform FP no mid-near pairs \(n k (1 + f)\)
    localmap NP, MN, two-hop FP in the last phase local computation confined to phase 3 same as pacmap
    supervised same-class NP, class-crossing FP labels decide which pairs exist \(n k (1 + m + f)\)

    pacmap is (1) at the defaults: \(k = 10\), mn_ratio \(= 0.5\), fp_ratio \(= 2.0\), \(450\) steps. At \(n = 250\) that is \(2500\) neighbour pairs, \(1250\) mid-near pairs and \(5000\) further pairs — \(8750\) terms per step, and the loss falls from \(2205\) at the first checkpoint to \(530.5\) at the end.

    knn_graph removes the mid-near pairs and nothing else, so it is the measurement of what they were carrying. Eight-neighbour overlap barely moves, \(0.773 \to 0.760\), while forty-neighbour overlap falls \(0.852 \to 0.786\) and distance correlation collapses \(0.799 \to 0.484\) on \(14\%\) fewer pairs. The mid-near term is not a refinement; it is the only term that sees the middle of the distance range.

    localmap follows the published LocalMAP (Wang, Sun, Huang & Rudin 2025), which keeps PaCMAP's first two phases and, in the final phase, resamples the further pairs to be local so that nearby clusters are pushed apart. Here the local set is the two-hop ring — the graph's own notion of proximal — and the swap is confined to the last phase, as the paper confines it. It does what it says: eight-neighbour overlap rises \(0.773 \to 0.784\), while forty-neighbour overlap falls \(0.852 \to 0.791\) and the distance correlation \(0.799 \to 0.629\). Start the same swap at step 0 and the layout never forms: \(0.624\) at eight neighbours, \(0.457\) at forty. Local computations have to arrive after the global arrangement, not instead of it.

    supervised lets the labels decide which pairs exist: neighbour pairs are kept only within a class, and further pairs are drawn across classes. On five touching eight-dimensional Gaussians the fraction of each point's eight layout neighbours that share its label goes from \(0.340\) to \(0.970\), from \(3997\) cross-class further pairs against \(4000\) unlabelled ones. A point with no same-class neighbour in its own ring keeps its nearest neighbour, so no point is left with repulsion only.

    Derivation #

    Write the data as \(X = (x_1, \dots, x_n) \subset \mathbb{R}^d\), the layout as \(Y = (y_1, \dots, y_n) \subset \mathbb{R}^2\), and \(d_{ij} = \lVert x_i - x_j \rVert\). Set \(k\) neighbours, \(m = \mathtt{mn\_ratio}\), \(f = \mathtt{fp\_ratio}\).

    1. Three sets of pairs #

    The three sets are indexed by the same pair array with an integer kind:

    \[\mathcal{N} = \{(i, j) : j \in \mathrm{kNN}_i\}, \qquad \mathcal{M} = \{(i, j) : j \sim \mathrm{MN}_i\}, \qquad \mathcal{F} = \{(i, j) : j \sim \mathrm{Unif}(\text{non-neighbours})\} \qquad (2)\]

    with \(|\mathcal{N}| = nk\), \(|\mathcal{M}| = nmk\) and \(|\mathcal{F}| = nfk\) before rejections. At the defaults those are \(2500\), \(1250\) and \(5000\).

    Two of the three are not the obvious construction, and both of the surprises are the method.

    2. Neighbours on scaled distances #

    A k-nearest-neighbour graph measures everything in the units of the densest region it touches: a point in a sparse region has large distances to all of its neighbours, so it loses its slots to points in a dense region that happen to be Euclidean-close. PaCMAP selects on

    \[s_{ij} = \frac{d_{ij}^2}{\sigma_i \sigma_j}, \qquad \sigma_i = \frac{1}{3}\left(d_{i,(4)} + d_{i,(5)} + d_{i,(6)}\right) \qquad (3)\]

    where \(d_{i,(r)}\) is the \(r\)-th smallest distance from \(i\). The scale is read off the ring just outside the neighbourhood, so it describes the region rather than the point, and dividing by both endpoints' scales takes density out of the comparison: a pair straddling a dense and a sparse region has one large \(\sigma\), and its scaled distance is smaller than its raw one.

    The measured effect is an equalisation rather than a preference. On two Gaussians whose spreads differ by a factor of five and which overlap in space, raw selection sends \(0.161\) of the sparse region's neighbour slots across and \(0.076\) of the dense region's; scaled selection sends \(0.124\) and \(0.095\). The sparse region stops being annexed by its dense neighbour.

    The selected pairs are still scored on the raw distance, because the layout is a statement about distances and not about \(s_{ij}\). The scaled quantity only decides who is allowed into the graph.

    3. Mid-near pairs are an order statistic #

    A mid-near pair is the second closest of six uniformly drawn points. One slot, one draw:

    \[j \sim \text{second smallest of } \{u_1, \dots, u_6\}, \qquad u_r \sim \mathrm{Unif}(\{1, \dots, n\} \setminus \{i\}) \qquad (4)\]

    Why this gives the middle of the distance range, and why it must be done per slot, is an order-statistic argument. If the six draws are treated as samples from the empirical distance distribution \(F_i\), the \(r\)-th order statistic sits near the \(r/7\) quantile, so the second of six sits near the \(2/7 \approx 0.29\) quantile. On the demo's roll that is a mean distance of \(1.54\), between the neighbour ring's \(0.44\) and a random pair's \(2.12\).

    The mistake the construction guards against is to draw six once and take the second through sixth as the five partners. Those five are the \(2/7\) through \(6/7\) quantiles, whose mean is at or above the median: measured, that variant puts the mid-near set at \(2.30\), no nearer than a random pair at \(2.12\), and the "mid-near" set stops being mid. Independence is the whole content of the term.

    4. Further pairs #

    The third set is uniform over the complement of the neighbour set, drawn with bounded rejection (five rounds; whatever is still invalid is dropped).

    This is the structural difference from UMAP and t-SNE, which sample negatives per edge from the complement of that edge's neighbourhood. Per-edge negatives are biased towards the neighbourhood boundary — they are the points most likely to be confused with a neighbour — and they say nothing about which regions are far apart. A uniform draw says: these two points are not neighbours, and their layout distance should reflect how far apart they are in the input. The demo measures the consequence: distance correlation \(0.799\) against UMAP's \(0.534\) and t-SNE's \(0.335\).

    5. The objective #

    With \(\tilde d_{ij} = 1 + \lVert y_i - y_j \rVert^2 \ge 1\),

    \[L = w_{\mathrm{NP}} \sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{N}} \frac{\tilde d_{ij}}{10 + \tilde d_{ij}} + w_{\mathrm{MN}} \sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{M}} \frac{\tilde d_{ij}}{10^4 + \tilde d_{ij}} + w_{\mathrm{FP}} \sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{F}} \frac{1}{1 + \tilde d_{ij}} \qquad (5)\]

    The two attractive terms are saturating; the repulsive one is a bounded potential. The constants are not arbitrary — each sets the length at which that term stops mattering.

    6. Three lengths: 3, 100, and a range that ends immediately #

    Write \(r = \lVert y_i - y_j \rVert\), so \(\tilde d = 1 + r^2\).

    Neighbour term. \(\tilde d/(10 + \tilde d)\) is at half of its supremum when \(\tilde d = 10\), i.e. \(r = 3\), and the force it exerts on the two points is \(20w_{\mathrm{NP}}\, r/(11 + r^2)^2\): it peaks at \(r = \sqrt{11/3} \approx 1.9\) and then falls as \(r^{-3}\), so a doubling of distance divides it by eight. The neighbour spring pulls hard across two or three layout units and has essentially no opinion beyond that, which is what lets clusters sit at any separation the other terms choose.

    Mid-near term. For \(r^2 \ll 10^4\) the potential is \(\tfrac{1}{10^4} r^2\) past a constant, a harmonic spring with a linear restoring force, and its force \(2\cdot10^4 w_{\mathrm{MN}}\, r/(10^4 + r^2)^2\) does not peak until \(r \approx 58\). This is the only term whose reach covers the whole layout, and it is why removing the mid-near pairs costs distance correlation and not local overlap.

    Further term. \(1/(1+\tilde d)\) is a bounded potential, and its force \(2w_{\mathrm{FP}}\, r/(2 + r^2)^2\) behaves as \(2w_{\mathrm{FP}}/r^3\) — the same exponent as the neighbour term, and at \(r = 1\) about \(1.6\) times its strength — but with no plateau. It is short-ranged: a further pair only does anything once the layout has already brought the two points close. That is what makes it a repulsion between regions rather than a pressure on the whole plane, and it is also why a further pair that is genuinely near in the input (which is what localmap samples) fights the neighbour term instead of complementing it.

    7. The gradient #

    Differentiate (5) with respect to \(y_i\). Each term is a scalar \(\phi(\tilde d)\) times \(\partial \tilde d/\partial y_i = 2(y_i - y_j)\):

    \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_i} = \sum_{j} c_{ij} \, (y_i - y_j), \qquad c_{ij} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{20\, w_{\mathrm{NP}}}{(10 + \tilde d_{ij})^2} & (i,j) \in \mathcal{N} \\[2mm] \dfrac{2\cdot10^4\, w_{\mathrm{MN}}}{(10^4 + \tilde d_{ij})^2} & (i,j) \in \mathcal{M} \\[2mm] -\dfrac{2\, w_{\mathrm{FP}}}{(1 + \tilde d_{ij})^2} & (i,j) \in \mathcal{F} \end{cases} \qquad (6)\]

    Because of the \(1+\) in \(\tilde d\), all three denominators are at least \(11^2\), \((10^4+1)^2\) and \(4\) even at coincidence: no clamp and no epsilon is needed anywhere, and the largest coefficient any pair can have is \(w_{\mathrm{FP}}/2\). Six centred differences of (5) against (6) agree to \(6.4 \times 10^{-10}\) relative.

    The sum is assembled with two bincount calls, one for the head of each pair and one for the tail, which is why the code never forms an \(n \times n\) gradient.

    8. Adam, with the correction folded into the step #

    Adam keeps an exponential moving average of the gradient and of its square and scales the step by the ratio. The bias correction for the zero-initialised averages can be kept on the learning rate:

    \[y \leftarrow y - \eta \, \frac{\sqrt{1 - \beta_2^{\,t+1}}}{1 - \beta_1^{\,t+1}} \cdot \frac{m_t}{\sqrt{v_t} + 10^{-7}} \qquad (7)\]

    with \(m_t = (1-\beta_1)\sum \beta_1^{t-s} g_s\) and similarly for \(v_t\). Folding it this way saves two arrays and matches the reference implementation, and it matters at the start: at \(t = 0\) the factor is \(\sqrt{0.001}/0.1 = 0.316\), so the first steps are a third the size of the asymptotic ones.

    9. The schedule #

    \[(w_{\mathrm{NP}}, w_{\mathrm{MN}}, w_{\mathrm{FP}})(t) = \begin{cases} \left(2,\; 1000 - \dfrac{997\, t}{100},\; 1\right) & t < 100 \\[2mm] (3,\; 3,\; 1) & 100 \le t < 200 \\[2mm] (1,\; 0,\; 1) & t \ge 200 \end{cases} \qquad (8)\]

    The decay is linear, not exponential, on purpose: the first phase should spend most of its budget near the large weight, which is where the regional arrangement is set. At \(t = 49\) the mid-near weight is \(511\); at \(t = 99\) it is \(12.97\).

    What the phases do is a measurement, not a claim — see the table in the intuition. In one sentence: phase one fixes the middle, phase two fixes the distances, phase three fixes the finest neighbourhoods and charges for both.

    10. Initialisation #

    The layout starts at the leading two principal components scaled by \(0.01\). That scale is smaller than a single Adam step, so the initialisation contributes direction and not shape: after one step the correlation with the rectangle is \(0.090\), not the \(0.280\) the projection itself has.

    It follows that the run does not depend on where it starts. Phase-three weights alone, from initial scales of \(0.01\), \(1\) and \(10\), converge to the same shape — eight-neighbour overlap \(0.755\), \(0.768\), \(0.749\) after \(750\) steps — but the layout then drifts out to about fifteen units of standard deviation on each axis whatever the starting scale was, which is the next point.

    11. What is identified #

    The objective is invariant to translation and to rotation and reflection of the layout, as any distance-based loss is. It is not invariant to scale in the sense of having a preferred one: all three terms are functions of \(\tilde d = 1 + r^2\), and at large \(r\) the attractive coefficients fall as \(r^{-4}\) while the repulsive ones do too. Their ratio is fixed, so the layout has a shape but no scale, and the optimiser drifts outward as long as it runs — the demo's full run ends at a standard deviation of \(7.54\), and the same weights run for \(750\) steps reach \(15\).

    This is not a bug to be fixed with a normalisation step at the end; it is what "the embedding is defined up to similarity" means in practice. It does mean that two runs with different iteration budgets should be compared on shape (overlap, correlation) and never on coordinates or on the loss.

    12. Supervised pairs #

    supervised changes the sets, not the weights. A neighbour pair is kept only if the two labels agree; a further pair is drawn only if they disagree:

    \[\mathcal{N}^{\text{sup}} = \{(i,j) \in \mathcal{N} : \ell_i = \ell_j\}, \qquad \mathcal{F}^{\text{sup}} = \{(i,j) \in \mathcal{F} : \ell_i \ne \ell_j\} \qquad (9)\]

    The geometry still decides which points are eligible — a cross-class pair has to be among the uniform draw — and the labels decide which eligible pairs survive. A point whose whole neighbourhood is cross-class would otherwise have no attractive term at all, so it keeps its single nearest neighbour. That fallback is not decoration: without it, such a point has only repulsion acting on it and drifts until the repulsion balances, which is where supervised embeddings get their stray points.

    Code walkthrough #

    PaCMAP(n_components=2, *, variant="pacmap", n_neighbors=10, mn_ratio=0.5, fp_ratio=2.0, n_iters=(100, 100, 250), learning_rate=1.0, seed=0) with fit(X, y=None) and fit_transform(X, y=None). Attributes: embedding_, pairs_, local_pairs_, neighbour_distances_, loss_, history_, weights_.

    1. Neighbours at the region's scale #

        n = D.shape[0]
    k_ext = int(min(k + extra, n - 1))
    dk, idx = _knn(D, k_ext)
    sigma = np.maximum(dk[:, 3:6].mean(axis=1), EPS)
    scaled = dk * dk / sigma[:, None] / sigma[idx]
    order = np.argsort(scaled, axis=1)[:, :k]
    return (np.take_along_axis(dk, order, axis=1),
            np.take_along_axis(idx, order, axis=1))

    (3), for all points at once. The candidate set is \(k + 20\) wide so that a point can be selected on scaled distance even if it was not among the raw \(k\); the bracket dk[:, 3:6] is the fourth through sixth raw neighbour, read before any reordering. The returned distances are raw and are what the rest of the code scores; only the selection uses scaled.

    2. Mid-near: the second of six #

        n = D.shape[0]
    out = np.empty((n, n_mn), dtype=np.int64)
    for i in range(n):
        for slot in range(n_mn):
            chosen = rng.choice(n - 1, size=6, replace=False)
            chosen = chosen + (chosen >= i)          # skip the point itself
            out[i, slot] = chosen[np.argsort(D[i, chosen])[1]]
    return out

    (4). chosen + (chosen >= i) maps a uniform index in \([0, n-2]\) to \([0, n-1]\) while skipping \(i\) — the standard trick for sampling without replacement around one excluded value. The [1] is the second nearest; the loop over slots is what makes the five partners independent draws instead of five order statistics of one draw.

    3. Further pairs, with bounded rejection #

        n_fp = int(min(n_fp, n - 1))
    pool = rng.integers(0, n, size=(n, n_fp))
    bad = pool == np.arange(n)[:, None]
    bad |= (pool[:, :, None] == neighbours[:, None, :]).any(axis=2)

    The first draw, then the mask. Each of the four following rounds replaces the bad slots with a fresh uniform draw and recomputes the mask; the surviving mask is returned with the pool, and the caller drops the rest. A while loop here would be the one place the demo could hang, and its termination would depend on data, so it is bounded and the loss is measured rather than assumed. At the defaults on the roll nothing is dropped; from the two-hop set, which is finite, \(1020\) of \(5000\) slots are.

    4. Two-hop pairs #

        hood = [set(row.tolist()) for row in neighbours]
    pool = np.zeros((n, n_fp), dtype=np.int64)
    keep = np.zeros((n, n_fp), dtype=bool)
    for i in range(n):
        two: set[int] = set()
        for j in hood[i]:
            two |= hood[j]
        two.discard(i)
        two -= hood[i]

    The local set of localmap. Set unions over the k-neighbourhood give the two-hop ring in \(O(nk^2)\) with no distance computation; subtracting hood[i] leaves only non-adjacent pairs, which is what a further pair has to be. The if not two: continue in the loop below is the reason the pair count is a measured quantity — a point in a degenerate sample can have an empty two-hop set.

    5. The schedule #

        p1, p2, _ = phases
    if t < p1:
        frac = t / p1
        return 2.0, (1.0 - frac) * 1000.0 + frac * 3.0, 1.0
    if t < p1 + p2:
        return 3.0, 3.0, 1.0
    return 1.0, 0.0, 1.0

    (8), in pair-kind order — and the order is the point. The three floats are indexed by NP, MN, FP at the call site, so returning them in any other order silently gives the neighbour pairs the mid-near weight. That bug costs \(0.773 \to 0.255\) in eight-neighbour overlap; see failure mode 1.

    6. The objective #

        d = Y[heads] - Y[tails]
    d2 = 1.0 + np.einsum("ij,ij->i", d, d)
    total = 0.0
    for kind, denom in ((NP, 10.0), (MN, 10000.0)):
        mask = kinds == kind
        if mask.any():
            total += weights[kind] * float((d2[mask] / (denom + d2[mask])).sum())
    mask = kinds == FP
    if mask.any():
        total += weights[FP] * float((1.0 / (1.0 + d2[mask])).sum())
    return total

    (5), written so that the finite-difference check compares against the objective and not against a surrogate. Note the asymmetry in the denominators: the two attractive terms put denom with d2, and the repulsive one puts 1.0 with d2 because its denominator in (5) is \(1 + \tilde d\) and \(\tilde d\) already carries its own one. Writing 1.0 / d2 there is failure mode 2.

    7. The gradient #

        coef = np.empty_like(d2)
    for kind, denom, sign in ((NP, 10.0, 1.0), (MN, 10000.0, 1.0),
                              (FP, 1.0, -1.0)):
        mask = kinds == kind
        if mask.any():
            coef[mask] = sign * weights[kind] * 2.0 * denom / (denom + d2[mask]) ** 2
    g = coef[:, None] * d
    grad = np.empty_like(Y)
    for j in range(Y.shape[1]):
        grad[:, j] = (np.bincount(heads, weights=g[:, j], minlength=Y.shape[0])
                      - np.bincount(tails, weights=g[:, j], minlength=Y.shape[0]))

    (6). coef is the scalar per pair, g = coef * d is the contribution to the head, and the two bincount calls scatter it with opposite signs at the two ends. One loop per output coordinate, so three coordinates would make the gradient three passes rather than one matrix — the cost is out of the pair count, not out of the loop.

    8. Adam #

        m += (1.0 - beta1) * (grad - m)
    v += (1.0 - beta2) * (grad * grad - v)
    rate = lr * np.sqrt(1.0 - beta2 ** (step + 1)) / (1.0 - beta1 ** (step + 1))
    Y -= rate * m / (np.sqrt(v) + 1e-7)

    (7). m and v are buffers owned by the caller, so the fit loop allocates them once. The step + 1 is the \(t+1\) of the bias correction; with step starting at zero, the first update uses \(\sqrt{1-\beta_2}/(1-\beta_1)\).

    9. Initialisation #

        Xc = X - X.mean(axis=0)
    U, S, _ = np.linalg.svd(Xc, full_matrices=False)
    take = min(n_components, U.shape[1])
    out = np.zeros((X.shape[0], n_components))
    out[:, :take] = U[:, :take] * S[:take]
    return 0.01 * out

    The leading principal components with their variances folded in — the usual projection — and then the \(0.01\). A full svd is affordable here and is the honest way to write a two-component projection; a production implementation would use the same randomised or Lanczos solver it uses for the neighbours.

    10. The pair assembly #

            base = (np.concatenate(heads + [head_fp]),
                np.concatenate(tails + [tail_fp]),
                np.concatenate(kinds + [np.full(head_fp.size, FP)]))

        local = None
        if self.variant == "localmap":
            pool2, keep2 = _sample_second_order(idx, n_fp, rng)
            head2 = np.repeat(np.arange(n)[:, None], n_fp, axis=1)[keep2]
            tail2 = pool2[keep2]
            local = (np.concatenate(heads + [head2]),
                     np.concatenate(tails + [tail2]),
                     np.concatenate(kinds + [np.full(head2.size, FP)]))

    The two triples differ only in their further pairs, so localmap costs one extra set of indices and no extra work per step: the loop swaps the triple it is pointing at. heads at this point already holds the neighbour and mid-near blocks, which is why both triples concatenate the same prefix — the first two phases are literally the same run.

    11. The loop #

            for step in range(total):
            if local is not None and step == switch:
                heads, tails, kinds = local
            weights = _weights(step, self.n_iters)
            grad = _pair_gradient(heads, tails, kinds, Y, weights)
            _adam(Y, grad, m, v, self.learning_rate, step)
            if step in checkpoints:
                self.history_.append(_pair_loss(heads, tails, kinds, Y, weights))
                self.weights_.append((step, *weights))

    switch is \(p_1 + p_2\): the last phase starts where the first two end, which is where LocalMAP puts its local computation. The history is recorded at six checkpoints — the loss is recomputed, not accumulated, so the number is the objective at the layout that produced it, and the checkpoints are the only pairs of (loss, weights) that are comparable with each other.

    Complexity #

    \(n\) samples, \(d\) dimensions, \(k\) neighbours, \(m = \mathtt{mn\_ratio}\), \(f = \mathtt{fp\_ratio}\), \(T\) steps, \(P = nk(1 + m + f)\) pairs.

    Operation Time Memory Note
    pairwise distances \(O(n^2 d)\) \(O(n^2)\) Gram identity
    raw kNN, \(k+20\) wide \(O(n^2)\) \(O(n^2)\) argpartition per row
    scaled selection \(O(n (k+20) \log(k+20))\) \(O(n(k+20))\) one sort per row
    mid-near draws \(O(n^2 m)\) \(O(nm)\) choice without replacement is \(O(n)\)
    further pairs \(O(5 n f k)\) \(O(nfk)\) one draw plus four retries
    two-hop pairs \(O(nk^2)\) \(O(nk)\) set unions, no distances
    PCA init \(O(n d \min(n, d))\) \(O(nd)\) one dense svd
    one step \(O(P)\) \(O(n)\) \(8750\) terms at the defaults
    full run \(O(TP)\) \(O(n)\) \(450 \times 8750 \approx 3.9 \times 10^6\) terms
    full demo — — 8 fits, 1.3 s

    The \(O(n^2)\) rows are the fixed cost and the reason this is a visualisation method: at \(10^5\) points the distance matrix is the whole budget. Production PaCMAP uses an approximate kNN index and never forms it, which turns the pipeline into \(O(n k)\) per step plus the index build. The mid-near draw is the one part that does not survive that change cheaply — it needs distances to random points, which is why the reference implementation samples the mid-near set once and keeps it, and why localmap's two-hop set is attractive: it needs no distances at all.

    fp_ratio is the only knob that buys more terms per step rather than a different graph: every further pair is a term in every step, so raising it from \(2.0\) to \(3.0\) adds \(2500\) terms per step and measurably loses — distance correlation \(0.799 \to 0.776\) (see failure mode 6).

    Failure modes #

    1. Returning the weights in the wrong order. _weights returns a triple that callers index by pair kind, NP = 0, MN = 1, FP = 2. Returning \((w_{\mathrm{MN}}, w_{\mathrm{NP}}, w_{\mathrm{FP}})\) — which is the order the phases were originally written in — gives the neighbour pairs weight \(1000\) in phase one and weight \(0\) in phase three. Measured, eight-neighbour overlap falls from \(0.773\) to \(0.255\), forty-neighbour from \(0.852\) to \(0.461\), and the distance correlation from \(0.799\) to \(0.320\). Nothing raises, the loss still falls, and the plot still looks like a swiss roll — it is simply a worse one. Assert on the phase values, not on the loss.

    2. Dropping the \(+1\) in the repulsive term. In (5) the denominator is \(1 + \tilde d\), and \(\tilde d\) already carries a \(+1\), so the code needs 1.0 / (1.0 + d2). Writing 1.0 / d2 produces a loss whose analytic gradient is off by a factor of about three for that term: on one probe the analytic gradient is \(-0.191\) where the centred difference says \(-0.590\). The layout barely notices, because the sign and the shape are right and Adam rescales per coordinate; the finite-difference check notices immediately, which is why the demo runs one against the objective rather than against a remembered gradient. After the fix the two agree to \(6.4 \times 10^{-10}\).

    3. Taking five partners from one draw of six. Taking the second through sixth order statistics of a single draw puts the mid-near set at a mean distance of \(2.30\) — no nearer than a random pair at \(2.12\), and beyond the further pairs' \(2.18\). The layout hardly notices (\(0.764\)/\(0.843\)/\(0.801\) against \(0.773\)/\(0.852\)/\(0.799\)), because at weight \(1000\) the term is carrying mostly its weight and not its scale. The pair distances are what tell you the construction is wrong, which is why the demo asserts them: \(0.44 < 1.54 < 2.18\) with each in its own range.

    4. Applying the local pairs from the first step. localmap's two-hop set is the right local set and the wrong early set. Swapped in at step 0 it gives \(0.624\) at eight neighbours and \(0.457\) at forty, against \(0.784\) and \(0.791\) when confined to the last phase. The reason is in §6: the further term is a short-ranged repulsion, so a pair that is genuinely near in the input is pushed apart, and before phase two has set the global arrangement every pair is near something. The published method stages it; the staging is not a detail.

    5. Reading the loss across variants, or across runs. localmap reports \(915.9\) against pacmap's \(530.5\), and the numbers are not comparable: they are sums over different pair sets with different counts. Within one run the loss is an honest objective value; across runs it is a different function. The same applies to two runs of the same variant with different iteration budgets, which end at different scales — see §11. Compare overlap and correlation.

    6. Raising k or fp_ratio because more must be better. More neighbours is a wider graph and a different graph, and more further pairs is more short-ranged pressure. Measured, \(k = 20\) keeps \(0.703\) at eight neighbours and correlates \(0.434\), against \(0.773\) and \(0.799\) at \(k = 10\); fp_ratio \(= 3.0\) correlates \(0.776\) against \(0.799\). The sweep is not monotone in either direction (\(k = 8\) reaches \(0.807\) and \(k = 15\) correlates \(0.983\)), because each value changes the sampled pair set as well as the neighbourhood, and a single roll is a single draw. Pick the neighbourhood size from the structure you want to see, then report the trade rather than a winner.

    Applications #

    Single-cell transcriptomics. PaCMAP's own evaluation is dominated by single-cell RNA-seq, where the questions are "which cells are the same type" and "how do the types relate", and where t-SNE's cluster separation and UMAP's speed had been the trade. The measured column that matters there is the middle one: mid-range overlap is what keeps a trajectory — a cell differentiating through intermediate states — visible instead of collapsing it into two islands.

    Image atlases and dataset exploration. The same argument on MNIST, Fashion-MNIST and activation spaces: a layout that keeps \(0.85\) of each point's forty nearest neighbours is a layout you can zoom into, because the region you zoom into is the region the input says belongs together.

    When the distances matter. The fp_ratio dial is the practical feature: at the default the layout is a good map, and at fp_ratio=1.0 it is a map that correlates \(0.969\) with the true isometry — not Isomap's \(0.9996\), but with local structure intact. For any downstream use that reads distances rather than adjacency, that is the configuration to try, and the cost is a measurable one (\(0.719\) at eight neighbours instead of \(0.773\)).

    Separating adjacent clusters. LocalMAP's target: two clusters that touch, where a global method smooths the boundary and a purely local one leaves them merged for a different reason. The recipe is PaCMAP for two phases and a local pair set for the third, which is what localmap implements.

    What it is not for. The output is not a metric embedding: distances are ordinal at best, densities are not preserved (every region gets the area its pairs demand, not the area its sampling had), and the scale is arbitrary. For reconstruction, denoising or as features for a supervised model, an autoencoder or a diffusion map is the right tool.
