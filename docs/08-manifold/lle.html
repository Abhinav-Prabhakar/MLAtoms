    08 · Manifolds & embeddings/tier 2/4 variants/source

    Locally linear embedding

    Intuition #

    Isomap estimates a distance and then embeds it. LLE refuses the first step. Its premise is that a distance is the wrong local object to measure from a sample: it needs a metric, it is dominated by wherever the sampling happens to be dense, and on a curved sheet the straight-line distance between two nearby points is exactly the quantity the curvature corrupts. What survives at the scale of a neighbourhood is weaker and more useful — where the point sits inside its neighbourhood, as the coefficients of the affine combination that reproduces it.

           neighbourhood in the data               the same weights in the layout

              x₂                                     y₂
               ·  0.30                                ·  0.30
                \                                      \
                 \                                      \
        x₁ ·──────· xᵢ ·──── x₃            y₁ ·──────· yᵢ ·──── y₃
            0.50         0.20                   0.50         0.20

        xᵢ = 0.50 x₁ + 0.30 x₂ + 0.20 x₃      minimise ‖yᵢ − Σⱼ wᵢⱼ yⱼ‖²

    The figure is the whole method. On the left the coefficients are found by least squares in the data; on the right the layout is asked to make the same coefficients hold. Nothing in the second half refers to a distance except the distances between neighbours, which were used once and then discarded.

    The weights have one property that matters more than the rest: they are invariant under similarities. Rotate the neighbourhood, translate it, scale it uniformly, and the coefficients do not move — measured to \(3 \times 10^{-14}\) on a plane. They describe the shape of a neighbourhood relative to itself, not its size or its position. The embedding inherits that indifference, and that is where both the method's reputation and its failure modes come from: what it recovers is a subspace, not a set of coordinates, so any comparison in a fixed coordinate frame is partly measuring a choice the eigen-solver made on its own.

    graph LR
    A["X ∈ ℝⁿˣᵈ"] --> B["kNN graph"]
    B --> C["barycentric weights<br/>Σⱼ wᵢⱼ = 1"]
    C --> D["M = (I−W)ᵀ(I−W)"]
    D --> E["bottom d+1 eigenvectors"]
    E --> F["drop the constant<br/>Y ∈ ℝⁿˣ²"]

    The demo's first measurement is that the algebra is exact where it should be. 200 points on a random plane in three dimensions, \(k = 10\): every row of weights is a barycentre to \(4 \times 10^{-16}\), the objective equals the sum of the two chosen eigenvalues to twelve digits, and the layout correlates \(0.99707\) with the plane's own coordinates while keeping \(0.974\) of each point's eight nearest neighbours. Rotating the input and mapping it through \(7x + 3\) leave every weight and every neighbourhood unchanged.

    The second measurement is that exact reconstruction weights say nothing about the metric of the layout. Hand the method the answer — the S-curve's own two coordinates, a rectangle — and it returns that rectangle up to a linear map: the raw correlation with the input is \(0.7425\), and after one least-squares affine map it is \(0.9956\), or \(0.9997\) at \(k = 15\). The map has a singular-value ratio of \(4.75\): the layout is the rectangle squashed on one axis, and essentially all of the loss is the squash.

    Variants #

    The four variants are three answers to the same defect plus one that moves the graph instead. The defect: with \(k\) neighbours and \(D\) input dimensions the local Gram matrix has rank at most \(D\), and the weights are pinned down by the constraint only while \(k \le D + 1\). Past that they form a \((k - D - 1)\)-dimensional affine family, the solver returns one member, and the eigenproblem then amplifies that arbitrary choice. modified keeps the whole family, hessian abandons weights and fits a quadratic, supervised changes which points are neighbours at all.

    On the 300-point S-curve at \(k = 10\):

    variant what it penalises overlap @8 @40 distance corr.
    standard reconstruction error with one chosen weight vector 0.521 0.742 0.828
    modified the same error over the whole weight family 0.571 0.592 0.757
    hessian curvature of the coordinate, weights discarded 0.577 0.596 0.760
    supervised identical objective, class-weighted neighbour search — — —

    modified and hessian both buy local overlap and pay for it in the middle range and in the distances: at forty neighbours they score \(0.592\) and \(0.596\) against \(0.742\), and they correlate \(0.757\) and \(0.760\) against \(0.828\). That is not a tuning accident but the design — both replace a global constraint with a local one, and overlap at eight neighbours is the quantity they were built to protect.

    supervised is the other kind of variant. It multiplies cross-class distances by \(1 + \lambda\) before the neighbour search and leaves the objective untouched. On two interleaved spiral arms the unweighted graph spends \(0.337\) of its edges across the arms; at \(\lambda = 2\) the cross-arm rate is \(0.001\) and the purity of each point's eight layout neighbours rises from \(0.728\) to \(0.935\). At \(\lambda = 5\) it is \(1.000\), which is also what a method that was no longer looking at the geometry would score.

    Derivation #

    1. The local problem #

    For one point \(i\) with neighbourhood \(\mathcal{N}_i\) of size \(k\):

    \[w_i = \arg\min_{w} \Big\lVert x_i - \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i} w_j x_j \Big\rVert^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_j w_j = 1 \qquad (1)\]

    Write \(Z\) for the \(k \times D\) matrix whose row \(a\) is \(x_{j_a} - x_i\), and \(G = ZZ^\top\). The Lagrangian is \(\lVert Z^\top w\rVert^2 + \lambda(\mathbf{1}^\top w - 1)\), and stationarity gives \(Gw = \lambda\mathbf{1}\). The multiplier is fixed by the constraint rather than by the data, so the solution is the direction of \(G^{-1}\mathbf{1}\):

    \[w = \frac{G^{-1}\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}^\top G^{-1}\mathbf{1}} \qquad (2)\]

    Translation invariance is already in the constraint, not in the centring: the weights see only differences. The code centres explicitly anyway so that the Gram matrix stays as well conditioned as the data allows.

    2. Rank, ridge, and why the exact answer is the wrong one #

    \(G = ZZ^\top\) with \(Z\) of shape \(k \times D\), so \(\mathrm{rank}(G) \le D < k\) for every interesting neighbourhood: \(G\) is singular and solve raises. At \(k = D + 1\) the constraint still pins down one minimiser, but past that the minimisers form a \((k - D - 1)\)-dimensional affine family. The implementation regularises with

    \[G \leftarrow G + \tau\,\mathrm{tr}(G)\,I \qquad (3)\]

    Scaling by \(\mathrm{tr}(G)\) makes \(\tau\) dimensionless, so it means the same thing in any units. As \(\tau \to \infty\), \(G^{-1} \to I/(\tau\mathrm{tr}G)\) and \(w \to \mathbf{1}/k\), the neighbourhood mean; as \(\tau \to 0\) we get the exact barycentric weights. The ridge is therefore not a numerical nuisance bolted onto a correct formula but a knob that interpolates between "average the neighbours" and "reproduce the point exactly", and the useful setting is in between. The demo walks the whole interval on the S-curve at \(k = 10\):

    \(\tau\) 1e-9 1e-6 1e-4 1e-3 1e-2 1e-1
    objective 4e-16 6e-13 4e-9 3.4e-7 1e-5 6e-5
    distance corr. 0.222 0.392 0.706 0.828 0.835 0.821
    eight-neighbour overlap 0.407 0.495 0.498 0.521 0.510 0.481

    At \(\tau = 10^{-9}\) the weights reconstruct every point to machine precision — the objective the method is written to minimise is essentially zero — and the correlation with the truth is \(0.222\), worse than a linear projection. At \(\tau = 10^{-3}\) the objective is \(3.4 \times 10^{-7}\), nine orders of magnitude larger by the method's own measure, and the correlation is \(0.828\). The reason is in (2): an exactly reconstructed neighbourhood can only be reconstructed by weights that cancel, which means coefficients of large magnitude with both signs — and those are precisely the coefficients whose arbitrary component the eigenproblem magnifies. reg=0.0 does not fail gracefully; it raises LinAlgError: Singular matrix on the first neighbourhood with \(k > D\).

    3. The global problem #

    Given the \(n \times n\) matrix \(W\) with \(W_{ij} = w_{ij}\) for \(j \in \mathcal{N}_i\) and zero elsewhere, the layout minimises the same reconstruction error at every point:

    \[\mathcal{E}(Y) = \sum_i \Big\lVert y_i - \sum_j W_{ij} y_j \Big\rVert^2 = \mathrm{tr}\left(Y^\top M Y\right), \qquad M = (I - W)^\top (I - W) \qquad (4)\]

    and the trivial solution \(Y = 0\) is removed by the normalisation

    \[Y = \arg\min_{Y^\top Y = I} \mathrm{tr}\left(Y^\top M Y\right) \qquad (5)\]

    The constraint says the columns are orthonormal and nothing else in the problem mentions scale. That is the point: (5) selects a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) — the span of the \(d\) eigenvectors with the smallest eigenvalues — and the coordinates are any orthonormal basis of it.

    \(M\) is symmetric positive semidefinite by construction, \(n \times n\), and built from \(W\) alone. No distance between non-neighbours ever enters it, which is why the method is cheap in graph terms and fragile in metric terms.

    4. The direction the constraint leaves free #

    Every row of \(W\) sums to one, so \((I - W)\mathbf{1} = 0\) and therefore \(M\mathbf{1} = 0\): the constant vector is an eigenvector with eigenvalue zero, and its eigenvalue carries no information. The code drops the bottom eigenvector and keeps the next \(d\). This is not a normalisation added for tidiness — it is the direction the constraint set leaves unconstrained, because a cost built from differences cannot see an additive constant.

    Exactly one direction sits at machine zero — the constant. On the S-curve the next eigenvalue is \(2.2 \times 10^{-9}\) against \(3.4 \times 10^{-7}\) for the one after it, so the first coordinate is very nearly free as well and the objective, \(3.4 \times 10^{-7}\), is almost entirely the price of the folded direction. On a disconnected graph there is one exactly-free direction per component, and the layout collapses; see failure mode 2.

    5. What the eigenproblem identifies, and what it does not #

    Two invariances are at work, and separating them is the whole difficulty of reading a LLE result.

    The weights are similarity-invariant. For \(x \mapsto sRx + b\) with \(R\) orthogonal, the residual \(Z^\top w\) is multiplied by \(sR\), so the minimiser is unchanged: rotation and \(7x + 3\) move no weight by more than \(3 \times 10^{-14}\). A non-uniform linear map does change them, because it changes which direction of a neighbourhood is worth cancelling.

    The embedding is basis-free. Since (5) depends on \(Y\) only through \(\mathrm{span}(Y)\), the answer is a \(d\)-dimensional subspace \(S\) of \(\mathbb{R}^n\). If \(T\) is another coordinate matrix with columns in \(S\) — the manifold's "true" parameters, when those happen to be linear in the embedding — then \(Y = TA\) for some invertible \(d \times d\) matrix \(A\), and \(A\) need not be orthogonal. Orthonormality is a normalisation the true coordinates do not share.

    The practical rule that follows: a raw score mixes how well the subspace was identified with which gauge the eigen-solver happened to return. Distance correlation survives a near-similarity; eight-neighbour overlap survives nothing, since a shear changes which points are nearest. The demo measures the difference on data where the answer is known.

    aspect of the rectangle 1 2 4 8 16 32
    raw distance corr. 0.9988 0.8729 0.7655 0.7270 0.7677 0.8416
    after one affine map 0.9992 0.9953 0.9992 0.9994 0.9998 0.9999
    singular-value ratio of that map 1.04 2.08 4.16 9.28 69.77 672.43
    raw overlap @8 0.979 0.761 0.580 0.510 0.440 0.630

    Uniform rectangles of 300 points, \(k = 10\). The layout is the rectangle to four decimal places once a single \(2 \times 2\) map is undone, at every aspect, and the map's condition number tracks the aspect until the layout becomes effectively one-dimensional and the ratio explodes. Everything the raw column lost is that map — which the method never claimed to fix.

    The same correction on the S-curve is the cautionary case. It takes the correlation from \(0.828\) to \(0.986\) and the eight-neighbour overlap from \(0.521\) down to \(0.395\), and both numbers are telling the truth. The sheet's own coordinates span \(9.4\) by \(2.0\), so pairwise distances — and therefore the correlation — are dominated by the long axis. The recovered map has a singular-value ratio of \(21\), it fixes that long axis to \(2.5\%\) of its range and leaves the short one at a correlation of \(0.23\) and an amplitude of \(0.55\) where the truth has \(2.0\). The aligned layout is a strip \(9.5\) long and \(0.55\) wide, in which neighbours can be ordered along essentially one dimension, so overlap collapses. Choosing the gauge that maximises a global distance statistic is not the same as choosing the gauge the method left you, and the two can differ by more than either differs from the truth.

    6. Modified LLE: keep the whole family #

    With \(k > D + 1\) the optimal weights of (1) form an affine set of dimension \(k - D - 1\). MLLE makes the embedding satisfy every member of that set instead of one arbitrary member. Let \(V_i\) be the \(k \times s_i\) matrix of the \(s_i\) smallest-eigenvalue directions of the local SVD basis \(U_i\). A regularised single weight vector is still needed as an anchor — the same vector (3) produces, only written in the SVD basis:

    \[w^{\mathrm{reg}}_i = U_i\,\mathrm{diag}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{i,r} + \tau\sum_s \lambda_{i,s}}\right)U_i^\top\mathbf{1}, \quad \text{renormalised so } \mathbf{1}^\top w^{\mathrm{reg}}_i = 1 \qquad (6)\]

    The number of directions to keep is read off the spectrum rather than fixed. With \(\eta\) the median, over all points, of the ratio of the tail eigenvalues to the leading \(d\),

    \[\eta = \mathrm{median}_i \frac{\sum_{r > d}\lambda_{i,r}}{\sum_{r \le d}\lambda_{i,r}} \qquad (7)\]

    \(s_i\) is the smallest number of trailing directions for which the eigenvalues being discarded together are at least \(\eta\) times those being kept. The trailing \(k - D\) directions of \(U_i\) have zero eigenvalues, so \(s_i \ge k - D\) by construction, and \(\eta\) decides only how far past the kernel the family reaches: on the S-curve at \(k = 10\) it stops at \(7 = k - D\) for half the points and takes one genuinely nonzero direction for the other half. No parameter has to be tuned per dataset, which is the point of using a median.

    Each member is then rotated so that it is a genuine barycentre. The rotation is a Householder reflection off the all-ones direction, followed by an admixture of the anchor:

    \[W_i = V_i - 2\,(V_i h_i)\,h_i^\top + (1 - \alpha_i)\,w^{\mathrm{reg}}_i\mathbf{1}^\top, \qquad \alpha_i = \frac{\lVert V_i^\top\mathbf{1}\rVert}{\sqrt{s_i}} \qquad (8)\]

    with \(h_i = (\alpha_i\mathbf{1} - V_i^\top\mathbf{1})/\lVert\cdot\rVert\), so that every column of \(W_i\) sums to one. The alignment matrix then takes a full block per point, placed at rows and columns \((i, \mathcal{N}_i)\):

    \[M \leftarrow M + \begin{bmatrix} s_i & -\mathbf{1}^\top W_i \\ -W_i\mathbf{1} & W_iW_i^\top \end{bmatrix} \qquad (9)\]

    This is what the np.ix_ line and the three scalar updates in the code do, one \(s_i\)-dimensional family at a time. The extra cost is a full SVD per neighbourhood instead of a \(k \times k\) solve; the gain is that the arbitrary member of the family is never chosen.

    7. Hessian LLE #

    Hessian LLE removes the weights from the problem. In each neighbourhood it builds the design matrix of a local quadratic — a constant, the \(d\) tangent coordinates, and their \(d(d+1)/2\) products — and orthonormalises it:

    \[Q_i = \mathrm{qr}\big[\,\mathbf{1},\; U_{i,1..d},\; \{U_{i,a}U_{i,b}\}_{a \le b}\,\big] \qquad (10)\]

    The first \(d + 1\) columns of \(Q_i\) span the constants and the linear functions — the coordinates whose local quadratic fit is exactly zero. The remaining \(d(d+1)/2\) columns are the part of the quadratic monomials orthogonal to them, so a vector in their span is a coordinate that is pure curvature in that neighbourhood. A weight vector built from them,

    \[w_i = \frac{Q_i[:, d+1:]}{\mathbf{1}^\top Q_i[:, d+1:]} \qquad (11)\]

    measures the curvature of the coordinate at \(i\), and the embedding minimises \(\sum_i w_i w_i^\top\). Linear coordinates have zero Hessian, so they sit in the null space and are exactly what the eigen-solver returns — which is why the method can separate a manifold from its own tangent space where standard LLE cannot.

    The design matrix has \(p = 1 + d + d(d+1)/2\) columns, so \(k \ge p\) is a hard requirement: six neighbours for two components, and the code raises rather than silently producing a degenerate \(Q\). On the S-curve at \(k = 10\) this buys \(0.577\) eight-neighbour overlap against \(0.521\) for standard.

    8. Supervised LLE #

    The last variant does not touch the algebra at all. It scales the distance matrix before the neighbour search, so that cross-class points have to be much closer before they compete for a slot:

    \[D_{ij} \leftarrow D_{ij}\left(1 + \lambda\,[\,y_i \ne y_j\,]\right) \qquad (12)\]

    The objective, the alignment matrix and the eigenproblem are unchanged, and the labels appear nowhere else. This is the same trick as dropping a graph edge, done smoothly: it is a graph construction, not a supervised loss.

    Code walkthrough #

    1. The graph #

        idx = np.argpartition(D, k, axis=1)[:, :k]
    dk = np.take_along_axis(D, idx, axis=1)
    order = np.argsort(dk, axis=1)
    return (np.take_along_axis(dk, order, axis=1),
            np.take_along_axis(idx, order, axis=1))

    argpartition finds the \(k\) smallest in a row without sorting the other \(n - k\), which is the whole reason a dense distance matrix is affordable here. The argsort afterwards puts only those \(k\) in order, so distances_[:, 0] is the nearest neighbour distance and weights_[:, 0] is its coefficient. The caller has already set the diagonal to inf; that is what stops a point from being its own neighbour.

    2. The weights #

        for i in range(n):
        Z = X[idx[i]] - X[i]
        G = Z @ Z.T
        trace = float(np.trace(G))
        G.flat[::k + 1] += reg * (trace if trace > EPS else 1.0)
        w = np.linalg.solve(G, ones)
        W[i] = w / w.sum()

    Line by line this is (1)–(3): centre, form the Gram matrix, add \(\tau\mathrm{tr}(G)\) to the diagonal through a strided view, solve \(Gw = \mathbf{1}\), normalise. The final division is the constraint, and it is also why the weights cannot drift: w.sum() is positive because \(G\) is positive definite after the ridge, so the row sums are exactly one up to rounding — measured at \(4 \times 10^{-16}\).

    The trace if trace > EPS else 1.0 guard covers a neighbourhood of identical points, where the local scale is undefined; without it the ridge would be zero and the solve would fail rather than return the uniform weights the mean solution implies.

    3. The alignment matrix #

        n = idx.shape[0]
    full = np.zeros((n, n))
    np.put_along_axis(full, idx, W, axis=1)
    A = np.eye(n) - full
    return A.T @ A

    put_along_axis scatters the \(k\) coefficients of each row into an \(n \times n\) matrix, so full is \(W\) with its rows padded by zeros. Then (4) is one matrix product. The comment on the function says it: the whole embedding problem is the Gram matrix of \(I - W\).

    This is also the asymptotically expensive line. \(W\) has only \(nk\) non-zeros, but A is the dense \(n \times n\) matrix \(I - W\), so A.T @ A costs \(O(n^3)\) — the same order as the eigendecomposition that follows. The two block-assembled variants never form this product, which is why they are faster than standard at \(n = 4000\) despite doing more arithmetic per point.

    4. MLLE's anchor vector #

        scale = np.maximum(evals.sum(axis=1), EPS) * reg
    tmp = np.einsum("nji,nj->ni", U, np.ones((n, k)))
    tmp[:, :nev] /= evals + scale[:, None]
    tmp[:, nev:] /= scale[:, None]
    w_reg = np.einsum("nij,nj->ni", U, tmp)
    w_reg /= w_reg.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

    Equation (6) with the einsums written out: Uᵀ1 for every point at once, divide each singular value by its regularised self, rotate back, renormalise. This is the same vector _barycentric computes, just in a basis where the ridge is a division instead of a solve — the two code paths agree to machine precision, and only one of them is used per variant.

    The nev = min(D, k) split matters: svd(..., full_matrices=True) returns \(k\) left singular vectors but only nev singular values, and the remaining columns of \(U\) span the exact kernel of \(Z\). For those the ridge is the only term, hence tmp[:, nev:] /= scale[:, None] rather than an addition.

    5. Choosing the family size #

        lead = np.maximum(evals[:, :d].sum(axis=1), EPS)
    rho = evals[:, d:].sum(axis=1) / lead
    eta = float(np.median(rho))
    cum = np.cumsum(evals, axis=1)
    ratio = cum[:, -1:] / np.maximum(cum[:, :-1], EPS) - 1.0
    s = np.array([np.searchsorted(ratio[i, ::-1], eta) for i in range(n)])
    s = np.maximum(s + k - nev, 1)

    rho is the per-point version of (7)'s ratio, and eta is its median. ratio is the same quantity for every prefix length, so cum[:, -1:] / cum[:, :-1] - 1 is \((\sum_{r>j}\lambda_r)/(\sum_{r\le j}\lambda_r)\) for each \(j\). The array is decreasing in \(j\), so [::-1] makes it ascending and searchsorted returns the smallest \(s\) that reaches \(\eta\); \(\eta\) being a median makes the rule scale-free. The + k - nev converts "how many tail eigenvalues" into "how many columns from the right of \(U\)", and the clip at one keeps at least the smallest direction.

    6. One MLLE block #

            Vi = U[i][:, k - si:]
        alpha = float(np.linalg.norm(Vi.sum(axis=0)) / np.sqrt(si))
        h = alpha - Vi.T @ np.ones(k)
        norm = float(np.linalg.norm(h))
        h = h / norm if norm > tol else np.zeros(si)
        Wi = Vi - 2.0 * np.outer(Vi @ h, h) + (1.0 - alpha) * w_reg[i][:, None]

    (8) directly. alpha is the projection of the all-ones direction onto the family, so Vi - 2(V_i h)hᵀ is the reflection that makes each column sum to \(\alpha\) instead of whatever it summed to; the anchor term supplies the rest, \(1 - \alpha\). When \(h\) is numerically zero — the family already orthonormal to the ones direction — the reflection is skipped, and the np.zeros(si) is the only branch in the whole variant.

    7. The block assembly #

            nb = idx[i]
        M[np.ix_(nb, nb)] += Wi @ Wi.T
        rows = Wi.sum(axis=1)
        M[i, nb] -= rows
        M[nb, i] -= rows
        M[i, i] += si

    (9), written as four sparse updates instead of one dense block: the \(s_i \times s_i\) quadratic form goes into the neighbourhood square with np.ix_, the two cross terms subtract the row sums, and the corner gets \(s_i\). Nothing outside those \(k + 1\) indices is touched, which is why MLLE's assembly stays \(O(nk^2)\) even though its matrix is dense in the output.

    8. Hessian design and residual #

            Y = np.empty((k, p))
        Y[:, 0] = 1.0
        Y[:, 1:1 + d] = U[:, :d]
        col = 1 + d
        for a in range(d):
            Y[:, col:col + d - a] = U[:, a:a + 1] * U[:, a:d]
            col += d - a
        Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(Y)

    The inner loop writes the upper triangle of the products \(U_aU_b\) in row-major order — \(d\) columns, then \(d-1\), and so on — so (10)'s column count is exactly p and the matrix is \((k \times p)\). qr then gives an orthonormal basis of the column space; the columns beyond \(d+1\) are the ones that a zero-Hessian function does not need.

            w = Q[:, d + 1:]
        s = w.sum(axis=0)
        s[np.abs(s) < tol] = 1.0
        w = w / s
        nb = idx[i]
        M[np.ix_(nb, nb)] += w @ w.T

    (11) and the assembly. The rescaling by the column sums is what makes the residual a weight vector at all: any vector in the orthogonal complement represents the same curvature, and dividing by its sum picks the one that is a barycentre — without it the eigenproblem would be solved on a basis rather than on the quantity it is meant to measure. The tol guard covers a column whose sum is zero, where the normalisation would divide by it.

    9. The eigenproblem, and the dispatch #

        vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(M)
    order = np.argsort(vals)
    return vecs[:, order][:, 1:1 + d], vals[order]

    eigh on a symmetric matrix returns ascending eigenvalues already, but the sort is explicit because the caller reads eigenvalues_ to compute the objective and to count null directions. 1:1 + d is the drop-the-constant rule of section 4 in one slice. The full spectrum is kept: it is what makes failure mode 2 visible after the fact, and what the demo asserts on.

            if self.variant == "hessian":
            M = _alignment_hessian(X, idx, d, self.hessian_tol)
        elif self.variant == "modified":
            M = _alignment_modified(X, idx, d, self.reg, self.modified_tol)
        else:
            M = _alignment_standard(_barycentric(X, idx, self.reg), idx)

    Three different matrices, one eigenproblem. standard and supervised share the barycentric path — supervised differs only in the distance matrix that produced idx — so the dispatch is exhaustive without a fourth branch.

    10. The supervised graph #

                labels = np.asarray(y)
            same = labels[:, None] == labels[None, :]
            D = D * np.where(same, 1.0, 1.0 + self.class_weight)

    (12), vectorised. Note the order: the scaling happens before the diagonal is set to inf and before the neighbours are chosen, so the graph is rebuilt from the weighted distances rather than filtered afterwards. That is what makes it a different graph and not a post-hoc edge deletion.

    Complexity #

    stage work implementation
    distances \(O(n^2D)\) one Gram matrix, sqrt of the clipped complement
    neighbours \(O(n^2)\) argpartition per row
    weights \(O(nk^3)\) one \(k \times k\) solve per point
    \(M\) \(O(n^3)\) dense scatter, A.T @ A; the block variants are \(O(nk^2)\)
    eigenproblem \(O(n^3)\) time, \(O(n^2)\) memory dense eigh

    Measured on the S-curve, one fit, \(k = 10\):

    \(n\) 250 500 1000 2000 4000
    standard 0.015 s 0.031 s 0.132 s 0.735 s 9.38 s

    At \(n = 2000\) the breakdown is \(0.050\) s for the distances and neighbours, \(0.018\) s for the weights, \(0.036\) s to assemble \(M\), and \(0.632\) s for eigh — 86% of the run in the eigen-solver, which is the \(O(n^3)\) term making itself known. Doubling from 1000 to 2000 costs \(5.6\times\); doubling again costs \(12.8\times\), as the \(n \times n\) matrix stops fitting in cache.

    This is the honest bill of the method: everything except the alignment product and the final eigenproblem is linear in \(n\) given a fixed \(k\), and those two are dense-cubic. A production implementation replaces the last line with a Lanczos solver for the few smallest eigenvectors — the same algorithm at a different complexity class — and keeps the graph sparse; here the dense version is what makes the code forty lines and the spectrum available for inspection. modified and hessian cost 8.17 s and 8.29 s at \(n = 4000\), faster than standard's 9.38 s because they assemble \(M\) blockwise and never form the dense \(I - W\).

    Failure modes #

    1. Minimising the objective is not the goal #

    The ridge table in section 2 is the cleanest statement: at \(\tau = 10^{-9}\) the objective is \(4 \times 10^{-16}\), and the embedding is worse than PCA. The objective is minimised by the weights that reconstruct exactly, and those weights cancel. Any implementation that treats exact reconstruction as correctness — or that reports the reconstruction residual as a quality measure — is measuring the wrong sign of the right quantity. reg = 0 does not even get an answer: LinAlgError: Singular matrix.

    2. A disconnected graph collapses the layout #

        nullity = int(np.sum(np.abs(wide.eigenvalues_) < 1e-10))

    The demo fits two clouds of 100 points twelve units apart at \(k = 8\). Every neighbourhood lies inside one cloud, so \(M\) is block diagonal and has 2 eigenvalues at zero — one per component. The two eigenvectors returned span the two indicator vectors: the first coordinate takes one value per cloud and varies within neither to \(3 \times 10^{-11}\), and the second is constant across the first cloud to \(0\), so that whole cloud collapses onto a single point. At \(k = 3\) the same data splits into 6 independently solvable patches, the first coordinate is exactly zero for half the points, and the objective is zero — the method reports success. The full spectrum is the only warning: count the eigenvalues below tolerance and compare with one per component.

    The fix is not a bigger \(k\) in general, since a gap that no \(k\) bridges is a statement about the data. It is to check connectivity, and to fall back on the component-wise solution or on a method that does not need a connected graph.

    3. \(k\) is a discrete decision with a discontinuous effect #

    On a 250-point swiss roll the eight-neighbour overlap is \(0.615\) at \(k = 6\), \(0.480\) at \(k = 8\), \(0.476\) at \(k = 10\), \(0.538\) at \(k = 12\) and \(0.431\) at \(k = 15\); the distance correlation follows the same non-monotone shape, \(0.813\), \(0.655\), \(0.425\), \(0.463\), \(0.372\). This is not a curve but a sequence of graphs, some of which are much better than their neighbours, and the best of them is at the smallest \(k\) tried.

    More points do not remove the problem. On the roll at \(k = 10\), going from 250 to 500 to 1000 points moves the distance correlation \(0.425 \to 0.757 \to 0.844\) while the eight-neighbour overlap goes \(0.476 \to 0.591 \to 0.537\). What improves is the global map, which is the quantity the objective constrains; the local metric does not converge at all.

    4. The variants are not uniformly better #

    modified is the textbook fix for the arbitrary weight vector, and on the roll it is worse than standard on both counts: \(0.385\) eight-neighbour overlap and \(0.236\) distance correlation, against \(0.476\) and \(0.425\). hessian buys the overlap (\(0.539\)) and loses the distances (\(0.265\)). On the S-curve both are ahead on overlap. A variant that stabilises a near-degenerate family can amplify a badly chosen \(s_i\) when the local spectra are noisy, and there is no guarantee to lean on: pick the variant on data that resembles the target, and read the spectrum before trusting the result.

    5. Reading the gauge as quality #

    The aspect table in section 5 is the measurement; the trap is using any single number. Neighbour overlap at a fixed radius is not invariant under the linear maps the method leaves free, so it understates the fit on anisotropic data by \(0.3\) or more (aspect 8: raw \(0.510\), the true distances recovered to \(0.9994\) after one map). It can also overstate: the S-curve correction reaches \(0.986\) while the short axis of the sheet is only \(0.23\) correlated, so a global statistic and a local one can disagree about the same layout in opposite directions. A pipeline that compares LLE against PCA or t-SNE on either number alone is comparing a gauge to an objective. Report distance correlation before and after an affine alignment, report overlap before and after, and say which gauge you are quoting.

    6. Supervised weighting can destroy the geometry #

    Labels are a prior, and a prior that is wrong or uninformative is damage. On the S-curve, whose labels are four bands of the parameter \(t\) and therefore already run along one axis of the answer, \(\lambda = 3\) leaves purity at \(0.838\) against \(0.919\) for the unweighted graph and drops eight-neighbour overlap from \(0.521\) to \(0.320\). On the spiral, where the labels cut across the geometry, the same knob takes purity from \(0.728\) to \(0.935\), and at \(\lambda = 5\) to \(1.000\). The variant is only as good as the assumption that class boundaries and manifold boundaries coincide.

    7. The Hessian variant has a hard floor #

    k >= 1 + d + d(d+1)/2 — six neighbours for two components, 12 for three. Below it the design (10) has more columns than rows, qr can return only \(k - d - 1\) directions where the curvature needs \(d(d+1)/2\), and the code raises rather than silently measuring a truncated curvature. A 5-nearest-neighbour graph is a perfectly ordinary choice for standard LLE and is not available here at all; a three-component embedding needs \(k \ge 12\) whatever the data says.

    Applications #

    • Face and expression images. The original Roweis–Saul demonstration: 64×64 photographs of one face, embedded to two dimensions that track pose and expression. The method's indifference to scale is a virtue there, since the lighting varies and the geometry should not.
    • Spectral initialisation and graph drawing. Because the output is a subspace, it can be used as an initialisation for methods that do care about the metric, and as a graph-drawing layout when only adjacency matters.
    • Local outlier scores. The per-point residual \(\lVert x_i - \sum_j w_{ij} x_j\rVert\) is a curvature-free measure of how badly a point fits its own neighbourhood, and it needs no distributional assumption about the rest of the data.
    • Sensor and manifold localisation. The weights are unchanged by global similarity, so embedding a set of local distance measurements recovers the configuration up to rotation and scale — which is exactly the gauge freedom a localisation problem has.
    • Feature extraction before a supervised model, in the era before representation learning made it redundant. It remains a useful baseline precisely because it fits nothing: no iterations, no learning rate, no random seed.
    • As an ancestor. Laplacian eigenmaps, the next atom in this chapter, is the same eigenproblem with \(M = L\) (the graph Laplacian) instead of \((I - W)^\top(I - W)\); LLE is the version where the matrix is chosen locally and optimally rather than from a fixed kernel.
