    08 · Manifolds & embeddings/tier 3/3 variants/source

    PHATE

    Intuition #

    The atom next door ends with a distance that saturates. A diffusion distance is the L2 gap between two rows of \(P^t\), weighted by the stationary distribution, and rows of a Markov operator are probability vectors: their entries are small, they decay geometrically with \(t\), and the difference between "very unlikely" and "impossible" is smaller than the difference between "likely" and "fairly likely". Two points at opposite ends of a data set end up at a distance that is mostly a statement about how many modes the walk has, not about where the points are.

    PHATE measures in the exponent instead. Given the diffused operator, define

    \[U = -\log P^t\]

    and use the ordinary Euclidean distance between rows of \(U\). The logarithm is not a smoothing trick; it changes what a distance means. Probabilities are compared multiplicatively, and the log turns a ratio into a difference:

       probability     1e-1        1e-3        1e-6        1e-9
   −log p          2.30        6.91       13.82       20.72

   each factor of 1000 is 6.91, wherever it happens on the scale, so the
   small end of the row is stretched until it can compete with the large end

    That single change is what makes the far field usable. A diffusion distance between two branches that the walk separates is dominated by the leading modes and stops growing; a potential distance keeps the whole row in play, so a pair of rows that differ anywhere differ in the potential, and the difference does not shrink to nothing as \(t\) grows. Multiplying \(P^t\) by a constant — which is most of what a longer walk does to a well-mixed operator — is an additive shift in \(U\), and distances ignore additive shifts. That is the sense in which the picture is robust to \(t\) where a diffusion map is not.

    The full pipeline, with the three design decisions that separate it from the atoms around it:

    graph LR
    A["X"] --> B["kNN graph<br/>k neighbours"]
    B --> C["kernel<br/>K = e^(−(d/σᵢ)^α)"]
    C --> D["walk<br/>P = D⁻¹K"]
    D --> E["P^t<br/>repeated squaring"]
    E --> F["potential<br/>U = −log P^t"]
    F --> G["distances<br/>‖Uᵢ − Uⱼ‖"]
    G --> H["metric MDS<br/>2 coordinates"]
    E --> I["spectrum<br/>Von Neumann entropy"]
    I --> J["auto-t<br/>knee point"]
    J --> E
    1. The bandwidth is per point. \(\sigma_i\) is the distance from \(i\) to its \(k\)-th neighbour, so \(d_{ij}/\sigma_i\) is a distance in units of local spacing. The kernel is then density-free by construction, which is the job diffusion maps gives to its \(\alpha\) normalisation — and it does it without an exponent to tune. On the folded sheet below, the kernel exponent itself turns out to be inert (0.002 of correlation between \(\alpha = 2\) and \(\alpha = 40\)); the bandwidth is the parameter that matters.
    2. The distance is in the exponent. Section 3 of the derivation shows the exact sense in which this stops the far field from collapsing.
    3. The embedding is MDS, not the eigenvectors. The potential distance is not a spectral distance, so there is no reason to believe its eigenvectors are its coordinates. Metric MDS on the \(n \times n\) distance matrix is the published choice, and the variant potential shows that it is optional — classical MDS gets you most of the way — while kernel_phate shows that the potential is not.

    The measured summary on the folded sheet the chapter uses throughout: PHATE keeps 0.516 of each point's ten nearest neighbours and correlates 0.770 with the sheet's own coordinates; the diffusion kernel on the same graph keeps 0.561 and correlates 0.702; PCA keeps 0.413 and correlates 0.872. The potential buys global geometry at the price of a little local fidelity, and the trade is controlled by one number, the exponent in the transform.

    Variants #

    The three variants are not three algorithms. They are three points in a two-dimensional design space — the potential exponent and the embedder — chosen so that each differs from phate in exactly one decision.

    variant potential embedder overlap @10 dist. corr. stress
    phate \(-\log P^t\) metric MDS 0.516 0.770 0.126
    potential \(-\log P^t\) classical MDS 0.502 0.783 0
    kernel_phate none (\(\gamma = -1\)) kernel PCA 0.561 0.702 0

    Four hundred points of an S-curve, \(k = 5\), \(t\) chosen automatically in all three rows. PCA on the same data scores 0.413 and 0.872. The three rows agree on the thing that matters — the potential is the source of the global structure — and disagree on the small print: classical MDS is a little better at distances than metric MDS here, because the potential distance matrix is nearly Euclidean and stress minimisation then has nothing left to fix. kernel_phate is the honest control. It skips the log entirely, and it keeps the most neighbours (0.561) and the least global geometry (0.702).

    The exponent that separates them is a one-parameter family, and because potential_transform is public the whole family can be measured without adding variants. The published family is the Box–Cox transform of the operator,

    \[\phi_\gamma(x) = \frac{x^{c}}{c}, \qquad c = \frac{1-\gamma}{2}, \qquad \phi_1(x) = \log x \qquad (1)\]

    with only the distances between rows of \(\phi_\gamma(P^t)\) ever used. Sweeping \(\gamma\) on the same sheet at \(t = 14\), embedding each by classical MDS:

    \(\gamma\) −1 −0.5 0 0.5 1
    potential dist. vs sheet 0.334 0.485 0.615 0.731 0.807
    overlap @10 0.575 0.568 0.541 0.547 0.502
    embedding vs sheet 0.699 0.702 0.694 0.722 0.783

    Monotone in one direction and monotone in the other: as \(\gamma\) rises the geometry gets better and the neighbourhoods get slightly worse. That is the whole parameter, and it is why the reference implementation exposes gamma between \(-1\) and \(1\) rather than exposing one potential.

    Derivation #

    1. The graph, with a bandwidth in local units #

    Edges are the \(k\) nearest neighbours, symmetrised. The weight on an edge is

    \[K_{ij} = \exp\!\Big(-\Big(\frac{\lVert \mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j \rVert}{\sigma_i}\Big)^{\!\alpha}\Big), \qquad \sigma_i = \text{distance from } i \text{ to its } k\text{-th neighbour} \qquad (2)\]

    with the kernel symmetrised by taking the larger of \(K_{ij}\) and \(K_{ji}\). The scale \(\sigma_i\) is what makes the exponent dimensionless, and the exponent is what makes the kernel a kernel: \(\alpha = 2\) is a Gaussian, and \(\alpha = 40\) — the reference default — is within a percent of a hard indicator, because \(0.9^{40} = 0.015\) and \(1.1^{40} = 45\).

    Two things follow, and only the first is obvious. First, a dense region gets a narrow kernel and a sparse region a wide one, so \(d/\sigma_i\) measures distance in units of "how far apart points here usually are" and the graph stops encoding sampling density. That is the same invariance diffusion maps obtains by dividing by \(q^\alpha\), obtained without an exponent to choose — which is why PHATE dropped its old alpha_decay parameter in favour of this kernel.

    Second, \(\alpha\) barely matters. On the folded sheet, \(\alpha = 2\), \(10\) and \(40\) give potential-distance correlations of 0.807, 0.806 and 0.807. The reason is visible in the code: every weight in a neighbourhood is between \(e^{-1} = 0.37\) and \(1\), so the row-normalised walk is nearly the unweighted graph regardless of \(\alpha\). What \(\alpha\) does change is the size of the neighbourhood, and that is n_neighbors.

    2. The walk #

    With \(d_i = \sum_j K_{ij}\),

    \[P = D^{-1}K, \qquad \pi_i = \frac{d_i}{\sum_j d_j} \qquad (3)\]

    exactly as in diffusion maps: row-stochastic, reversible with respect to \(\pi\), and similar to the symmetric matrix \(D^{-1/2} K D^{-1/2}\), so its spectrum is real and can be computed with eigh. Nothing about the potential needs the eigenvectors, and the variant that does not use them never touches the spectrum for anything except choosing \(t\).

    The diffusion operator at time \(t\) is the \(t\)-th power. It is computed by repeated squaring rather than from the spectrum, because the potential needs every entry of \(P^t\) to full precision: a spectral reconstruction has absolute error around \(10^{-16}\), and entries whose true size is \(10^{-14}\) would be reconstructed as noise — which the logarithm then amplifies.

    3. Why a diffusion distance saturates #

    Write the spectral expansion of the diffusion distance:

    \[D_t(i,j)^2 = \sum_{l \ge 1} \lambda_l^{2t}\big(\psi_l(i) - \psi_l(j)\big)^2 \qquad (4)\]

    Every term is weighted by \(\lambda_l^{2t}\), and the weights fall geometrically. Whatever the data says about the difference between two far-apart points, that information lives in the modes with \(\lambda_l\) well below the leading one, and those terms are exactly the ones that die as \(t\) grows. The distance does not grow to reflect a large separation; it shrinks, uniformly. Measured with the kernel variant on the sheet, the correlation between the embedding's distances and the truth is 0.697 at \(t = 1\), 0.701 at \(t = 10\) and 0.717 at \(t = 50\) — a ceiling, not a trend, because the leading two modes are all that is left.

    4. The potential #

    Define the potential matrix and the potential distance by

    \[U = -\log P^t, \qquad D_P(i,j) = \big\lVert U_i - U_j \big\rVert_2 \qquad (5)\]

    \(D_P\) is a genuine metric: it is the Euclidean distance between two rows of a real matrix, so symmetry, definiteness and the triangle inequality come free. The demo checks all \(512{,}000\) triangle inequalities of an 80-point example and finds a worst excess of exactly zero.

    The mechanism is that \(-\log\) is a monotone concave map applied entrywise. Two rows of \(P^t\) that differ by a factor of \(10^3\) in one coordinate differ by \(\log 10^3 = 6.9\) in the potential regardless of whether the entries are \(10^{-1}\) and \(10^{-4}\) or \(10^{-6}\) and \(10^{-9}\). A diffusion distance is dominated by the large entries, where a factor of \(10^3\) is a large absolute difference; a potential distance is dominated by the ratios, which are the same everywhere. Since the entries of \(P^t\) are the walk's transition probabilities, the potential distance asks "how much more likely is the walk from \(i\) to reach \(k\) than from \(j\)" and answers in nats.

    Its growth with \(t\) is the opposite of (3). Measured on the sheet, the correlation between the potential distance and the truth is

    \(t\) 1 2 5 10 25 50
    potential distances 0.123 0.261 0.566 0.764 0.848 0.894
    PHATE embedding 0.279 0.477 0.580 0.737 0.836 0.889
    kernel embedding 0.697 0.697 0.698 0.701 0.707 0.717

    Short walks are local, so the potential is local too and the picture is a smear; long walks let every entry of the row contribute, and the geometry keeps improving. The kernel starts better and finishes worse, which is the correct summary of the difference: at \(t = 1\) the diffusion distance already sees the local structure, and it never sees anything else.

    5. The Box–Cox family #

    The log is the endpoint of a family, and it is worth knowing what the rest of it does. Because only distances between rows are used, the transform is defined up to an affine change, so the natural family is the Box–Cox transform without its constant:

    \[\phi_\gamma(x) = \frac{x^{c}}{c}, \quad c = \frac{1-\gamma}{2}; \qquad \lim_{c \to 0} \frac{x^c - 1}{c} = \log x \qquad (6)\]

    The two are the same up to the additive \(1/c\), which distances ignore. The endpoints are the interesting part: \(\gamma = -1\) gives \(c = 1\) and recovers the operator itself, whose row distances are diffusion distances; \(\gamma = 1\) gives the log potential; \(\gamma = 0\) gives \(2\sqrt{P^t}\), the square-root potential the reference still accepts as potential_method="sqrt". Equation (6) is the reason the whole family is one line of code rather than three — and the reason phate and kernel_phate share a graph.

    The demo verifies the limit numerically: with \(c = 10^{-6}\), \((x^c-1)/c\) differs from \(\log x\) by at most \(1.7 \times 10^{-5}\) over the entries of a random \(P^t\), which is first order in \(c\) as the expansion predicts.

    6. Choosing \(t\) from the spectrum #

    The diffusion time is a scale, and the method wants the smallest one that has explored enough to be global. The published rule is the knee of the Von Neumann entropy of the diffused operator, and it has a property that makes it cheap: treating \(\rho_t = P^t / \mathrm{tr}(P^t)\) as a density matrix, its eigenvalues are \(\lambda_l^t / \sum_m \lambda_m^t\), so

    \[H(t) = -\sum_l p_l(t) \log p_l(t), \qquad p_l(t) = \frac{|\lambda_l|^t}{\sum_m |\lambda_m|^t} \qquad (7)\]

    depends on the spectrum alone. No matrix powers are needed to compute the curve; one eigendecomposition gives it for every \(t\) up to t_max. (The absolute value is there because a kNN-masked kernel has negative eigenvalues, and a density matrix does not.)

    \(H(0) = \log n\) exactly, and \(H(t) \to 0\) as one mode survives. The demo checks the first to \(10^{-12}\) and checks the curve against twenty matrix powers of \(P\) computed independently, to \(6.8 \times 10^{-15}\).

    The knee is found by rescaling \(H(t)\) onto the unit square, drawing the chord from the first point to the last, and taking the point farthest below it — the Kneedle rule. The published implementation fits two least-squares lines and minimises absolute error; on the sheet the two rules agree, and on the reference's own example curve, \(\exp(-t/10)\) over twenty points, both return \(t = 8\) of 20.

    7. Embedding the potential distances #

    The potential distance is not a spectral distance, so it has no eigenvectors to plot. Metric MDS finds coordinates \(X\) minimising the normalised stress

    \[\mathrm{stress}(X) = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i

    by iterating the Guttman transform

    \[X \leftarrow \frac{1}{n} B(X) X, \qquad B_{ij} = -\frac{D_P(i,j)}{d_{ij}}, \qquad B_{ii} = \sum_{j \ne i} \frac{D_P(i,j)}{d_{ij}} \qquad (9)\]

    Each step is the exact solution of the weighted least-squares problem with the distances held fixed, so the stress decreases monotonically and there is no learning rate, no momentum and no epoch count to tune — only an initial configuration, taken from classical MDS, which double-centres the squared distances and keeps the top eigenpairs:

    \[B = -\tfrac{1}{2} J D_P^2 J, \qquad J = I - \tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top \qquad (10)\]

    On a genuinely Euclidean \(D_P\) this is exact: the demo embeds 30 random 3-D points and recovers their distances to \(4.7 \times 10^{-9}\) relative. On a non-Euclidean \(D_P\) it is the closest Euclidean distance matrix in Frobenius norm, which is why it is a good starting point rather than a solution.

    8. Where the kernel variant lands #

    kernel_phate skips the potential, so it must embed a kernel rather than a metric, and kernel PCA is the spectral way to do that: for a kernel \(K_t = P^t\) the coordinates are the eigenvectors scaled by the square roots of the eigenvalues. With \(\psi_l = D^{-1/2}v_l\) the right eigenvectors and gains \(\lambda_l^{t/2}\),

    \[\big\lVert \psi_l \lambda_l^{t/2} \big\rVert^2_{\text{diff}} = \sum_l \lambda_l^{t}\big(\psi_l(i)-\psi_l(j)\big)^2 = D_{t/2}(i,j)^2 \qquad (11)\]

    so kernel PCA on the diffusion kernel at time \(t\) is a diffusion map at time \(t/2\) — the factor of two is the difference between a kernel, which is squared, and a distance, which is not. The demo confirms it directly: the kernel variant at \(t = 8\) reproduces the definition of the diffusion distance at \(t = 4\), with the \(1/\pi\) weights and all, to \(5 \times 10^{-4}\). The residual is not rounding: a kNN-masked kernel is not positive semidefinite, and kernel PCA requires one, so the negative eigenvalues are clipped. On that example they carry \(4 \times 10^{-3}\) of the spectral mass at \(t = 8\), which is exactly the size of the gap.

    That is also why the variant is worth having. It is the \(\gamma = -1\) endpoint of the family in (1), it needs no MDS, and it is measurably the worst of the three at global geometry — which is the empirical case for the logarithm.

    Code walkthrough #

    1. The adaptive kernel #

        scaled = distances / sigma[:, None]
    kernel = np.where(graph, np.exp(-(scaled ** decay)), 0.0)
    return np.maximum(kernel, kernel.T)

    Three operations. sigma[:, None] broadcasts the per-row bandwidth over the columns, so scaled[i, j] is the distance from i to j in units of i's own spacing — the whole point of (2), and the reason no global \(\varepsilon\) appears anywhere in the class. np.where(graph, ..., 0.0) restricts the kernel to the \(k\)-nearest-neighbour edges; without it the expression is a full Gaussian kernel and the "graph" is a fiction. The last line symmetrises by maximum, not by averaging: the weights on the two directions of an edge were computed with two different bandwidths, and the larger one is the one consistent with "either endpoint thought this pair was close". Averaging would shrink an edge that one endpoint considered important, which is the wrong direction for a graph.

    2. The potential, and the floor #

        if gamma == 1.0:
        return -np.log(np.maximum(power, 1e-12))
    c = (1.0 - gamma) / 2.0
    return power ** c / c

    Equation (5), with the log special-cased because it is the limit and not a member. The np.maximum is the one place where a numerical choice leaks into the geometry. P^t can contain exact zeros — a sparse walk at small \(t\), or entries that underflow after enough squaring — and -log 0 is infinite. The floor is what turns "impossible" into "very unlikely", and it sets the scale of the far field: two points whose rows are disjoint at time \(t\) are \(24.7\) apart in every coordinate they cannot reach, which is the plateau that makes the far field finite and puts a ceiling on how much a single pair can differ.

    The else branch is the rest of the family in one line, and its two constants are worth reading: c = 1 at \(\gamma = -1\) returns power unchanged, and c = 0.5 at \(\gamma = 0\) returns 2 * sqrt(power), which the demo asserts bit-for-bit.

    3. The entropy, in log space #

        log_weights = t[:, None] * np.log(lam)[None, :]
    log_weights = log_weights - log_weights.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    weights = np.exp(log_weights)
    prob = np.maximum(weights / weights.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True), 1e-300)
    return t, -np.sum(prob * np.log(prob), axis=1)

    Equation (7), computed the only way that survives t_max = 100. A direct lam ** t / (lam ** t).sum() underflows at large \(t\): at \(t = 99\) with \(\lambda = 0.99\) every weight in a row is \(0.37\), fine, but with a spectrum that starts at \(0.9\) they are all \(10^{-5}\), and one that starts at \(0.5\) gives \(10^{-30}\) — and then the ratio is \(0/0\), which is nan, which silently poisons the knee and therefore the embedding. Subtracting the row maximum first makes the largest weight exactly one, so the row always normalises. The 1e-300 on the next line does the same job for the log: p * log p with p underflowed is 0 * -inf.

    4. The knee #

        xn = (x - x[0]) / span_x
    yn = (y - y[0]) / span_y
    chord = yn[0] + (yn[-1] - yn[0]) * xn
    # A curve below the chord has a knee; a convex curve does not, and then the
    # largest distance is the answer all the same.
    return float(x[int(np.argmax(np.abs(yn - chord)))])

    Four lines of arithmetic and a comment: rescale both axes to the unit square, draw the straight line between the endpoints, and return the abscissa of the point farthest from it. The rescaling is not cosmetic — without it the answer depends on whether \(t\) is counted in steps or in hundreds of steps — and the absolute value is what makes the same code work for a curve that lies above its chord as well as below. For the entropy curve, which is decreasing and convex, the farthest point is where the decay stops accelerating, which is the scale at which the walk has stopped finding new modes.

    5. Classical MDS #

        centring = np.eye(n) - 1.0 / n
    gram = -0.5 * centring @ (distances ** 2) @ centring
    values, vectors = np.linalg.eigh(gram)
    order = np.argsort(values)[::-1][:n_components]
    kept = np.maximum(values[order], 0.0)
    return vectors[:, order] * np.sqrt(kept)[None, :]

    Equation (10), and the reason it is worth writing out: for a Euclidean distance matrix, \(B\) is the Gram matrix of the centred points, so its eigenpairs are the coordinates — this is a two-line proof that classical MDS is exact, and the demo's \(4.7 \times 10^{-9}\) is the numerical confirmation. For a non-Euclidean \(D\), \(B\) has negative eigenvalues, and they are where the "closest Euclidean" part of the claim lives: np.maximum(..., 0.0) discards the directions that cannot be realised in \(n_\text{components}\) dimensions rather than taking the square root of a negative number.

    6. The Guttman step, damped #

            ratio = distances / np.maximum(_pairwise(X), 1e-9)
        transform = -ratio
        np.fill_diagonal(transform, ratio.sum(axis=1))
        step = (transform @ X) / n - X
        alpha = 1.0
        for _ in range(20):
            trial = X + alpha * step
            new_stress = _stress(distances, trial, scale)
            if new_stress < stress:
                break
            alpha *= 0.5

    Equation (9) in the first four lines, and the rest is the guard that makes it usable. ratio is \(D_{ij}/d_{ij}\); negating it and putting the row sums on the diagonal produces \(B\) in (9) without ever forming a diagonal matrix — the row sum is \(B_{ii}\). Subtracting X turns the transform into a step, so the loop below can measure it.

    The backtracking exists because the Guttman step is only a descent direction. When two embedded points land on top of each other, \(D_{ij}/d_{ij}\) is enormous, the step is enormous, and the undamped iteration throws the configuration across the plane and back — the classic way a SMACOF implementation diverges on a non-Euclidean target. Halving the step until the stress actually falls guarantees a monotone sequence, and since the initial configuration is classical MDS, the result is never worse than the spectral answer. The demo reaches a normalised stress of 0.126 on the sheet; the variant potential skips the loop and reports zero by definition.

    7. The graph and its bandwidths #

            graph = _knn_graph(self.distances_, self.n_neighbors)
        self.sigma_ = np.sort(np.where(graph, self.distances_, np.inf), axis=1)[
            :, self.n_neighbors - 1]
        if np.any(self.sigma_ <= 0.0):
            raise ValueError("duplicate points: some point has zero distance to "
                             "its k-th neighbour, so its kernel is a delta")

    np.where(graph, distances, inf) followed by a sort takes the \(k\)-th smallest distances to graph neighbours — not the \(k\)-th smallest distance overall, which after symmetrisation would be the wrong neighbour for points that gained edges from the other direction. Reading column \(k-1\) rather than \(k\) is the off-by-one of the whole file: a point's own distance to itself is not in the masked matrix, so the \(k\)-th neighbour is the \((k-1)\)-th smallest entry.

    The check catches the one degeneracy that the kernel cannot survive. A duplicate point has \(\sigma_i = 0\), and then scaled is a division by zero: the kernel becomes a delta on that row, the walk leaves the point immediately, and the eigenvectors are dominated by a direction that is an artefact of the duplicate. It is much better to refuse than to hand back a plot.

    8. The walk, and the two embeddings #

            degree = self.kernel_.sum(axis=1)
        self.transition_ = self.kernel_ / degree[:, None]
        self.stationary_ = degree / degree.sum()

    Equation (3), unchanged from the atom next door. Then the branch that makes the variants:

                gains = np.maximum(self.eigenvalues_, 0.0) ** (self.t_ / 2.0)
            coords = (self.eigenvectors_ / np.sqrt(degree)[:, None]) * gains[None, :]

    This is (11). eigenvectors_ / sqrt(degree) is \(D^{-1/2}v\), the right eigenvector of the walk, and the gain is the square root of \(\lambda^t\) because kernel PCA coordinates are square roots. np.maximum(..., 0.0) is the clip discussed above: a fractional power of a negative eigenvalue is nan, and this kernel — a masked Gaussian — always has negative eigenvalues.

                self.potential_ = potential_transform(power, gamma=1.0)
            self.potential_distances_ = _pairwise(self.potential_)
            start = classical_mds(self.potential_distances_, self.n_components)

    The published path, in three lines: the transform, the \(n \times n\) distance matrix, the spectral starting point. phate then refines start with SMACOF and potential returns it. Note what is not here — no eigenvectors, no normalisation, no first index to skip. The potential distance is a distance between rows of an \(n \times n\) matrix, and every row includes the constant mode like every other, because the log does not care about the spectral decomposition at all.

    Complexity #

    For \(n\) points in \(m\) dimensions, \(k\) neighbours, \(t\) diffusion steps and \(d\) output dimensions:

    stage time memory
    pairwise distances \(O(n^2 m)\) \(O(n^2)\)
    neighbour mask and bandwidths \(O(n^2)\) \(O(n^2)\)
    kernel and walk \(O(n^2)\) \(O(n^2)\)
    spectrum \(O(n^3)\) \(O(n^2)\)
    \(P^t\) by repeated squaring \(O(n^3 \log t)\) \(O(n^2)\)
    potential distances \(O(n^3)\) \(O(n^2)\)
    metric MDS \(O(n^2 d)\) per iteration \(O(n^2)\)

    Two cubics, and neither is inherent. The spectrum is only needed to choose \(t\) with t="auto"; with a fixed \(t\) the class never calls eigh, and the variant kernel_phate is the only one whose coordinates come from eigenvectors. The \(P^t\) cost is a user choice too: when \(t\) is a few steps, repeated squaring is cheaper than the cubic, and a sparse implementation of the walk would make it \(O(nk \log t)\) — which is how the published version handles \(10^5\) cells. The potential distance matrix is the real wall: \(n = 10^5\) would need 80 GB for that matrix alone, whatever the arithmetic. Measured on the sheet, \(k = 5\), t="auto", best of two:

    \(n\) 200 400 800 1600
    fit time 0.011 s 0.041 s 0.193 s 1.08 s

    Ratios of 3.7, 4.7 and 5.6 for a doubling of \(n\): the cubic is approached from below, as it always is when fixed costs and cache behaviour compete with the asymptote.

    Failure modes #

    A folded sheet, and the ceiling of a monotone filter #

    The S-curve is the chapter's standard hard case, and PHATE does not solve it. Four hundred points of the sheet, \(k = 5\) where the method takes one, two coordinates, sorted by neighbourhood fidelity:

    method overlap @10 distance corr.
    t-SNE, perplexity 30 0.874 0.980
    UMAP, 5 neighbours 0.685 0.586
    Isomap, 5 neighbours 0.547 0.930
    diffusion map, \(k = 5\) 0.547 0.710
    PHATE, auto-t = 14 0.516 0.770
    PCA 0.413 0.872

    Read the last two columns as a trade. PHATE loses 0.031 of overlap to the diffusion map next door and buys 0.060 of distance correlation for it — the log is doing exactly what it was introduced to do, and nothing more. Against the methods that are not functions of the graph's spectrum it loses both columns: t-SNE and Isomap are not refinements of the walk. The reason is structural: the potential is a monotone transform of the same operator, so it cannot create an ordering the graph does not have. t-SNE adds repulsion between non-neighbours, which is a force the walk does not contain; Isomap builds geodesics, which is a long-range operation the walk does not contain. A monotone filter of a graph can only choose which of the graph's scales to show.

    Too little diffusion, and the auto-\(t\) rule #

    At \(t = 1\) the potential distance correlates 0.123 with the sheet, and the embedding looks like a smear. Nothing is wrong with the code — \(P^1\) is the graph, its rows are one-hop neighbourhoods, and the log of a sparse row is mostly the floor. The method needs the walk to have explored before its rows mean anything global, which is why t="auto" exists and why it picked 14 here.

    The rule's weakness is that the knee of \(H(t)\) is a property of the spectrum, and a spectrum with many modes near one — the sheet has 0.9996, 0.9968, 0.995, … — decays slowly and pushes the knee late. On this data late is fine (the potential at \(t = 25\) scores 0.848). On data where the interesting scale is shorter than the knee, the rule will over-smooth, and the honest procedure is to look at the embedding at a few \(t\) and check that the picture is stable — which it is: the rank correlation between the potential distances at \(t = 10\) and \(t = 50\) is 0.852, and the embedding's score moves by 0.152 across that window.

    A disconnected graph #

    Two clumps of five points a hundred units apart, two neighbours each, \(t = 3\): nothing raises. The unit eigenvalue has multiplicity two, the walk never crosses, and every cross-clump entry of \(P^3\) underflows to the floor. The consequences are worth stating exactly, because they look like a result: nothing inside a clump is more than 1.4 from anything else in it, all twenty-five pairs across the clumps sit between 81.7 and 82.2, and the gap is set by the floor of the log. MDS will place the two clumps a sensible distance apart and the plot will look like a finding about the relationship between two populations. The diagnostic is eigenvalues_[1]: if it is one, the graph is disconnected and no picture of the relative placement of the components means anything.

    Duplicates and near-duplicates #

    A duplicated point has \(\sigma_i = 0\) and the constructor's check raises. A nearly duplicated point is worse, because it passes: \(\sigma_i\) is tiny, the row of the kernel is a spike, and the point's potential row is dominated by one entry. That is not wrong in the mathematics — the point really is a spike — but it is a large leverage point in the MDS, and one duplicate pair can bend the layout. Deduplicate before fitting, or accept that the two copies will sit on top of each other.

    The floor, and how far the far field can reach #

    The \(10^{-12}\) in potential_transform is a choice, and it is the one parameter the doc has not argued for. Entries below it are indistinguishable, so every pair of points whose rows are disjoint at time \(t\) is at the same potential distance — the plateau in the classified-clumps example above. A smaller floor (like the reference's \(10^{-7}\) on a row-stochastic operator) moves the plateau down and keeps more of the tail in play; a larger one compresses the far field sooner. It does not change the local structure, which is why the method is not sensitive to it in the regime where it works, and it does change whether two disconnected components look far apart or merely separate.

    Choosing the exponent instead of the bandwidth #

    Since \(\alpha\) is inert (0.002 of correlation between 2 and 40) and n_neighbors is not, tuning \(\alpha\) is a way to spend an afternoon learning nothing. The neighbourhood size is the resolution of the graph:

    \(k\) 3 5 10 20
    overlap @10 0.596 0.516 0.402 0.402
    distance corr. 0.657 0.770 0.944 0.972

    Small \(k\) keeps neighbourhoods and loses distances; large \(k\) keeps distances and blurs the plot into a few global modes. The default 5 is the reference's choice for single-cell data, where the local structure is the point; for a continuous manifold with a million points, 20 is not unreasonable.

    Applications #

    • Single-cell developmental trajectories. The motivating use. A differentiation experiment is a tree of cell states with a small number of branches, and the picture you want shows the branches joined at their progenitors rather than as separate islands. The potential makes the branches comparable in distance to the trunk, which a diffusion distance does not.
    • Any Markov chain. Nothing in (5) needs the matrix to have come from points. Given a transition matrix over states — a random walk on a graph, a Markov state model of a protein, a user-flow model — the potential distance is a metric on states, computed with one matrix power and one log.
    • Choosing a scale from the spectrum. The Von Neumann entropy rule in (7) is the cheapest part of the method to reuse: any diffusion time in any method downstream of a graph can be chosen by the knee of \(H(t)\) without running the embedding at each candidate.
    • Manifold learning for downstream models. A two- or three-coordinate PHATE embedding is a feature map that preserves branching, and it is used as an input to clustering and trajectory inference where t-SNE's clumping would destroy the topology. It is deterministic given the data, unlike t-SNE.
    • Denoising and imputation. The same operator powers MAGIC, which replaces each cell's expression with its diffused value — a different downstream use of \(P^t\), and a reminder that the operation is a general smoothing of functions on the graph.
