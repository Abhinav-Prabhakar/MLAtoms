    08 · Manifolds & embeddings/tier 1/5 variants/source

    UMAP

    Intuition #

    t-SNE in this chapter answers "which points are neighbours?" with a probability and then scores a layout with a KL divergence. UMAP asks the same question and then scores the layout with cross entropy, which is the one substantive difference between the two methods.

    The KL of t-SNE is one-sided. If a pair is an edge in the graph and the layout puts the points far apart, the KL is large; if the layout invents an edge the graph does not have, the KL is nearly free. That is why t-SNE plots look like archipelagos: clusters are tight and the space between them is whatever the optimiser happened to leave. Cross entropy charges for both mistakes. A spurious edge costs, a missing edge costs, and the balance is what makes UMAP's layouts hold their global arrangement better — and what makes its mid-range neighbourhoods survive, which is measurable: on the demo's swiss roll UMAP keeps \(0.834\) of each point's forty nearest neighbours where t-SNE keeps \(0.769\).

    The other half is the fuzzy graph. A k-nearest-neighbour graph is a hard object: an edge exists or it does not. UMAP gives every edge a membership in \([0, 1]\) and normalises each point's row so that it carries a fixed amount of mass, which makes the graph a fuzzy simplicial set — a simplicial complex whose simplices have memberships instead of existence.

       kNN graph, hard                    fuzzy graph, memberships
   ·───·                                  1.00
   │  ╱                               ╱        ╲ 0.42
   ·─·   ·                          0.83   0.07

    That construction is what makes the method density-agnostic in the input and density-destroying in the output. Every point's row carries \(\log_2 k\) of mass, so a dense region and a sparse region are described by the same amount of graph, and the layout allocates them the same area. That is a feature for visualisation and a bug for anything that cares about density — which is what the densmap variant exists to fix.

    The demo measures the claim and the bill. On 250 points from a swiss roll — a surface that is exactly isometric to a flat rectangle — the layout keeps \(0.833\) of each point's eight true neighbours, against \(0.489\) for the leading two principal components. Its distance correlation with the rectangle is \(0.534\), so it is a neighbour map and not a ruler, and it is a better ruler than t-SNE's \(0.335\) without being a good one: Isomap, in this same chapter, reaches \(0.9996\).

    Variants #

    Variant The objective What changes Cost per epoch
    umap (4), complement sampled the reference \(O(nk\cdot r)\) terms
    fuzzy_simplicial (4), complement enumerated exact gradient, dense \(O(n^2)\)
    supervised (4) with label targets weights become agreements \(O(nk\cdot r)\)
    densmap (4) plus a radius term local density survives \(O(n^2)\) for the radii
    parametric (4), then distilled a network replaces the free points training only

    umap is the published optimiser: positives from the graph, negatives sampled uniformly per edge, per-coordinate gradient clipped to \(\pm 4\), learning rate decaying linearly to zero. The demo's fit reaches cross entropy \(528\) from \(1905\) in \(500\) epochs.

    fuzzy_simplicial is the same objective with no sampling — every pair, once per iteration, as one product with the coefficient matrix. The demo validates the two implementations against each other: the matrix form and the per-pair form agree to \(8.8 \times 10^{-12}\), six finite-difference probes against the objective agree to \(4.4 \times 10^{-7}\), and the two solvers reach \(531\) and \(528\) with layouts that share \(0.891\) of their eight-neighbourhoods. The price is exact: \(31125\) pairs per iteration against \(13122\) sampled terms per epoch, and the ratio grows with \(n\) because the graph stays at \(O(nk)\).

    supervised replaces each edge's fuzzy weight with the label agreement: 1 within a class, 0 across. The kNN support still comes from the geometry, so the weights are "these points are eligible to be neighbours, and here is whether they should be". The demo uses five 8-dimensional Gaussian clusters whose centres are \(1.5\) apart and whose standard deviation is \(1.1\) — close enough that the plain graph mixes them — and the purity of the eight layout neighbours goes from \(0.386\) to \(1.000\). Nothing about the geometry changes; only the target the cross entropy is measured against.

    densmap (Narayan, Berger & Menon 2021) adds a term on the radius of each point's neighbourhood, so that regions the input sampling made dense stay dense. The demo's curve has its parameter drawn from a Beta distribution, and the correlation between log input radius and log embedding radius goes from \(0.054\) to \(0.786\) at a cross-entropy cost of \(362 \to 412\). The term here is a 1-D optimal transport of the radii rather than the published regression — see §11, where the regression is measured failing.

    parametric replaces the \(n\) free coordinates with a function of the input. Fit the ordinary layout as a teacher, then regress it through a small network; new points are placed by evaluating the network. The demo's teacher separates its six clusters at \(0.922\), the network reproduces it on training points at \(0.955\) eight-neighbour overlap, and the held-out points land in the right cluster \(0.767\) of the time against the \(0.967\) of a 10-neighbour average of the fitted coordinates. Distillation works; transform is not a substitute for refitting.

    Derivation #

    Write the input as \(X = (x_1, \dots, x_n) \subset \mathbb{R}^d\) and the layout as \(Y = (y_1, \dots, y_n) \subset \mathbb{R}^2\), and let \(d_{ij} = \lVert x_i - x_j \rVert\).

    1. A fuzzy neighbourhood per point #

    For each point, take its \(k\) nearest neighbours and give the \(j\)-th one the membership

    \[w_{j|i} \;=\; \exp\!\left(-\frac{d_{ij} - \rho_i}{\sigma_i}\right), \qquad (1)\]

    where \(\rho_i\) is the distance to \(i\)'s nearest neighbour and \(\sigma_i\) is chosen so that the row sums to a fixed amount:

    \[\sum_{j \in \mathrm{kNN}(i)} w_{j|i} \;=\; \log_2 k. \qquad (2)\]

    Subtracting \(\rho_i\) is not cosmetic. It makes the nearest neighbour's membership exactly 1, so every point is connected to the graph with full strength and no point can be isolated by a bandwidth choice. It also makes the exponent non-positive for every term, so the exponential cannot overflow.

    The target \(\log_2 k\) is the fuzzy analogue of t-SNE's perplexity: it fixes how much neighbourhood a point is allowed to have, regardless of how dense the sampling is around it. In a dense region \(\sigma_i\) comes out small, in a sparse region large, and both rows carry the same mass.

    2. Solving for the bandwidth #

    The mass is monotone decreasing in \(\sigma_i\) — wider kernel, more spread, more mass — so a one-dimensional search suffices:

    \[M_i(\sigma) \;=\; \sum_{j} \exp\!\left(-\frac{d_{ij} - \rho_i}{\sigma}\right), \qquad M_i(\sigma) \downarrow 1 \text{ as } \sigma \to 0,\quad M_i(\sigma) \uparrow k \text{ as } \sigma \to \infty. \qquad (3)\]

    Bisection on \(\sigma\) with a doubling search for the upper bracket. In the code all \(n\) searches run at once: the bracket is two vectors, and each iteration is one exponential and one sum over the \(n \times k\) neighbour block. Sixty-four iterations take the row sums to \(\log_2 k\) to machine precision, which the demo asserts.

    3. The fuzzy union #

    The directed memberships \(w_{j|i}\) are not symmetric: \(j\) can be among \(i\)'s neighbours while \(i\) is not among \(j\)'s. The undirected graph is their fuzzy union,

    \[w_{ij} \;=\; w_{j|i} + w_{i|j} - w_{j|i}\, w_{i|j}, \qquad (4)\]

    which is the probabilistic t-conorm "i or j is a neighbour". It is not the average: if either direction is certain, the union is certain, so a one-sided edge survives at full strength. That is what keeps the graph from developing direction-dependent holes at the edge of a sample.

    4. The layout kernel #

    The layout's membership for a pair is

    \[q_{ij} \;=\; \left(1 + a\, \lVert y_i - y_j \rVert^{2b}\right)^{-1}, \qquad (5)\]

    with \(a, b\) fitted so that (5) matches a target curve: membership 1 up to min_dist, then \(\exp(-(r - \mathrm{min\_dist})/\mathrm{spread})\). Two parameters for a curve with one knee is enough because the fitted function is itself a two-parameter family; for the default (0.1, 1.0) the fit gives \(a = 1.5770\), \(b = 0.8951\), matching the constants in the reference implementation to four digits, and the demo checks them.

    min_dist is the smallest distance the layout is willing to give two neighbours. It is the knob between "clusters are dots" and "clusters are blobs", and it is not a metric parameter: changing it changes how much room the optimiser may leave between points that the graph says are neighbours.

    6. The gradient #

    Differentiate (7) with respect to \(y_i\). For a pair at squared distance \(d^2 = \lVert y_i - y_j \rVert^2\) with \(A = a (d^2)^b\),

    \[\frac{\partial}{\partial d^2}\left(-w \log q\right) = \frac{w a b (d^2)^{b-1}}{1 + A}, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial d^2}\left(-(1-w)\log(1-q)\right) = -\frac{(1-w)b}{d^2 (1 + A)}, \qquad (8)\]

    and \(\partial d^2/\partial y_i = 2(y_i - y_j)\), so the force — the direction to move \(y_i\) in a descent step — is

    \[F_{ij} \;=\; \left[-\frac{2 w a b (d^2)^{b-1}}{1 + A} + \frac{2(1-w)b}{d^2(1+A)}\right](y_i - y_j). \qquad (9)\]

    The first coefficient is negative and points from \(i\) towards \(j\); the second is positive and pushes away. A weak edge with \(w = 0.3\) has a small pull and a large push, so it is only satisfied at a distance where the two balance — the equilibrium length in §5, now as an explicit force balance.

    7. Negative sampling #

    The second sum in (7) runs over \(n(n-1)/2\) pairs, of which only \(O(nk)\) have \(w > 0\). Enumerating it is what fuzzy_simplicial does; umap samples it, drawing negative_rate uniform indices per edge and rejecting draws that hit either endpoint. The estimator is unbiased for the complement's sum, and its variance is what the learning-rate decay is there to absorb.

    8. The optimiser #

    Three practices, none of them in the objective:

    • Spectral initialisation. The leading eigenvectors of the normalised affinity \(D^{-1/2} W D^{-1/2}\) are the low-frequency modes of the graph, and starting there is both cheap and deterministic. Each eigenvector's sign is fixed by making its largest absolute entry positive, because \(\pm v\) are equally valid and a LAPACK build may return either.
    • Per-chunk updates. Edges are visited in random order and applied in chunks of 64. Summing a whole epoch and applying it once makes a point with 17 incident edges move by 17 times a single edge's step; the demo's first version did that and the layout left its initial standard deviation of 10 for 87 in one epoch, where the kernel is flat and the run freezes at an eight-neighbour overlap of \(0.22\).
    • Clipping and decay. Each per-coordinate force is clipped to \(\pm 4\) and the learning rate falls linearly to zero. The clip is the only regularisation; the decay is what makes a fixed epoch budget converge rather than oscillate.

    9. Supervised targets #

    With labels \(c_i\), replace the membership in (6) by

    \[w_{ij} \;=\; \mathbb{1}[c_i = c_j] \cdot \mathbb{1}[(i,j) \in E], \qquad (10)\]

    where \(E\) is the kNN support. Positives are same-class edges; the complement is everything else, sampled as before. The geometry of \(X\) now only decides which pairs are eligible; the objective decides where they go. That is why the result is a picture of the labels rather than of the data, and why the demo's purity reaches \(1.000\) while the layout's distances stop meaning anything.

    10. Density preservation #

    UMAP's membership normalisation (2) is exactly what makes the layout density-blind, so preserving density needs a second term. DensMAP's is built on the mean radius of each point's \(k\)-neighbourhood,

    \[r_i \;=\; \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j \in \mathrm{kNN}(i)} \lVert y_i - y_j \rVert, \qquad (11)\]

    and asks that the \(r_i\) keep the relative order and spacing they have in the input. The published form regresses \(\log r_i\) on the input's log radius and penalises the residual. That objective has a degenerate optimum: the regression is free to choose slope zero, and then flattening every radius drives the penalty to zero. Measured, the correlation goes from \(-0.14\) to \(-0.01\) while the cross entropy rises — uniform density, not preserved density, and the layout gets worse.

    What is implemented is the 1-D optimal transport of the same idea: sort the embedding's log radii and give point \(i\) the radius at its input rank,

    \[t_i \;=\; \left(\mathrm{sort}(\log r)\right)_{\mathrm{rank}(\log r_i^{\text{in}})}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{dens}} \;=\; \lambda \sum_i \left(\log r_i - t_i\right)^2. \qquad (12)\]

    The multiset of radii is then driven to the input's multiset up to a monotone transform, which is what "the relative densities are preserved" means, and no flat solution satisfies it. Its gradient is (11) differentiated through the radius, with the sign that shrinks a radius that is too large — a sign the first version of this file had backwards, which is why the demo's early attempt at a strong weight gave a correlation of \(-0.64\).

    11. Parametric #

    Replace \(y_i\) by \(f_\theta(x_i)\) with a one-hidden-layer network

    \[f_\theta(x) \;=\; W_2 \tanh(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2, \qquad (13)\]

    fit to the teacher's coordinates by least squares and then Adam. The demo's result is the honest one: reproduction \(0.955\) on training points, \(0.767\) purity on held-out points against the neighbour average's \(0.967\). A layout is not a smooth function of \(x\); a network that is smooth can memorise the training layout and still be a poor interpolator, and adding capacity moves the two numbers in opposite directions.

    12. What is identified #

    \(Y\) is identified up to rotation, reflection and translation, and the scale is fixed only weakly by min_dist. Cluster areas and inter-cluster distances are artefacts of the optimiser and the initialisation, not properties of the data — the same caveat as t-SNE, with a different constant. The graph's weights are identified up to the choice of \(k\), which changes what "neighbour" means.

    lo = np.zeros(n)
    hi = np.full(n, np.inf)

    (3), for all points at once. diff is dk - rho[:, None], already sorted in each row, and target is \(\log_2 k\). The bracket per point is two scalars in two vectors; a point that has not yet found a finite upper bound doubles, and the rest bisect. The returned block is (1); the caller scatters it into a matrix by neighbour index.

    2. The graph, and its union #

    A = np.zeros_like(D)
    np.put_along_axis(A, idx, memberships, axis=1)

    (4), one line. A is the directed membership matrix; the union is its sum minus the product, which is the t-conorm. put_along_axis writes each row's sorted memberships at the neighbour indices returned by _knn, and the D the caller passes has an infinite diagonal, so no point is its own neighbour.

    3. Fitting the kernel #

        p = np.log(np.array([1.5, 0.9]))
    for _ in range(64):
        r = residual(p)
        J = np.empty((x.size, 2))
        for j in range(2):
            step = np.zeros(2)
            step[j] = 1e-6
            J[:, j] = (residual(p + step) - r) / 1e-6
        move = np.linalg.lstsq(J, r, rcond=None)[0]
        p = np.clip(p - np.clip(move, -0.5, 0.5), [-4.0, -3.0], [4.0, 3.0])

    Gauss–Newton on \(\log a, \log b\), so positivity is automatic. The step is clipped to half a log-unit and the parameters are clamped: without that, a Newton step from a bad start makes \(x^{2b}\) overflow and the least-squares solve fails outright. The starting point (1.5, 0.9) is a warm start near the answer for the usual min_dist; from a point further away the iteration still converges but needs more of the 64 steps, and from the wrong sign it walks into the clamp.

    4. The objective #

        pos = w > 0.0
    neg = w < 1.0
    total = 0.0
    if pos.any():
        total += float((w[pos] * np.log(w[pos] / q[pos])).sum())
    if neg.any():
        total += float(((1.0 - w[neg])
                        * np.log((1.0 - w[neg]) / (1.0 - q[neg]))).sum())
    return total

    (6), evaluated densely over the upper triangle. The two masks are the limits that keep the logs finite: a pair with \(w = 0\) contributes only the second term and a pair with \(w = 1\) only the first, so neither needs a \(0 \log 0\). q is clipped away from both 0 and 1 first, because a pair of coincident layout points has \(q = 1\) exactly and would otherwise return an infinity that a caller cannot recover a gradient from.

    5. The per-pair gradient #

        d = Y[rows] - Y[cols]
    d2 = np.einsum("ij,ij->i", d, d)
    denom = 1.0 + a * np.maximum(d2, 0.0) ** b
    safe = np.maximum(d2, 1e-12)
    pull = -2.0 * w * a * b * safe ** (b - 1.0) / denom
    push = 2.0 * (1.0 - w) * b / (safe * denom)
    g = (pull + push)[:, None] * d
    return g if clip is None else np.clip(g, -clip, clip)

    (9), for a batch of pairs. safe guards \(d^{2(b-1)}\) at coincidence, where \(b < 1\) makes the attraction's derivative infinite; clip is the optimiser's \(\pm 4\) and is None in the demo's gradient check, which is what lets the check reproduce the objective's derivative exactly.

    6. The dense gradient #

        C = (W * (-2.0 * a * b * safe ** (b - 1.0) / denom)
         + (1.0 - W) * (2.0 * b / (safe * denom)))
    np.fill_diagonal(C, 0.0)
    return np.clip(Y * C.sum(axis=1)[:, None] - C @ Y, -4.0, 4.0)

    (9) for all pairs, as a matrix product: \(F_i = \sum_j c_{ij}(y_i - y_j)\) is \(y_i \sum_j c_{ij} - (C Y)_i\). The diagonal is zeroed because \(c_{ii}\) would be the repulsion of a point from itself, which is infinite at zero distance. This is the whole of fuzzy_simplicial — one product per iteration, quadratic, and the reference the sampled variant is checked against.

    7. Spectral initialisation #

        d = np.maximum(W.sum(axis=1), EPS)
    dinv = 1.0 / np.sqrt(d)
    M = dinv[:, None] * W * dinv[None, :]
    vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(M)
    order = np.argsort(vals)[::-1]
    take = order[1:n_components + 1]        # drop the constant eigenvector
    V = vecs[:, take]
    signs = np.sign(V[np.argmax(np.abs(V), axis=0), np.arange(V.shape[1])])
    V = V * np.where(signs == 0.0, 1.0, signs)
    scale = float(np.abs(V).max())
    return V / max(scale, EPS) * 10.0

    The normalised affinity, its leading eigenvectors, and a sign convention. The order[1:] is the part that was wrong first: eigh returns eigenvalues ascending, so the last column is the constant eigenvector and taking range(1, ...) selects the highest-frequency modes — noise. Starting there gave a layout whose standard deviation was \(0.9\) instead of \(10\), most points piled at the origin, and an eight-neighbour overlap of \(0.22\); the fix moved the same demo to \(0.83\). Scale to a maximum absolute coordinate of 10, which is the reference implementation's convention.

    8. The density term #

        log_r = np.log(np.maximum(radius, EPS))
    target = np.sort(log_r)[ranks]
    c = -weight * (log_r - target) / (np.maximum(radius, EPS) * k)

    (12). ranks is the input radius rank of each point, computed once; target is the embedding radius that currently sits at that rank. The minus sign is the difference between a gradient and its negative: with +, a point whose radius is too large is pushed further out and the demo's correlation went to \(-0.64\). The scatter adds each point's contribution to the radius of its own neighbourhood and subtracts it from each neighbour's, because \(r_i\) depends on every \(y_j\) in \(i\)'s neighbourhood.

    9. The sampled loop #

            for epoch in range(self.n_epochs):
            lr = self.learning_rate * (1.0 - epoch / self.n_epochs)
            order = rng.permutation(rows.size)
            for start in range(0, rows.size, chunk):
                take = order[start:start + chunk]
                r, c, wt = rows[take], cols[take], w[take]
                neg = rng.integers(0, n, size=(r.size, self.negative_rate))
                neg = np.where(neg == r[:, None], (neg + 1) % n, neg)
                neg = np.where(neg == c[:, None], (neg + 1) % n, neg)
                heads = np.concatenate([r, np.repeat(r, self.negative_rate)])
                tails = np.concatenate([c, neg.ravel()])
                weights = np.concatenate(
                    [wt, np.zeros(r.size * self.negative_rate)])
                g = _pair_gradients(heads, tails, weights, Y, self.a_, self.b_)
                _apply(Y, heads, g, lr)
                _apply(Y, tails, -g, lr)

    The optimiser. Each chunk holds its positives and their sampled negatives in one pair list — weights is the fuzzy weight for a positive and zero for a negative, which is all the difference (9) needs. _apply scatters with bincount, so a point that appears twice in a chunk gets both contributions; fancy assignment would silently keep only the last. The learning rate decays across the outer loop, and chunk = 64 is the sequential coupling that keeps one epoch from moving a point by the sum of all its forces.

    10. The parametric readout #

            self.mu = X.mean(axis=0)
        self.sd = X.std(axis=0) + EPS
        Xs = (X - self.mu) / self.sd
        n = X.shape[0]
        A = np.tanh(Xs @ self.W1.T + self.b1)
        self.b2 = Y.mean(axis=0)
        self.W2 = np.linalg.lstsq(A, Y - self.b2, rcond=None)[0].T

    (13), with the readout solved rather than trained. W1 is random and fixed, so tanh(W1 x) is a fixed feature map and the best linear function of it is one lstsq; Adam then moves the features themselves. Centring Y before the solve is what makes the intercept meaningful — fitting Y directly and adding the mean afterwards overshoots, which is a real mistake the first version made.

    Complexity #

    \(n\) samples, \(d\) input dimensions, \(k\) neighbours, \(r\) negatives per edge, \(T\) epochs, \(E \approx nk\) edges.

    Operation Time Memory Note
    pairwise distances \(O(n^2d)\) \(O(n^2)\) Gram identity
    kNN \(O(n^2\log k)\) \(O(n^2)\) argpartition per row
    bandwidth search \(O(64\,nk)\) \(O(nk)\) vectorised bisection
    fuzzy union \(O(n^2)\) \(O(n^2)\) one masked update
    spectral init \(O(n^3)\) \(O(n^2)\) dense eigh; a Lanczos solver is \(O(n^2)\)
    sampled epoch \(O(E(1+r))\) \(O(n)\) one chunk at a time
    dense iteration \(O(n^2)\) \(O(n^2)\) two matrix products
    density term \(O(nk)\) after \(O(n^2)\) radii \(O(n^2)\) radii recomputed each epoch
    parametric readout \(O(nh^2 + h^3)\) \(O(nh)\) one lstsq plus Adam
    full demo — — 10 fits, 11.1 s

    The \(O(n^2)\) rows are the fixed cost and the reason UMAP, like t-SNE, is a visualisation method. The spectral initialisation is the one that surprises people: a dense eigendecomposition is cubic, and at \(10^5\) points it is the bottleneck even though the optimiser is linear per epoch. Production implementations use a sparse Lanczos solver on the graph, which is what makes the whole pipeline near-linear.

    fuzzy_simplicial is the honest statement of the objective: \(O(n^2)\) per iteration, no sampling variance, and the demo measures \(2.4\) times more terms per pass at \(n = 250\) than the sampled variant. The ratio grows with \(n\), because the graph's support stays at \(O(nk)\) while the complement does not.

    Failure modes #

    1. Treating the target as an average instead of a log. The row sum in (2) is \(\log_2 k\), not \(k\). Setting it to \(k\) makes every membership larger — at \(k = 15\) the graph's total weight goes from \(1463\) to \(4374\) — so the cross entropy's first term dominates, the layout contracts to a standard deviation of \(3\) where the correct run keeps \(10\), and the eight-neighbour overlap falls from \(0.833\) to \(0.693\) with the cross entropy rising from \(528\) to \(3261\). The optimiser cannot satisfy the inflated weights at any layout, so it settles for a tight picture with the wrong neighbourhoods in it.

    2. Summing a whole epoch's forces. The per-edge update is a step; the sum of an epoch's per-edge steps is not. Doing it in one batch moved the demo's layout from a standard deviation of 10 to 87 in a single epoch, past the scale where the kernel has structure, and the run then froze at an eight-neighbour overlap of \(0.22\). The chunks in §9 exist because of this measurement, and the fact that a larger chunk (512 edges) reproduces the same failure is the demonstration that it is about coupling, not about vectorisation.

    3. A spectral initialisation that selects noise. eigh returns ascending eigenvalues, so the constant eigenvector is the last column. Taking the first \(k\) after index 1 selects the highest-frequency modes, piles most points near the origin, and costs \(0.6\) of eight-neighbour overlap — with no error and no warning, just a bad picture.

    4. A regression with a flat optimum. The published density term's best slope can be zero, and then uniform density satisfies it. Measured here, the correlation between input and embedding local radius went from \(-0.14\) to \(-0.01\) at the cost of a worse cross entropy, which looks like a working regulariser and is not one. The rank-matching form in §10 has no such solution.

    5. A sign error in the density gradient. In the first version the force grew the radius that was already too large. At a strong weight the layout inverted: the correlation was \(-0.64\) rather than \(+0.8\). A regulariser that makes its own metric worse is always a sign error, never a tuning problem.

    6. A floor that is not in the objective. The reference implementation's \(1/(0.001 + d^2)\) in the repulsion is a regularisation, not the derivative of the cross entropy. It is fine for the optimiser and it breaks any gradient check at the \(5\%\) level — which is how it was found here: the finite-difference check disagreed by \(0.4\%\) of the force's scale on every entry until the floor was removed and replaced by a guard at \(10^{-12}\).

    7. Supervised mode read as an embedding. The demo's purity reaches \(1.000\) by construction: the objective is built from the labels, so the layout is a picture of them. Using it for anything beyond classification triage — cluster sizes, distances, "how separate are these classes really" — reads information into the picture that the optimiser put there.

    8. min_dist read as a metric property. It controls how tightly the layout packs neighbours, not how faithful it is. Lower is not better: it makes clusters dots and the space between them arbitrary, which is fine for a picture and wrong for any downstream distance.

    9. Trusting transform. The demo's network reproduces its teacher at \(0.955\) on the training points and places \(0.767\) of held-out points correctly, against \(0.967\) for a 10-neighbour average. Adding capacity raises the first and lowers the second. A parametric variant is for placing many new points cheaply, not for a better map.

    10. Reading density off the default layout. Membership normalisation (2) is designed to make the layout density-blind, so the area a cluster gets says nothing about how many points are in it. densmap is the fix and it is partial: the demo moves the radius correlation from \(0.054\) to \(0.786\), not to 1.

    Applications #

    • Single-cell genomics. The second canonical use after t-SNE, and now the default in several pipelines: the graph is built on a PCA of the expression matrix, n_neighbors around 15, and the layout is used to look at cell types and trajectories. The densmap variant is used when the question is about population sizes rather than cluster identity.
    • Feature-space audit before modelling. The supervised variant is a quick way to ask whether a label is learnable from a representation at all: if the supervised layout cannot separate the classes, no downstream classifier will.
    • Topic and document maps. n_neighbors in the tens and min_dist around \(0.1\) gives the standard "map of the corpus" picture, with the same caveat as t-SNE about distances.
    • Embedding quality comparisons. Because the cross entropy charges for spurious edges, UMAP layouts are a better instrument than t-SNE for "does this representation preserve mid-range structure" — the demo's \(0.834\) against \(0.769\) at forty neighbours is the measurement.
    • Interactive exploration at scale. The sampled optimiser is linear per epoch and the graph is \(O(nk)\), which is why UMAP is the method most often used on million-point datasets where t-SNE needs the FFT or Barnes–Hut machinery in the sibling atom.
    • Density-aware embeddings. densmap is used where cluster size is a biological or physical signal — cell population frequency, cluster mass in a particle detector — and where the default layout's density blindness would silently destroy it.

    Not for: distance-based downstream tasks, cluster-size estimation without the density term, or any pipeline where the layout must be a deterministic function of new data (transform is an approximation, and the ordinary variants have no transform at all).
