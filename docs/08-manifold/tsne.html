    08 · Manifolds & embeddings/tier 1/5 variants/source

    t-SNE

    Intuition #

    t-SNE is the answer to one question: what should a two-dimensional picture of high-dimensional data preserve?

    Not distances. A neighbourhood of 30 points around a sample in 500 dimensions cannot be drawn as 30 points at their original distances — the volume that fits 30 neighbours in 500 dimensions has a radius that maps to a circle far larger than the canvas. Preserving distances is impossible; the interesting content, which points are near which, is not.

       high-dimensional neighbourhood              the layout
        ·  ·                                    ·   ·
     ·   x   ·         →                   ·    x    ·
        ·  ·                                    ·   ·
   radius r, 30 points                    radius r', 30 points
   (distances 0.7 … 3.1)                  (whatever fits)

    So the method throws the distances away and keeps the probabilities. Each point gets a Gaussian around it, with the width chosen so that the Gaussian holds a fixed number of neighbours — that is the perplexity. The pair probabilities \(p_{ij}\) say how likely \(i\) and \(j\) are to be neighbours. The layout is then scored by how well its own pair probabilities \(q_{ij}\) match them, and the matching is done by gradient descent on a KL divergence.

    What \(q\) is matters as much as what \(p\) is. In the layout, the affinities are a Student-\(t\) kernel \((1 + \lVert y_i - y_j \rVert^2)^{-1}\), whose tail is heavy enough that a point can push a moderately distant cluster away without dragging its own neighbours along. That choice is the t in t-SNE, and it is the fix for the crowding problem: in two dimensions the available area grows too slowly to hold a high-dimensional neighbourhood, and a Gaussian \(q\) would rather collapse everything than leave a neighbour out.

    graph LR
    A["X ∈ ℝⁿˣᵈ"] --> B["pairwise squared distances"]

    The demo measures both halves of the claim on 250 points from a swiss roll, which is exactly isometric to a flat rectangle. The leading two principal components keep \(0.489\) of each point's eight true nearest neighbours; t-SNE keeps \(0.857\). And its distance correlation with the rectangle the roll unrolls to is \(0.335\), where Isomap — in this same chapter — reaches \(0.9996\). It keeps what it was asked to keep, and nothing else.

    Variants #

    Variant The repulsion sum What changes Cost per iteration
    exact all \(n^2\) pairs the reference one \(n \times n\) product
    barnes_hut quadtree cells \(\theta\) trades accuracy for terms \(O(n\log n)\) terms
    fft convolution on a grid grid_size sets resolution \(O(G^2\log G)\), any \(n\)
    multiscale exact averages the input affinities over perplexities one extra affinity pass
    parametric exact, then distilled a network replaces the \(n\) free points training only

    exact is the 2008 paper as published: every pair contributes to every force, and the cost is quadratic in the samples. At 250 points this is not a problem — a fit of 600 iterations takes about \(0.2\) s, and the dense kernel is already in cache, so one matrix product against it beats gathering the \(nk\) nonzeros. It is the reference every approximation is measured against, and it is what multiscale and parametric run internally.

    barnes_hut (van der Maaten 2013) groups distant points into quadtree cells and uses one term per cell. A cell is accepted when its side is below \(\theta\) times the distance to the query point; the error in the force is then \(O(\theta^2)\) relative. The demo measures it on a fixed layout: \(\theta = 0\) reproduces the exact force to \(1.0 \times 10^{-14}\), and the normaliser to \(5.8 \times 10^{-16}\) — the traversal carries no bookkeeping error at all. \(\theta = 0.25\) gives \(0.013\) force error at \(25814\) terms, \(\theta = 0.5\) gives \(0.052\) at \(13343\), and \(\theta = 1.0\) gives \(0.210\) at \(5882\), against \(62250\) pairs. The term count is the point: \(0.214\) of the pairs at 250 points, and \(0.341\), \(0.140\), \(0.049\) at \(n = 200, 800, 3200\). That is an asymptotic win and not a constant-factor one — this numpy traversal is slower than one BLAS matrix product until a few thousand points, which is why the demo's \(\theta = 0.5\) fit costs more than the exact fit it is compared to.

    fft (Linderman et al. 2019, FIt-SNE) turns the same sum into a convolution. Deposit the points on a grid, convolve with the kernel and its two first moments, read the field back — the convolution is evaluated by FFT at every lag at once, so the cost depends on the grid and not on the sample size. The demo holds one layout fixed and sweeps the grid: relative force error \(0.2594\) at \(32^2\), \(0.0752\) at \(64^2\), \(0.0213\) at \(128^2\), \(0.0051\) at \(256^2\). Each doubling buys a factor of four, which is the bilinear deposit's second order.

    multiscale (the multi-scale extension in the FIt-SNE paper) changes the input rather than the sum: average the conditional affinities over several perplexities, so one run carries coarse and fine neighbourhoods at once. On the roll it scores \(0.840\)/\(0.761\) at eight and forty neighbours against the single perplexity \(30\)'s \(0.857\)/\(0.769\) — \(98.7\%\) and \(99.0\%\) of the best scale in the mixture, without knowing which scale that is. It is a hedge, not an improvement.

    parametric replaces the \(n\) free coordinates with a function of the input. Fit the exact layout as a teacher, then regress it through a small network; new points are placed by evaluating the network. The demo's network reproduces the training layout at \(0.988\) eight-neighbour overlap and puts \(0.900\) of the held-out points nearest their own cluster. The baseline it has to beat is duller and better: a 10-neighbour average of the fitted coordinates scores \(1.000\) on the same held-out points. On a curved sheet both drop — \(0.428\) for the network against \(0.477\) for the neighbour average — and the ordering does not change.

    Derivation #

    Write the input as \(X = (x_1, \dots, x_n) \subset \mathbb{R}^d\) and the layout as \(Y = (y_1, \dots, y_n) \subset \mathbb{R}^2\).

    1. A Gaussian neighbourhood per point #

    Each point gets its own bandwidth \(\sigma_i\), and the conditional affinity of \(j\) to \(i\) is

    \[p_{j|i} \;=\; \frac{\exp\!\left(-\lVert x_i - x_j \rVert^2 / 2\sigma_i^2\right)} {\sum_{k \neq i} \exp\!\left(-\lVert x_i - x_k \rVert^2 / 2\sigma_i^2\right)}, \qquad p_{i|i} = 0. \qquad (1)\]

    A single bandwidth for all points would be wrong on data of uneven density: in a dense region the Gaussian would hold hundreds of neighbours, in a sparse one it would hold none, and the layout would be dominated by the dense region. Per-point bandwidths make the neighbourhood size a constant.

    2. Perplexity as an entropy target #

    The natural measure of "how many neighbours the Gaussian holds" is its entropy,

    \[H(P_i) \;=\; -\sum_j p_{j|i} \log p_{j|i}, \qquad (2)\]

    and t-SNE fixes it at \(\log \kappa\) for a parameter \(\kappa\), the perplexity:

    \[\mathrm{Perp}(P_i) \;=\; 2^{H(P_i)} \;=\; \kappa. \qquad (3)\]

    So a perplexity of 30 means the conditional distribution has the entropy of a uniform distribution over 30 points. The width is not a distance — it adapts to the local density, which is the whole point.

    3. Solving for the bandwidth #

    Let \(\beta_i = 1/2\sigma_i^2\) and \(d_{ij} = \lVert x_i - x_j\rVert^2\). The entropy is monotone decreasing in \(\beta_i\) — a narrower Gaussian is more peaked, so its entropy is smaller — which makes one-dimensional root finding enough. In the form the code uses, with \(Z_i(\beta) = \sum_{j \neq i} e^{-\beta d_{ij}}\),

    \[H_i(\beta) \;=\; \log Z_i(\beta) + \beta\,\frac{\sum_{j \neq i} d_{ij} e^{-\beta d_{ij}}}{Z_i(\beta)}, \qquad (4)\]

    and bisection on \(\beta\) drives \(H_i\) to \(\log \kappa\). Every point's bracket is independent, so the whole search is a vector of 64 iterations over the matrix rather than \(n\) scalar searches. Sixty-four steps is far more than needed; the demo's first assertion is that the resulting conditional rows sum to one, and the iteration reaches machine precision in about thirty.

    Two numerical details, each of which produced a wrong answer first. exp(-inf) is zero and harmless, but inf * 0 is nan — so the diagonal must be zero distance before the exponential, not infinite distance. And the distances are shifted per row so the nearest neighbour sits at zero: exp(-β d) underflows for any reasonable \(\beta\) on unnormalised data, and the shift leaves \(Z_i\) and \(H_i\) unchanged because it cancels between the numerator and the normaliser.

    4. Symmetrisation and sparsity #

    The joint distribution is the symmetrised conditional,

    \[p_{ij} \;=\; \frac{p_{j|i} + p_{i|j}}{2n}, \qquad (5)\]

    which sums to one over ordered pairs. The \(1/2n\) is not cosmetic: it puts \(P\) and \(Q\) on the same total mass, without which the KL below is minimised by shrinking the layout.

    The matrix is almost empty. At perplexity 30 a row has about 30 terms that matter, and the remaining 220 are smaller by hundreds of orders of magnitude. The code truncates each row to its \(3\kappa\) largest terms and renormalises. This is not an approximation at the level of the objective; it is what makes the attraction sum \(O(n\kappa)\) instead of \(O(n^2)\), and it is measurably better than the alternative. Flooring every entry of the dense matrix at \(10^{-12}\) — the obvious way to keep \(\log q\) finite — makes the matrix dense again, and a 400-point fit then takes \(6.0\) s instead of \(0.6\) s. The floor belongs on the support: \(p_{ij}\) is raised to \(10^{-12}\) only where it was already positive.

    5. The layout kernel #

    In the layout the affinities are

    \[q_{ij} \;=\; \frac{(1 + \lVert y_i - y_j \rVert^2)^{-1}}{\sum_{k \neq l}(1 + \lVert y_k - y_l \rVert^2)^{-1}}, \qquad (6)\]

    a Student-\(t\) with one degree of freedom. The tail matters. Suppose a point is surrounded by 30 neighbours that the map cannot fit at their true relative distances; with a Gaussian \(q\) the cheapest way to raise \(q\) for the 29 near ones is to pull all of them in, which collapses the cluster to a point. The heavy tail makes a moderately distant point cheap to leave out, so the arrangement that minimises the divergence is a spread-out cluster with its neighbourhood intact. This is the formal version of the crowding argument in the introduction.

    6. The objective #

    \[\mathrm{KL}(P \,\|\, Q) \;=\; \sum_{i \neq j} p_{ij} \log \frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}} \;=\; \sum_{i \neq j} p_{ij} \log p_{ij} - \sum_{i \neq j} p_{ij} \log q_{ij}. \qquad (7)\]

    The first term does not depend on \(Y\), so minimising the KL is maximising \(\sum p_{ij} \log q_{ij}\). The KL is asymmetric on purpose: a small \(p_{ij}\) with a large \(q_{ij}\) costs almost nothing, but a large \(p_{ij}\) with a small \(q_{ij}\) costs a lot. Nearby points in the input are pulled together; distant points are only weakly pushed apart. That is the local/global asymmetry of the method in one line.

    7. The gradient #

    With \(w_{ij} = (1 + \lVert y_i - y_j\rVert^2)^{-1}\) and \(Z = \sum_{k \neq l} w_{kl}\), the derivative of (7) with respect to \(y_i\) is

    \[\frac{\partial \mathrm{KL}}{\partial y_i} \;=\; 4 \sum_j (p_{ij} - q_{ij})\, w_{ij}\, (y_i - y_j). \qquad (8)\]

    That form is correct and is not the form the code computes, because it needs \(q\) — a full matrix — every iteration. Expanding \(q = w/Z\) splits it into two sums that the approximations can each evaluate without the matrix:

    \[\frac{\partial \mathrm{KL}}{\partial y_i} \;=\; 4\left[ \underbrace{\sum_j p_{ij} w_{ij} (y_i - y_j)}_{A_i} \;-\; \frac{1}{Z}\underbrace{\sum_j w_{ij}^2 (y_i - y_j)}_{F_i} \right]. \qquad (9)\]

    \(A_i\) is a product against the sparse \(P\) — \(O(n\kappa)\). \(F_i\) and \(Z\) are the repulsion, and they are what the three variants approximate. The descent step is \(-\partial \mathrm{KL}/\partial y_i\), so the return value is \(-4(A - F/Z)\).

    Two facts about (9) are load-bearing and both were wrong in the first version of this file.

    \(Z\) is a sum over ordered pairs, \(\sum_{k \neq l}\), matching \(P\), which sums to one over ordered pairs. The unordered sum \(\tfrac{1}{2}\sum w\) is half of it, and using it doubles the repulsion. A doubled repulsion pushes the layout past the scale where the kernel has any structure: the run expands, the forces balance at a huge scale, everything freezes, and the eight-neighbour overlap reads \(0.43\) where it should read \(0.86\).

    The \(1/Z\) sits on the repulsion term alone. Moving it onto both terms is a globally rescaled gradient — the same direction, invisible in the mathematics, and fatal in the code, because the learning rate and the momentum schedule are fixed numbers rather than derived quantities. The demo checks (9) against a central difference of (7), and that check is the only reason either bug was found.

    8. The optimiser #

    Three schedule details, none of which is in the objective:

    • Early exaggeration. For the first quarter of the run, \(p_{ij}\) is multiplied by 12. This multiplies the attraction, so each point pulls its neighbours into a tight clump and the clumps find their large-scale arrangement while the repulsion is weak. Releasing the factor then spreads each clump to its final size. Without it the layout converges to a single blurry blob on many datasets.
    • Momentum. \(0.5\) rising to \(0.8\) at the same moment. A layout that is still repositioning clusters should not carry that velocity into the fine phase.
    • Learning rate. \(n/12/4\) with a floor of 50 — the scheme from the scikit-learn implementation, which is chosen so the step size scales with the number of terms in the gradient rather than with the data's units.

    The classic per-parameter gains rule from the original paper is not used, and the reason is worth recording. Gains compare the sign of the update with the sign of the previous update, scale by \(1.2\) or \(0.8\), and need a guard against runaway growth. The version that compares against the displacement — which looks equivalent — collapses every gain to its floor and freezes the layout. The version that compares against the previous gradient works. Without a guard at all, a doubling of the same direction every iteration reaches \(1.9 \times 10^{10}\) and sends the layout to a scale of \(10^9\).

    The initial layout is the leading two principal components, scaled to unit deviation. It is a convenience and not a load-bearing choice, which is easy to get wrong: with (9) right, a unit-scale random start, a \(10^{-4}\) random start and the principal components all reach the same place (eight-neighbour overlap \(0.841\), \(0.837\), \(0.857\)). Initialisation only looked decisive while the normalisation was wrong, because a wrong objective has a different equilibrium scale and the run then spends its exaggeration phase travelling to it.

    9. Barnes–Hut #

    For the repulsion, a cell containing a cloud of points at centre \(c\) and total mass \(m\) acts as one body at \(c\), provided the query point is far enough away:

    \[\frac{2h}{r} \;<\; \theta, \qquad (10)\]

    where \(h\) is the cell's half-width and \(r\) the distance from the query point to its centre. The neglected multipoles are the cell's internal spread, so the error is second order in the ratio: \(O(\theta^2)\) relative. Two guards are needed beyond (10): a cell containing the query point is never accepted (it stands in for a cloud that includes the query, and its self-interaction would be counted), and a leaf is accepted only when it is not the query point itself.

    The tree is built by sorting points into quadrants level by level. Points that are equal to the last bit of a float would split forever, so a node at the depth cap becomes one body at its points' mean — the right answer, since those points are indistinguishable to every other point.

    10. FFT #

    Write the repulsion as a sum over a density. If \(\rho(c)\) is the number of points deposited in cell \(c\), then at cell \(c\) the field is

    \[\phi_c \;=\; \sum_{c'} \rho_{c'}\, k(c - c'), \qquad k(\delta) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + \lVert \delta \rVert^2}, \qquad (11)\]

    and the two force components use \(k^2 \delta_x\) and \(k^2 \delta_y\) as kernels. A convolution of this shape is one multiplication in Fourier space, and the FFT evaluates it at all lags for the price of a transform of the grid,

    \[\phi \;=\; \mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left( \mathcal{F}(\rho)\, \mathcal{F}(k) \right). \qquad (12)\]

    Three implementation facts. The kernel must be ifftshifted so that zero lag sits at index zero, or the field is rotated by half a grid. The transform is padded to \(2G - 1\) so the linear convolution is not aliased into a circular one. And each point's own contribution must be removed: it is \(\approx 1\) at zero distance but the deposit spreads it over four cells, so the correct subtraction is the analytically computed bilinear interpolation of \(k\) at the point's own sub-cell offsets, not a flat \(-1\). The flat version leaves a bias of order \(1/(1+r^2)\) that does not shrink as the grid is refined.

    The accuracy is governed by how many cells fit inside one kernel width, and the kernel's unit length is the layout's own scale. t-SNE's layout grows with \(n\) (the early phase spreads the points to make room), so a fixed grid becomes relatively coarser on larger data. The cost is bounded by the grid; the accuracy is not.

    11. Multiscale #

    Average the conditional distributions before symmetrising:

    \[p_{j|i} \;=\; \frac{1}{|\mathcal{K}|} \sum_{\kappa \in \mathcal{K}} p^{(\kappa)}_{j|i}. \qquad (13)\]

    The mixture is itself a valid conditional distribution — an average of distributions is one — so the rest of the algorithm is unchanged. Averaging in probability space rather than concatenating neighbourhoods is what keeps the perplexity meaningful; averaging the distances instead would not correspond to any kernel.

    12. Parametric #

    Replace \(y_i\) by \(f_\theta(x_i)\) with

    \[f_\theta(x) \;=\; W_2 \tanh(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2, \qquad (14)\]

    fit \(f_\theta(x_i) \approx y_i^{\text{teacher}}\) by least squares and then Adam. The teacher is the exact layout; the network's job is to be a smooth function that passes near it. This is distillation, not dimensionality reduction, and the KL the class reports afterwards is the teacher's layout measured against \(P\) — which is the honest description. Fine-tuning the network directly on the KL, which is the obvious alternative, collapses it: Adam's step is scale-blind, and a KL gradient through a net is large in the wrong direction long before it is useful.

    13. What is identified #

    \(Y\) is identified up to rotation, reflection and translation — the objective depends only on pairwise distances in the layout. Scale is fixed by the kernel's unit length, weakly: the KL does determine a preferred scale, since shrinking raises all \(q\) and growing lowers them, but the optimum is flat and the exaggeration phase picks the starting scale for the search. Nothing else is identified. Cluster shapes, relative cluster areas, and the distances between clusters are artefacts of the optimisation, not properties of the data.

    Code walkthrough #

    TSNE(n_components=2, *, variant="exact", perplexity=30.0, n_iter=1000, learning_rate=None, exaggeration=12.0, theta=0.5, grid_size=128, perplexities=(10.0, 30.0), hidden=64, seed=0) with fit(X), fit_transform(X), and transform(X_new) for parametric only. Attributes: embedding_, kl_initial_, kl_divergence_, history_, interactions_ (repulsion terms in the last iteration), and teacher_ for parametric.

    X is a (samples, features) point cloud; unlike MDS the method needs the points, because the affinities are built from them.

    1. Bisection for the bandwidth #

        target = np.log(perplexity)
    beta = 1.0 / np.median(off, axis=1)
    lo = np.zeros(n)
    hi = np.full(n, np.inf)
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        P = np.exp(-beta[:, None] * d2)
        P[eye] = 0.0
        s = P.sum(axis=1)
        entropy = np.log(s) + beta * (d2 * P).sum(axis=1) / s
        too_flat = entropy > target                  # needs a larger beta
        lo = np.where(too_flat, beta, lo)
        hi = np.where(too_flat, hi, beta)
        beta = np.where(np.isfinite(hi), 0.5 * (lo + hi),
                        np.minimum(2.0 * beta, 1e6))

    (4), for all points at once. The bracket is stored as two vectors, lo and hi, and each point's update reads only its own entries — so the search is 64 iterations of matrix work rather than \(n\) independent scalar loops. Points whose upper bracket is still infinite are still doubling; the rest have switched to bisection. beta = 2 * beta is the exponential search that gets a finite upper bracket in the first place.

    2. Symmetrisation and truncation #

        idx = np.argpartition(-P, k, axis=1)[:, :k]
    keep = np.zeros_like(P)
    np.put_along_axis(keep, idx, 1.0, axis=1)
    P = P * keep
    P = P / P.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    J = (P + P.T) / (2.0 * n)
    np.fill_diagonal(J, 0.0)
    support = J > 0.0
    J[support] = np.maximum(J[support], 1e-12)    # floor the KL's log q
    return J / J.sum()

    (5), preceded by the truncation to \(3\kappa\) terms per row. argpartition finds the threshold in \(O(n)\) per row; the mask and the renormalisation keep each conditional row a distribution, so the symmetrisation still produces a joint one. The floor is applied only on the support — J > 0 — and the final division undoes the mass the floor added.

    3. The exact repulsion, in three matrix products #

        d2 = np.maximum(sq[:, None] + sq[None, :] - 2.0 * Y @ Y.T, 0.0)
    np.fill_diagonal(d2, np.inf)                     # (1 + inf)^-1 = 0, exactly
    w = 1.0 / (1.0 + d2)
    w2 = w * w
    phi = w2.sum(axis=1)
    f = np.stack([Y[:, 0] * phi - w2 @ Y[:, 0],
                  Y[:, 1] * phi - w2 @ Y[:, 1]], axis=1)
    return f, float(w.sum()), w, n * (n - 1)

    \(F_i = \sum_j w_{ij}^2 (y_i - y_j)\) is \(y_i \sum_j w_{ij}^2\) minus \(\sum_j w_{ij}^2 y_j\): a row sum and a matrix product, no broadcast of the \((n,n,2)\) difference. The diagonal is set to infinite distance rather than zero so that \((1 + d^2)^{-1}\) is exactly zero there — the self-term drops out without a mask. The returned w is handed back to the gradient rather than recomputed, which is why the exact path costs one kernel and not two.

    4. The gradient #

        if w is not None:
        pw = P * w * exaggeration
        attraction = np.stack([Y[:, 0] * pw.sum(axis=1) - pw @ Y[:, 0],
                               Y[:, 1] * pw.sum(axis=1) - pw @ Y[:, 1]], axis=1)
    else:
        diff = Y[rows] - Y[cols]
        pair_w = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.einsum("ij,ij->i", diff, diff))
        pw = vals * pair_w * exaggeration
        total = np.bincount(rows, weights=pw, minlength=n)
        ax = np.bincount(rows, weights=pw * Y[cols, 0], minlength=n)
        ay = np.bincount(rows, weights=pw * Y[cols, 1], minlength=n)
        attraction = np.stack([Y[:, 0] * total - ax,
                               Y[:, 1] * total - ay], axis=1)
    return -4.0 * (attraction - f / max(z, EPS))

    Two paths, same formula. When the repulsion already built the dense kernel, the attraction is one product against P * w; when it did not (Barnes–Hut, FFT), the attraction is gathered over the \(nk\) nonzeros with a bincount, which is \(O(n\kappa)\) and does not materialise the dense matrix. exaggeration multiplies the attraction only — applying it to both terms would rescale the gradient rather than reweight the objective. The final -4(A - F/Z) is the descent direction.

    5. The quadtree, one level at a time #

                idx = order[start:stop]
            cx, cy = com[node]
            h = half[node] * 0.5
            quad = (Y[idx, 0] > cx).astype(np.int64) * 2 + (Y[idx, 1] > cy)
            perm = np.argsort(quad, kind="stable")
            order[start:stop] = idx[perm]
            cuts = np.searchsorted(quad[perm], np.arange(5))

    Each node's points are a contiguous slice of one order array, so the level is handled by sorting the slice by quadrant number and splitting it at four searchsorted positions. The bits are assigned \(2 \cdot [x > c_x] + [y > c_y]\), consistent with the child offsets in the next lines. The cost of one level is one argsort over the points in that level; the obvious implementation, np.flatnonzero(assign == node) per node, scans all \(n\) points once per node and turns the "sub-quadratic" method into an \(O(n^2)\) one.

    The first version of this function also had the two quadrant bits swapped between the comparison and the child's centre, which builds a tree whose cells do not contain their points. It is invisible at \(\theta = 0\) — leaves are still leaves — and only shows up as a force error that does not shrink with \(\theta\).

    6. The acceptance test #

        while pi.size:
        dx = Y[pi, 0] - com[pc, 0]
        dy = Y[pi, 1] - com[pc, 1]
        r2 = dx * dx + dy * dy
        single = leaf[pc] >= 0
        accept = single | (2.0 * half[pc] / np.sqrt(np.maximum(r2, EPS)) < theta)
        inside = ~single & (np.abs(dx) <= half[pc]) & (np.abs(dy) <= half[pc])
        accept = accept & ~(single & (leaf[pc] == pi)) & ~inside

    (10), evaluated for every live (query, node) pair at once. pi and pc are the parallel arrays of query indices and current nodes; accepted pairs are folded into f and Z with bincount, rejected pairs are expanded to their four children and pushed into the next round. The traversal is a level-order walk of the tree for all \(n\) queries simultaneously, which is why there is no Python loop over points.

    inside is the guard that a cell containing the query point is never accepted; the leaf[pc] == pi term is the self-interaction. Both are needed: the first catches interior nodes, the second catches the leaf that holds the query itself.

    7. The FFT kernel and padding #

        size = 2 * grid_size - 1
    lag = np.arange(-(grid_size - 1), grid_size)
    dx = lag[:, None] * h
    dy = lag[None, :] * h
    k0 = 1.0 / (1.0 + dx * dx + dy * dy)
    kernel = np.fft.ifftshift(
        np.stack([k0, k0 * k0 * dx, k0 * k0 * dy]), axes=(1, 2))
    spectrum = np.fft.rfft2(charge, s=(size, size))
    field = np.fft.irfft2(
        spectrum[None, :, :] * np.fft.rfft2(kernel, s=(size, size)),
        s=(size, size))
    field = field[:, :grid_size, :grid_size]

    (11)–(12) for three kernels in one transform. ifftshift moves the zero lag from the centre of the lag grid to index zero, which is what rfft2 assumes. size = 2G - 1 is the exact linear convolution length: anything smaller wraps the far field around into the near field. The three kernels travel as a stacked axis so one rfft2 call handles them. The crop back to grid_size drops the half of the padded domain that cannot contain a source.

    8. Removing the self-term #

        for di, dj, weight in taps:
        ox = (di - fu) * h
        oy = (dj - fv) * h
        k = 1.0 / (1.0 + ox * ox + oy * oy)
        own_phi += weight * k
        own_x += weight * k * k * ox
        own_y += weight * k * k * oy
    phi = phi - own_phi
    return (np.stack([fx - own_x, fy - own_y], axis=1), float(phi.sum()),
            None, grid_size * grid_size)

    The point contributed to four cells with weights taps, and the field it reads back is the interpolated sum of those four cells' convolutions. Its own contribution is therefore the same four cells convolved with the kernel and interpolated back — evaluated here in closed form, with ox, oy the offset from the point to each tap in world units. Subtracting a flat \(1\) instead leaves a bias of order the deposit's spread, which does not vanish as the grid is refined; this is the difference between the \(0.005\) error at \(256^2\) and no convergence at all.

    9. The parametric readout #

            A = np.tanh(Xs @ self.W1.T + self.b1)
        self.W2 = np.linalg.lstsq(A, Y, rcond=None)[0].T
        self.b2 = Y.mean(axis=0)
        params = [self.W1, self.b1, self.W2, self.b2]
        m = [np.zeros_like(p) for p in params]
        v = [np.zeros_like(p) for p in params]
        for step in range(steps):
            A = np.tanh(Xs @ self.W1.T + self.b1)
            residual = 2.0 * (A @ self.W2.T + self.b2 - Y) / n
            dA = (residual @ self.W2) * (1.0 - A * A)
            grads = [dA.T @ Xs, dA.sum(axis=0), residual.T @ A,
                     residual.sum(axis=0)]

    The readout is solved, not trained: with W1 random, tanh(W1 x) is already a rich feature map, and the best linear function of it is one lstsq. Adam then moves the features themselves, with the gradient of the last layer obtained by backpropagating the residual. 1 - A * A is the tanh derivative; the 2/n is the derivative of the mean squared error. The whole fit is deterministic for a given seed, since the only random draw is the initial W1.

    10. The loop #

            lr = (self.learning_rate if self.learning_rate is not None
              else max(n / self.exaggeration / 4.0, 50.0))
        release = max(1, self.n_iter // 4)
        update = np.zeros_like(Y)
        sample = max(1, self.n_iter // 10)
        self.history_ = [_kl_divergence(P, Y)]
        for it in range(self.n_iter):
            f, z, w, self.interactions_ = self._repulsion(Y)
            g = _gradient(P, rows, cols, vals, Y, f, z, w,
                          self.exaggeration if it < release else 1.0)
            update = (0.5 if it < release else 0.8) * update + lr * g
            Y = Y + update
            if (it + 1) % sample == 0:
                self.history_.append(_kl_divergence(P, Y))

    The whole optimiser. release is the single moment where exaggeration drops and momentum rises — they are coupled in the classic schedule and separating them makes the layout worse. The KL is sampled ten times through the run for history_, and computed once more at the end for kl_divergence_, which is the value measured against the final embedding rather than the last sample.

    Complexity #

    \(n\) samples, \(d\) input dimensions, \(\kappa\) perplexity, \(T\) iterations, \(G\) grid size, \(h\) hidden width.

    Operation Time Memory Note
    pairwise distances \(O(n^2d)\) \(O(n^2)\) Gram identity, no \((n,n,d)\) array
    bandwidth search \(O(n^2 \cdot 64)\) \(O(n^2)\) vectorised bisection, fixed 64 steps
    symmetrise + truncate \(O(n^2 + n\kappa)\) \(O(n^2)\) argpartition per row
    exact attraction \(O(n\kappa + n^2)\) \(O(n^2)\) product against the dense kernel
    exact repulsion \(O(n^2)\) \(O(n^2)\) three matrix products
    Barnes–Hut repulsion \(O(n\log n)\) terms \(O(n)\) \(\theta\)-dependent constant
    quadtree build \(O(n\log n)\) \(O(n)\) one argsort per level
    FFT repulsion \(O(G^2\log G)\) \(O(G^2)\) independent of \(n\)
    multiscale affinity $O( \mathcal{K} n^2)$
    parametric readout \(O(nh^2 + h^3)\) \(O(nh)\) one lstsq plus Adam
    full demo — — 12 fits, 5.5 s

    The first three rows are the fixed cost, quadratic in \(n\), and they are why t-SNE is a visualisation method and not a preprocessing step: a million points needs \(10^{12}\) distance entries before the optimiser runs. The usual answer is not a faster kernel but a sample — fit on a subset and place the rest with transform, or with an out-of-sample extension.

    The repulsion rows are the ones the variants change. exact is \(O(n^2)\) per iteration; barnes_hut has \(O(n\log n)\) expected terms but a Python-level constant that makes it slower than one BLAS product below a few thousand points — measured term counts are \(0.341\), \(0.140\) and \(0.049\) of all pairs at \(n = 200, 800, 3200\), so the crossover is where \(n\log n \cdot c_{\text{tree}}\) falls below \(n^2 \cdot c_{\text{BLAS}}\). fft is the one that is genuinely independent of the data size, at the price of a fixed resolution.

    Memory is \(O(n^2)\) for the exact variants and for the affinities, and that — not time — is the binding constraint. Barnes–Hut still needs the full \(P\) matrix for the attraction in this implementation (the truncation keeps it sparse in principle but not in storage), which is the paper's own compromise.

    Failure modes #

    1. Double-counting the repulsion. The normaliser in (9) is \(\sum_{i \neq j} w_{ij}\) over ordered pairs. Using the symmetric-half \(\tfrac{1}{2}\sum w\) halves \(Z\), doubles the effective repulsion, and changes the answer qualitatively: the layout expands to a scale where the kernel is flat, the gradient vanishes, and the eight-neighbour overlap on the demo's roll reads \(0.43\) instead of \(0.86\). Diagnosis is by finite differences against (7) — a normalisation error is a constant factor and is invisible in a plot of the KL. This is the most common way to write t-SNE from the paper and get a wrong picture.

    2. A rescaled gradient. Putting \(1/Z\) on both terms of (9), or folding a factor into the learning rate, is mathematically harmless and practically not, because the step size, the momentum and the exaggeration factor are all tuned numbers. A gradient that is off by a constant drifts: the layout finds a different equilibrium scale, and the exaggeration phase spends its iterations travelling instead of arranging. The symptom is a layout that looks like a correct t-SNE plot at the wrong zoom, with every cluster the same shape.

    3. A quadtree that is not a partition. Swapped quadrant bits, a leaf centred on its cell rather than on its point, or a cell accepted when it contains the query — each breaks the \(\theta \to 0\) limit, where the approximation must be exact. The demo's first assertion is that limit: force error \(1.0\times10^{-14}\) and normaliser error \(5.8\times10^{-16}\) at \(\theta = 0\). Any bookkeeping error shows up there, and only there — at \(\theta = 0.5\) it is buried under the \(5\%\) approximation.

    4. An inefficient tree build. The obvious build scans all \(n\) points for every node. That is \(O(n^2)\) bookkeeping inside a method advertised as \(O(n\log n)\), and it made this implementation's Barnes–Hut slower than its exact solver at every size tested. The fix is structural: keep the points in one contiguous order array with per-node slices, so a level costs one sort of the level.

    5. A dense "sparse" matrix. Flooring every entry of \(P\) at \(10^{-12}\) keeps \(\log q\) finite and makes the attraction \(O(n^2)\). Measured: a 400-point fit takes \(6.0\) s instead of \(0.6\) s. The floor belongs on the support only.

    6. Gains that deadlock. The original paper's per-parameter gains need a correct sign comparison. Comparing the update's sign against the displacement rather than the gradient collapses every gain to its floor: the update becomes tiny, the displacement has the same sign as the update by construction, the gain never recovers, and the layout freezes. No error, no warning, a picture of the initialisation.

    7. Perplexity larger than the sample. The entropy target \(\log \kappa\) is unreachable when \(\kappa \geq n - 1\), because the maximum entropy of \(n-1\) nonzero entries is \(\log(n-1)\). The bisection then runs its upper bracket to infinity and the exponential underflows to a uniform row. The class raises instead, because a uniform row silently turns t-SNE into a different algorithm.

    8. Reading distances off the plot. The demo's layout of a swiss roll has distance correlation \(0.335\) with the rectangle the roll is isometric to, where Isomap has \(0.9996\). Cluster gaps, cluster areas and the global arrangement are artefacts of the optimisation. What survives is neighbourhood membership — \(0.857\) of the eight nearest neighbours at perplexity 30 — and the order of cluster separations (correlation \(0.947\) on six Gaussians), which is why the method is a good exploratory tool and a poor feature extractor.

    9. Perplexity read as a smoothing parameter. It is not: \(5\) gives \(0.772\) eight-neighbour overlap and \(80\) gives \(0.751\), while \(30\) gives \(0.857\). Both ends lose, for different reasons — too small and the affinities are dominated by noise in the nearest few points, so the layout fragments; too large and every point's neighbourhood overlaps every other's, so the local structure is averaged away. The demo's \(k=40\) column shows the same shape (\(0.430\), \(0.769\), \(0.254\)). Values between 5 and 50 cover almost every published use.

    10. Trusting transform as if it were the layout. The parametric variant is a regression onto the teacher's coordinates, so it inherits the teacher's artefacts and adds a smoothness assumption. On the demo's clusters the network scores \(0.900\) against a 10-neighbour average's \(1.000\); on a curved sheet both fall to \(0.428\) and \(0.477\). A parametric map is worth having when new points must be placed cheaply, and it is always worse than the fit it distils.

    11. A fixed grid on growing data. The FFT variant's error is set by how many cells fit in one kernel width, and the layout's scale grows with \(n\). A grid that gives \(0.005\) error at 250 points is coarse at 25,000 — the same \(G\) resolves a smaller fraction of the picture. Scale the grid with the data, or measure the error against the exact force on a subsample.

    Applications #

    • Exploratory single-cell genomics. The canonical use: tens of thousands of cells, each a vector of thousands of gene counts, embedded to find cell types and trajectories. The parametric and FFT variants exist largely because of this data — it is where \(n\) is large enough that the exact kernel is too slow and where new cells arrive continuously.
    • Embedding spaces and model diagnostics. Comparing representations from two models by embedding a shared sample in both and looking at which structures survive. The neighbourhood-overlap measure in the demo is the quantitative version: it says what fraction of a point's true neighbours the map keeps.
    • Text and document maps. Perplexity 30–50 on TF-IDF or sentence embeddings is the standard "map of the corpus" picture. The colour-by-metadata view is the point; the distances are not.
    • Image collections. Nearest-neighbour galleries from a t-SNE layout are a standard dataset-audit tool: duplicated images, label errors and near-duplicate classes all show up as tight clumps.
    • Feature-space sanity checks before clustering. If k-means on the embedding finds the same clusters as k-means on the original features, the structure is real; if not, the embedding has invented it.
    • Teaching nonlinearity. The method is the standard demonstration that "close" is a choice: the demo's swiss roll keeps its neighbourhoods and loses its rectangle, and both facts are visible in one picture.

    Not for: distance-based downstream tasks (the distances are not preserved), metric learning (the layout is not a metric space embedding), or anything where cluster sizes matter. UMAP, in this chapter, has the same failure and a better global-arrangement story.
