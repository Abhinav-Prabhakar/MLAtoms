    08 · Manifolds & embeddings/tier 2/5 variants/source

    Classical MDS

    Intuition #

    You are given numbers that say how far apart things are, and nothing else. Not coordinates, not directions, not a metric space — a table of dissimilarities, one per pair. Market research asks respondents how similar two brands feel; ecology counts species and computes Bray–Curtis between sites; genetics has distances between sequences; a graph has shortest-path lengths. In every case the question is the same: is there a configuration of points whose distances look like this table?

         δ (a table of numbers)            X (a plot)
      ┌───────────────┐              y│      ·   ·
   b  │ 0   .   .   . │               │   ·    ·
   r  │ .   0   .   . │     ──▶       │      ·    ·
   a  │ .   .   0   . │               │  ·      ·
   n  │ .   .   .   0 │               └────────────── x
      └───────────────┘

    MDS is the answer, and the answer is not unique: any rotation, reflection or translation of the configuration has the same distances. That is a feature, not a defect — it means distances are the data and coordinates are a choice of picture.

    What the picture should preserve is where the design space opens. The standard objective is stress,

    \[\sigma(X) \;=\; \sum_{i

    the summed squared disagreement between the embedding's distances and the targets. It is not convex, it has many local minima, and every variant in this file is a different answer to which \(\delta\) should be matched and how.

    There are five, and they differ more than the name suggests. Classical MDS ignores (1) entirely: it minimises a linear surrogate with a closed form, no seed and no local optima, and the demo recovers a 40-point planar configuration from its distances to a Gram error of \(1.1\times10^{-15}\). SMACOF minimises (1) by majorisation and cannot go uphill. Metric minimises (1) by gradient descent with a line search and cannot promise that. Nonmetric matches only the order of the \(\delta\), which on the demo's cubed distances recovers the geometry at correlation \(1.0000\) where metric MDS gets \(0.9022\). Weighted matches a subset of the pairs, and recovers a 40-point configuration from 230 of its 780 distances — where filling the missing entries with the mean gives a Gram error of \(0.8670\).

    Variants #

    Variant Objective Algorithm Cost
    classical strain, \(\lVert B - XX^{\mathsf T}\rVert^2\) double centre, eigendecompose \(O(n^3)\) once
    smacof (1) majorisation, monotone \(O(n^3)\) per sweep
    metric (1) gradient descent + line search \(O(n^2 k)\) per step
    nonmetric Kruskal's stress-1 isotonic regression + SMACOF \(O(n^2\log n)\) per sweep
    weighted (1) with \(w_{ij}=0\) on missing SMACOF with a weight matrix \(O(n^3)\) per sweep

    classical is Torgerson scaling. Square the distances, double-centre them, take the top eigenvectors, and the coordinates fall out (§2). It minimises strain, a different objective from stress, and the two coincide exactly when the dissimilarities are Euclidean distances of points in the requested dimension. That is a strong assumption and it is why Isomap computes geodesic distances and then calls this: on a manifold, the ambient Euclidean distances are not the right \(\delta\), the graph distances are.

    smacof is the workhorse. Each sweep replaces the non-convex problem with a quadratic that touches it at the current point and lies above it everywhere, then minimises that exactly. The result is the Guttman transform, and the stress cannot increase. On the demo's 50 noisy distances it takes 131 sweeps with zero uphill steps to reach \(0.004864\), which is \(27.5\%\) below the closed form's \(0.006707\) — the closed form is not minimising the thing the iteration does.

    metric is the same objective by a different route: the analytic gradient of (1), a step, and a backtracking line search that halves until the stress falls. It is here because SMACOF's monotonicity is bought with a specific update that ignores the geometry of the problem, and descent is the alternative that uses it. The demo's honest result is that descent reaches the same stress to \(2\times 10^{-10}\) in fewer steps — 33 against 131 — because a line search is aggressive when it works. The difference is the guarantee, not the speed: majorisation cannot go uphill for any weights and any dissimilarity, while descent is monotone only while its search succeeds.

    nonmetric is Kruskal's ordinal MDS. It assumes only that the numbers are ordered, not that they are on a ratio scale — which is what a Likert score or a similarity judgement actually gives you. Each sweep regresses the current distances onto the order of the \(\delta\) by isotonic regression (pool adjacent violators, §5), then does a SMACOF step towards those disparities. On the demo's distances cubed — strictly increasing, nowhere near linear — metric MDS faithfully reproduces the cube (correlation \(0.9022\) with the truth, \(0.8354\) of the local neighbourhoods kept) while nonmetric recovers the geometry (\(1.0000\) and \(1.0000\)).

    weighted is (1) with a weight matrix, where \(w_{ij} = 0\) means "this pair was never observed". It is the same solver as smacof — the weights are a first-class part of the formulation, not a separate algorithm — and the code refuses to run it without at least one zero so that it cannot silently duplicate smacof. With \(230\) of \(780\) pairs observed the demo recovers the configuration at Gram error \(0.0005\) and a mean relative residual of \(3.3\times10^{-12}\) on the pairs it saw; imputing the mean and running the closed form gives \(0.8670\).

    Derivation #

    1. Stress is a graph problem, not a matrix one #

    Write (1) out as three terms:

    \[\sigma(X) \;=\; \sum_{i

    The first term is a quadratic form in \(X\) — it is \(\mathrm{tr}(X^{\mathsf T} V X)\) with \(V\) the weighted Laplacian of the graph whose edges are the observed pairs. The second is the only hard part: \(-\lVert x_i - x_j\rVert\) is concave, and maximising a concave function is the non-convexity in the problem. The third term does not involve \(X\) at all.

    That decomposition is the whole derivation. SMACOF linearises the concave term at the current point, which turns (2) into a quadratic in \(X\) with a closed-form minimiser; gradient descent differentiates it; classical MDS throws it away.

    2. Classical MDS: strain, and why it has a closed form #

    Suppose the \(\delta\) are Euclidean distances, \(\delta_{ij} = \lVert z_i - z_j\rVert\) for some unknown \(z\). Then

    \[\delta_{ij}^2 \;=\; \lVert z_i\rVert^2 - 2\,z_i^{\mathsf T}z_j + \lVert z_j\rVert^2, \qquad (3)\]

    which says the matrix \(D^{(2)}\) of squared distances is a rank-\(\le k\) Gram matrix plus two rank-one terms from the norms. Centring removes those terms. With \(J = I - \tfrac1n \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}\),

    \[B \;=\; -\tfrac12\, J D^{(2)} J, \qquad B_{ij} = (z_i - \bar z)^{\mathsf T}(z_j - \bar z), \qquad (4)\]

    so \(B\) is the Gram matrix of the centred coordinates, and any factorisation \(B = XX^{\mathsf T}\) is an embedding with the right distances. Since \(B\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite, the factorisation is an eigendecomposition,

    \[B = V\Lambda V^{\mathsf T} \;\Longrightarrow\; X = V_k \Lambda_k^{1/2}, \qquad (5)\]

    keeping the \(k\) largest eigenvalues. No iteration, no seed, and — when the data is exactly Euclidean in \(k\) dimensions — no error: the demo's Gram error is \(1.1\times10^{-15}\) and its stress \(1.3\times10^{-31}\), floating-point zero.

    When \(B\) has negative eigenvalues, (5) truncates them to zero, which is a projection onto the nearest positive semidefinite matrix. That is the classical answer to non-Euclidean dissimilarities: not the minimiser of stress, the minimiser of strain,

    \[\lVert B - XX^{\mathsf T}\rVert_F^2, \qquad (6)\]

    a least-squares problem in the squared distances. Strain and stress disagree whenever the \(\delta\) are not Euclidean, and the demo's 1-D fit shows the cost of asking for too few dimensions: stress \(0.0860\) where two dimensions give zero.

    3. The majorisation, and the Guttman transform #

    The concave term in (2) is \(-\lVert x_i-x_j\rVert\). The function \(u \mapsto -\sqrt{u}\) is convex, so its tangent at \(u_0\) lies below it, which gives the inequality

    \[-\lVert x_i - x_j\rVert \;\le\; -\frac{(x_i-x_j)^{\mathsf T}(x_i^{t}-x_j^{t})}{\lVert x_i^{t}-x_j^{t}\rVert} \;+\;\text{const}, \qquad (7)\]

    and substituting into (2) gives a quadratic upper bound on the stress that touches it at \(X^t\). Minimising that bound is a linear system:

    \[\frac{\partial}{\partial X}\Big[\mathrm{tr}(X^{\mathsf T}VX) - 2\,\mathrm{tr}\big(X^{\mathsf T}B(X^t)X^t\big)\Big] = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; VX = B(X^t)X^t, \qquad (8)\]

    with \(V\) the weighted Laplacian and

    \[B_{ij}(X) \;=\; \frac{w_{ij}\,\hat\delta_{ij}}{\lVert x_i - x_j\rVert}\;(i\ne j), \qquad B_{ii} = -\sum_{j\ne i}B_{ij}. \qquad (9)\]

    For unit weights \(V = nI - \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}\), whose pseudo-inverse is \(I/n\) on the subspace orthogonal to \(\mathbf{1}\), so (8) becomes the textbook update \(X \leftarrow \tfrac1n B(X)X\). The code solves the system instead, because a general weight matrix is not the complete graph and \(\tfrac1n\) is then wrong. Because the bound touches the stress and lies above it, the updated point can only lower the stress — the monotonicity is a theorem, not an observation, and the demo counts zero uphill steps as a check on the implementation rather than on the theory.

    4. Descent #

    Differentiating (1) directly, with \(d_{ij} = \lVert x_i-x_j\rVert\),

    \[\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial x_i} \;=\; 2\sum_{j\ne i} w_{ij}\,\frac{d_{ij}-\delta_{ij}}{d_{ij}}\,(x_i - x_j), \qquad (10)\]

    which is a Laplacian-times-\(X\) product in disguise: build the matrix of coefficients \(c_{ij} = w_{ij}(d_{ij}-\delta_{ij})/d_{ij}\), and the gradient is \((\mathrm{diag}(c\mathbf{1}) - c)X\). A step along \(-\nabla\sigma\) lowers the stress for a small enough step, and the line search finds "small enough" by halving until it does. Backtracking guarantees monotonicity per step, but only because it checks; a fixed step can and does diverge, which is the sense in which descent is the less safe of the two methods.

    5. Ordinal constraints and isotonic regression #

    Nonmetric MDS replaces each \(\delta_{ij}\) with a free value \(\hat\delta_{ij}\) subject to preserving the order,

    \[\hat\delta_{ij} \le \hat\delta_{hk} \quad\text{whenever}\quad \delta_{ij} \le \delta_{hk}, \qquad (11)\]

    and chooses those values to sit as close as possible to the current distances:

    \[\hat\delta \;=\; \arg\min_{z\ \text{monotone}}\ \sum_{i

    which is isotonic regression and is solved exactly by pool-adjacent-violators: scan in the order of the \(\delta\), and whenever the fitted value would decrease, merge the offending block into its mean. The result is the closest monotone sequence in squared error — no step size, no tolerance.

    Then one SMACOF step towards \(\hat\delta\) and the loop repeats. This is why any strictly increasing transformation of the \(\delta\) leaves the answer unchanged: the algorithm only ever reads their order.

    The objective is Kruskal's stress-1,

    \[\mathrm{stress}_1 \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i

    and the choice of that denominator matters. Raw stress against re-fitted disparities can be driven to zero by shrinking the configuration towards a point, because a collapsing configuration is perfectly monotone in almost anything. Stress-1 divides by the scale of the current distances, so it measures only the departure from monotonicity — which is the quantity the model claims to preserve. The code records stress-1 for nonmetric and normalised raw stress for the other four.

    6. Missing pairs #

    A zero weight removes the pair from both sums in (1) and from the Laplacian in (8). The objective is then defined on the observed graph only, and the embedding is determined by the connectivity of that graph: a point with no observed pair contributes nothing and is placed arbitrarily. The demo asserts every point keeps at least one pair, and on a sparse graph the degrees of freedom are visibly smaller — \(230\) distances constrain \(2n-3 = 77\) coordinates, which is why the fit to those distances is essentially exact (\(3.3\times10^{-12}\)) and the recovery of the unseen pairs is the interesting number.

    This is a genuinely different operation from imputing the missing entries. An imputed value enters the objective at full weight and pulls the configuration towards a number nobody measured; a zero weight lets the geometry be determined by its neighbours. The demo's \(0.0005\) against \(0.8670\) Gram error is that difference at \(70\%\) missingness.

    7. What is identified #

    Distances determine coordinates only up to an isometry:

    \[X \mapsto XQ + \mathbf{1}t^{\mathsf T}, \qquad Q^{\mathsf T}Q = I, \qquad (14)\]

    so comparing embeddings coordinate by coordinate is meaningless, and comparing them by Procrustes rotation only slightly less so. The honest comparison is the Gram matrix \(XX^{\mathsf T}\), which is invariant to (14), or the distances themselves. Every claim in the demo is a Gram error, a distance correlation or a neighbourhood overlap, for this reason.

    Code walkthrough #

    MDS(n_components=2, *, variant="classical", n_iter=300, tol=1e-9, seed=0) with fit(D, weights=None) and fit_transform(D, weights=None). D is (n, n), symmetric, non-negative, with a zero diagonal; weights is the same shape, and a zero weight means a missing pair. Attributes: embedding_ (n, n_components), stress_, stresses_ (one value per sweep) and disparities_ (the monotone targets, nonmetric only).

    1. Distances without a Python loop #

        diff = X[:, None, :] - X[None, :, :]
    return np.sqrt(np.einsum("ijk,ijk->ij", diff, diff))

    The broadcast builds the \((n,n,k)\) array of differences, and the einsum contracts the last axis. Writing \(\sqrt{\sum_k (x_{ik}-x_{jk})^2}\) this way costs \(n^2k\) memory but removes the loop that every naive implementation has and every profile then shows. np.linalg.norm(diff, axis=2) is the same thing and allocates the same array; the einsum skips the intermediate square root of a sum along a strided axis.

    2. The closed form, and the truncation that hides in it #

            n = D.shape[0]
        J = np.eye(n) - np.full((n, n), 1.0 / n)
        B = -0.5 * J @ (D ** 2) @ J
        evals, evecs = np.linalg.eigh(B)
        order = np.argsort(evals)[::-1][: self.n_components]
        keep = np.maximum(evals[order], 0.0)
        return evecs[:, order] * np.sqrt(keep)

    (4)–(5). Two details are the whole function. eigh returns ascending eigenvalues, so the [::-1] is not decoration — remove it and the embedding is the worst dimensions, which is a plausible-looking plot of noise. And np.maximum(..., 0.0) is the projection of (6): a negative eigenvalue has no real square root, and clipping it is the least-squares answer rather than an error. Both are silent when wrong, which is why the demo's first assertion is a Gram error against a known configuration and not a fit statistic.

    3. Stress counts each pair once #

        d = _distances(X)
    iu = np.triu_indices_from(D, k=1)
    return float(np.sum(W[iu] * (d[iu] - D[iu]) ** 2))

    (1). The upper triangle, because the full matrix counts every pair twice and doubles the diagonal, which is zero anyway. Normalising by \(\sum_{i — done by the caller — makes the number comparable across datasets and puts it in a range where \(0.004864\) and \(0.006707\) are visibly different rather than both "small".

    4. The Guttman transform with weights #

        d = _distances(X)
    ratio = W * target / np.maximum(d, EPS)
    np.fill_diagonal(ratio, 0.0)
    B = ratio.copy()
    np.fill_diagonal(B, -B.sum(axis=1))

    V = -W.copy()
    np.fill_diagonal(V, 0.0)
    np.fill_diagonal(V, -V.sum(axis=1))
    return np.linalg.lstsq(V + 1e-12 * np.eye(n), B @ X, rcond=None)[0]

    (8)–(9). B's diagonal is the negative row sum, which makes it the matrix whose quadratic form is the majoriser; V is the Laplacian, built the same way from the weights. V is singular — constants are in its null space, because shifting every point changes nothing — so a plain solve fails and lstsq returns the minimum-norm solution, which is the one with the points centred at the origin. The 1e-12 ridge is there for the degenerate case of a point with no observed pair: without it the system is inconsistent rather than merely singular, and lstsq returns a least-squares compromise that moves every other point to accommodate the unconstrained one.

    5. Pool adjacent violators #

        means: list[float] = []
    sizes: list[int] = []
    for v in y:
        means.append(float(v))
        sizes.append(1)
        while len(means) > 1 and means[-2] > means[-1]:
            v2, s2 = means.pop(), sizes.pop()
            v1, s1 = means.pop(), sizes.pop()
            means.append((v1 * s1 + v2 * s2) / (s1 + s2))
            sizes.append(s1 + s2)
    return np.repeat(np.asarray(means), np.asarray(sizes))

    (12). The blocks are kept as (mean, size) pairs, and a violation merges the last two blocks into their weighted mean — one pass, \(O(n)\) amortised, exact. Two things are easy to get wrong: the merge has to be weighted by block size (an unweighted mean of means is not the least-squares fit), and the while has to keep checking after a merge, because merging can create a new violation with the block before it. A single if passes the small examples and fails on real data.

    6. Ordinal targets, and the reconstruction that must be symmetric #

            iu = np.triu_indices_from(D, k=1)
        order = np.argsort(D[iu], kind="stable")
        d = _distances(X)[iu]
        fitted = np.empty_like(d)
        fitted[order] = _isotonic(d[order])

    The sort is on the targets and the regression is on the distances: the isotonic fit is monotone in \(\delta\), applied to \(d\). fitted[order] = ... is the inverse permutation that puts the fitted values back in pair order, so the disparity matrix can be rebuilt and symmetrised. kind="stable" matters: ties in the \(\delta\) are common on ordinal data (a 1–7 scale has six possible ties per pair), and an unstable sort makes the fitted values depend on memory layout. This is Kruskal's primary approach — ties are allowed to be broken by the embedding — as opposed to the secondary approach, which forces tied dissimilarities to tied distances.

    7. Descent with a line search #

                ratio[iu] = W[iu] * (d[iu] - D[iu]) / np.maximum(d[iu], EPS)
            ratio = ratio + ratio.T
            grad = 2.0 * (np.diag(ratio.sum(axis=1)) - ratio) @ X
            slope = float(np.sum(grad * grad))
            if slope < EPS:
                break
            trial, scale = X, step
            while scale > 1e-16:
                trial = X - scale * grad
                candidate = _stress(trial, D, W)
                if candidate <= sigma - 1e-4 * scale * slope:
                    break
                scale *= 0.5

    (10). ratio holds \(c_{ij}\) on the upper triangle and is symmetrised, which is what makes the Laplacian product correct; forgetting the + ratio.T halves the gradient and doubles the iteration count without changing the fixed point. The Armijo-style test candidate ≤ σ − 10⁻⁴·scale·‖g‖² is stronger than "the stress went down": it requires a decrease proportional to the step, which rules out the near-zero steps that a bare comparison accepts and then stalls on.

    8. A zero weight is not a small number #

            if self.variant == "weighted" and np.all(W > 0.0):
            raise ValueError("weighted MDS needs a missing pair; pass weights with zeros")

    The guard is about honesty of the API: weighted with a full matrix is smacof, and silently returning the same numbers under a different name would make the variant table a fiction. The same logic is why _init_missing fills the holes and then throws the fill away — it is a starting configuration, not a model of the missing data, and the numbers it invents never appear in the objective.

    Complexity #

    Operation Time Memory Note
    _distances \(O(n^2k)\) \(O(n^2k)\) the broadcast is the cost
    classical \(O(n^3)\) \(O(n^2)\) one symmetric eigendecomposition
    one smacof sweep \(O(n^3)\) \(O(n^2)\) the Laplacian solve
    one metric step \(O(n^2k)\) \(O(n^2)\) gradient plus a line-search evaluation
    one nonmetric sweep \(O(n^2\log n)\) \(O(n^2)\) the sort dominates
    weighted \(O(n^3)\) per sweep \(O(n^2)\) sparse weights do not make lstsq sparse
    full demo — — 6 fits, 0.5 s

    The awkward row is smacof's \(O(n^3)\). For the complete graph the update is \(B(X)X/n\), which is \(O(n^2k)\), and that is what every production implementation uses. The code solves a general Laplacian system because the weighted variant needs it, and pays \(n^3\) for the generality. Above a few thousand points, the right structure is to keep the unweighted update and handle missing pairs by masking the edges rather than the matrix entries.

    Classical MDS is \(O(n^3)\) once, which is cheaper than a hundred SMACOF sweeps for small \(n\) and hopeless for large \(n\): at \(n = 10^5\) the eigendecomposition is \(10^{15}\) operations. That is the reason Isomap has a landmark variant and why large-scale MDS is done by subsampling or by stochastic descent on a minibatch of pairs.

    Failure modes #

    1. The closed form is not minimising stress. On non-Euclidean dissimilarities classical MDS still returns coordinates, with negative eigenvalues quietly truncated to zero, and they are the minimiser of strain — a different objective. The visible symptom is a configuration that looks plausible and a stress that is worse than SMACOF's: \(0.006707\) against \(0.004864\) on the demo's noisy Euclidean distances, and a much larger gap on genuinely non-Euclidean data.

    2. Rotation, reflection and translation are free. (14). Comparing embeddings across runs by coordinates, or by which point ends up at the top of the plot, is comparing an arbitrary basis. Use Gram errors, distance correlations or neighbourhood overlap.

    3. Local minima are real and the start decides. SMACOF is monotone but not global, and the code starts from the classical embedding precisely because a random start lands in worse basins. If two restarts give different stress, report both; if they give the same stress with different configurations, the problem has a symmetry you have not noticed.

    4. Nonmetric stress can be driven to zero without being right. Raw stress against re-fitted disparities goes to zero as the configuration collapses, since a collapsing configuration is monotone in everything. That is why the variant reports stress-1 and why the demo's nonmetric run shows stress-1 still falling geometrically after 200 sweeps: the objective has no interior minimum, it has a limit.

    5. Ordinal data does not give you metric answers. Nonmetric MDS recovers the configuration only up to a monotone transformation of the distances, so a distance in the embedding cannot be read as "twice as far". The demo's correlation of \(1.0000\) is a statement about geometry, not about scale.

    6. Missing pairs must leave the graph connected enough. A point with no observed pair is unconstrained and wanders; a graph that splits into two components leaves their relative placement arbitrary — each component can be rotated and translated independently. The demo asserts every point keeps a pair, which is the weakest useful condition; the real one is that the graph is connected.

    7. A zero dissimilarity is not a missing pair. Two distinct objects can legitimately be at distance zero (identical species counts, tied ranks), and a weight of zero says something completely different. The code distinguishes them — D may contain zeros, W may contain zeros — but a caller who confuses the two gets a configuration that ignores real data or trusts an invented one.

    8. Truncating to too few dimensions is silent. The 1-D fit of planar data costs stress \(0.0860\) and still produces a plot. There is no warning, no negative eigenvalue to notice, and the picture looks like data. Always report the stress and compare against one more dimension.

    Applications #

    • Perceptual mapping. Brands, products or political candidates placed by judged similarity; the axes are interpreted post hoc, which is legitimate as long as nobody claims they are unique.
    • Psychometrics and survey research. Ordinal similarity judgements, preference rankings and Likert-scale profiles are exactly the data nonmetric MDS was built for.
    • Ecology and genomics. Bray–Curtis, UniFrac and Jaccard dissimilarities between sites or samples; classical or weighted MDS is the standard ordination, and the missing-data variant handles incomplete sampling.
    • Isomap and manifold learning. The second stage of isomap is classical MDS on graph geodesics, which is why this file is a prerequisite for that one.
    • Graph layout. Fruchterman–Reingold and its descendants are stress majorisation on graph distances; Kamada–Kawai is SMACOF on shortest paths.
    • Molecular conformation. Distances from NMR or cross-linking experiments, with many pairs unmeasured — the weighted variant's original application.

    Where it is a trap: reading coordinates as features; choosing the number of dimensions by looking at the plot; trusting classical MDS on non-Euclidean dissimilarities; reporting nonmetric distances quantitatively; and running an \(O(n^3)\) solve on a dataset that has ten thousand points and could have been subsampled.
